# Hiring Well

Hiring is the highest-leverage decision most managers make and the one they
are least trained for. A good hire compounds quietly for years; a poor one
costs money, absorbs the team's patience, and usually ends in a conversation
nobody enjoys. The difference between the two is rarely luck — it is almost
always how much structure the manager put in *before* meeting anyone.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Define a role by the outcomes it must produce rather than by a wish list.
- Run a structured interview that produces evidence instead of impressions.
- Name the biases that make unstructured interviews unreliable.
- Put a figure on what a bad hire costs, and compare it to the cost of
  hiring more carefully.
- Check references and make an offer without losing the candidate you want.

## Define the role before you define the person

Most bad hires begin with a vague requirement. "We need another engineer" is
a headcount request, not a role. Before advertising, write three things down:

- **The outcomes.** What must be true in twelve months because this person
  exists? "Installations completed per week rises from six to nine without
  the rework rate moving" is a role. "Strong team player" is a mood.
- **The must-haves and the nice-to-haves, separated honestly.** Everything
  in the must-have column shrinks your candidate pool, so each entry has to
  earn its place. A licence you can pay to obtain is not a must-have. Ask of
  each: *would I genuinely reject an otherwise excellent candidate for
  lacking this?* If not, move it.
- **What the job actually contains.** The proportion of the week that is
  routine, the parts that are unpleasant, the travel, the hours. Candidates
  who learn this at interview stay; candidates who learn it in month two
  leave, and they leave feeling misled.

The discipline here is the same one the Decision Making course applies to
any choice: criteria before options. Write the criteria while you have no
candidate in front of you, because afterwards you will unconsciously reshape
them around whoever you liked most.

## Structure is what makes an interview predictive

The unstructured interview — a friendly conversation, different questions
for each candidate, a judgement formed in the first few minutes — is
comfortable, universal, and close to worthless as a predictor. It mostly
measures how much the candidate resembles the interviewer.

Three changes fix most of it:

- **Ask everyone the same core questions, in the same order.** Comparison
  requires comparable evidence. Different questions produce different
  stories, and you end up comparing your impressions rather than their
  answers.
- **Ask for specific past behaviour, not hypotheticals.** "Tell me about a
  time a job overran and you had to tell the customer" beats "how would you
  handle an overrun?" People are fluent about hypotheticals and concrete
  about what they actually did. Push for the detail: what was the situation,
  what did *you* personally do, what happened, what would you change.
- **Score against the criteria immediately, before discussion.** Each
  interviewer writes their score alone, then they compare. Discussing first
  produces one loud opinion wearing four faces — the group-decision failure
  from the Decision Making course, applied to a candidate.

Add a **work sample** wherever the job allows one: a short real task under
realistic conditions. Thirty minutes of someone doing the actual work tells
you more than an hour of them describing it, and it is far harder to fake in
either direction.

## The biases that decide hires nobody meant to make

| Bias | How it shows up | The guard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First impressions | A judgement formed in the first four minutes, then evidence collected to support it | Score each answer as you go, not at the end |
| Similarity | Warmth toward the candidate who shares your background, school, town or manner | Ask what evidence you have beyond rapport |
| Halo | One strong answer or credential colouring every other rating | Score each criterion separately |
| Confidence-as-competence | Reading fluency as ability, which favours the practised interviewer | Use a work sample |
| Recency | The last candidate seen looking best | Score before the next interview, never after all of them |

None of these are character flaws and none of them go away with awareness
alone. They go away with structure — which is the entire argument for the
previous section.

## Worked example: what a bad hire actually costs

A ₦250,000-a-month hire who is clearly wrong by month three and leaves at
month five:

| | Cost |
| --- | ---: |
| Recruitment — 20 hours of manager time at ₦2,400, plus ₦60,000 advertising | ₦108,000 |
| Salary paid over five months | ₦1,250,000 |
| Colleagues covering — 4 hours a week for 20 weeks | ₦192,000 |
| Recruiting the replacement | ₦108,000 |
| Replacement's three-month ramp at roughly half output | ₦375,000 |
| **Total** | **₦2,033,000** |

Now price the alternative. Six additional hours spread across a structured
process — writing the criteria, adding a work sample, a second interviewer
scoring independently — costs about **₦14,400** of manager time.

**The bad hire costs roughly 141 times the extra care that would most likely
have prevented it.**

Three honest caveats, because the number is easy to overclaim. Structure
does not guarantee a good hire; it improves the odds. Some of that salary
bought real work, so the true net is lower than ₦2,033,000. And the largest
cost is missing from the table entirely — the effect on the team of watching
someone underperform for five months while nothing visible happens about it,
which is unmeasurable and, in most cases, worse than the money.

The direction of the argument survives all three caveats. Hiring is the one
management activity where slowing down reliably pays.

## References and the offer

**Check references, and check them properly.** A reference call that asks
"was he good?" gets "he was fine" and tells you nothing. Ask what the person
was responsible for, how they compared to others doing the same job, what
they would need support with, and — the question that earns its place —
*"would you re-hire them?"* Hesitation before the answer is itself the
answer.

**Make the offer promptly and completely.** Good candidates hold other
options, and a week of silence after a warm final interview reads as doubt.
State the salary, the terms, the start date, and what happens next, in
writing. Verbal-only offers are the beginning of a dispute the Communication
course would have prevented in one sentence.

And **be honest about the job in the offer conversation**, including the
parts that are hard. Everything you conceal to close the hire, you pay for
in the exit interview.

## Do this at work

1. **Write outcomes for one open role.** What must be true in twelve months
   because this person exists? If you cannot write it, the role is a
   headcount request rather than a job.
2. **Halve your must-have list.** For each item ask whether you would truly
   reject an otherwise excellent candidate for lacking it. Most lists lose
   half their entries and gain a much larger field.
3. **Write four core questions.** Behavioural, specific, asked of everyone
   in the same order. Use them for the next three candidates and notice how
   much easier comparison becomes.
4. **Add one work sample.** Thirty minutes of the real task, under
   realistic conditions. It is the single highest-return change on this list.
5. **Score independently before discussing.** If two of you interview, write
   scores separately and compare. Where you disagree is where the useful
   conversation is.
6. **Cost your last bad hire.** Recruitment, salary, cover, replacement,
   ramp. Take the figure to whoever thinks a careful process is slow.
7. **Ask the re-hire question.** On your next reference call. Listen to the
   pause more than the words.

## Summary

- Define the role by outcomes and honest must-haves before meeting anyone;
  criteria written after you like someone are criteria shaped around them.
- Structured interviews — same questions, same order, behavioural evidence,
  independent scoring — are what make interviewing predictive at all.
- A work sample beats an hour of description, and is hardest to fake.
- Bias is fixed by structure, not by awareness. Score as you go, score each
  criterion separately, and never rank only at the end.
- A bad hire runs to roughly ₦2m and dwarfs the few hours of extra care that
  would likely have prevented it — with the worst cost, the team's morale,
  not in the table at all.
- Check references with real questions, offer promptly and in writing, and
  describe the job honestly, including the unpleasant parts.
