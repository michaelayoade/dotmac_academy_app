# Retention & Exit

People leave. The question a manager controls is not whether but *who*, *how
often*, and *whether you found out why in time to act*. This chapter treats
retention the way the Finance course treats customer churn — as arithmetic
with a leading indicator — and treats the exit itself as a process worth
running well, because everyone still employed is watching how it goes.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Put a cost on turnover and on a point of improvement in it.
- Name the reasons people actually leave, in the order they actually apply.
- Hold a stay conversation before the resignation rather than after.
- Run a resignation, notice period and handover without losing knowledge.
- Get honest information out of an exit, and act on the pattern.

## Turnover is a number, and it has a price

Some turnover is healthy — people grow, roles end, some hires are wrong for
both sides. Turnover becomes a problem when it is high, when it is
concentrated in your strongest people, or when you cannot say why it is
happening.

Cost it the way Chapter 1 costed a bad hire: recruitment, cover during the
vacancy, the replacement's ramp, and the knowledge that walked out. For a
mid-level role that lands around **₦900,000** per leaver.

| Team of 20 | Leavers a year | Annual cost |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| At 25% turnover | 5 | ₦4,500,000 |
| At 15% turnover | 3 | ₦2,700,000 |
| **Ten points of retention is worth** | | **₦1,800,000 a year** |

That is a recurring saving, available without winning a customer or cutting
a cost — and it is usually cheaper to buy than it looks, because the reasons
people leave are mostly not expensive to fix.

Two honest qualifications. Zero turnover is not the goal; a team nobody ever
leaves is often a team nobody can be promoted out of. And the average hides
the important part: **losing your five weakest and losing your five
strongest cost the same on this table and are entirely different events.**
Track who leaves, not only how many.

## Why people actually leave

Ranked roughly by how often they turn out to be the real reason, rather than
the reason given:

1. **Their manager.** The relationship, the fairness, whether they felt
   seen. It is a cliché because it keeps being true.
2. **No visible future.** Not necessarily promotion — growth, new skills,
   scope. People rarely leave a job they are still learning in.
3. **Feeling unrecognised.** Consistent effort that nobody named.
4. **Workload and its distribution.** Especially the reliable person quietly
   carrying more, from the Team Leadership capacity arithmetic — they do not
   complain, they resign.
5. **Pay** — which is usually the *stated* reason and, on its own, less often
   the deciding one. Someone content elsewhere rarely leaves for 10%.
   Someone already unhappy takes 10% as the trigger and the explanation.

Note what that ordering implies: most retention is bought with attention
rather than money, and money spent while the first four remain unaddressed
buys a short delay at permanent cost.

## Stay conversations beat exit interviews

An exit interview tells you why someone left. A **stay conversation** tells
you why someone might, while you can still do something. Twice a year, ask
directly:

- What makes a good day here, and what makes a bad one?
- What would you like to be doing in two years that you are not doing now?
- Is there anything that would make you consider leaving?
- What is one thing I could change that would make a real difference?

Then act on at least one thing, visibly. A stay conversation that produces
nothing teaches the person that speaking up is pointless — which is worse
than not having asked.

Watch the leading indicators too, in the Data course's sense: disengagement
from things they used to care about, withdrawal from the team, a sudden
uptick in leave taken, going quiet in meetings where they used to argue.
Resignations are usually visible for months. The letter is a lagging
indicator.

## When someone resigns

- **Do not take it personally, and do not react in the moment.** Thank them,
  ask for a conversation, and find out honestly why.
- **Counter-offer rarely, and never reflexively.** A counter-offer that
  fixes only pay leaves the actual reason in place; a large share of
  accepted counter-offers end in a departure within a year anyway. If the
  reason was genuinely pay and the pay was genuinely wrong, fix it — and
  then ask why it took a resignation to surface.
- **Run the notice period properly.** Agree the handover in writing: what
  they own, what state it is in, who receives each item, what only they
  know. The undocumented knowledge is the expensive part, and the notice
  period is your only chance at it.
- **Let them leave well.** How you treat someone on the way out is watched
  closely by everyone staying, and it determines whether they recommend you,
  refer candidates, or come back. Departing staff become customers,
  suppliers, colleagues elsewhere, and occasionally returning employees who
  are cheaper and better than any new hire.

## Getting truth out of an exit

Most exit interviews collect polite fiction, because the person has nothing
to gain from candour and a reference to protect. Improve the odds:

- **Have someone other than their manager do it.** Especially if the manager
  is the reason.
- **Ask about the decision, not the job.** "When did you first start
  thinking about leaving, and what was happening then?" gets closer to truth
  than "why are you leaving?"
- **Ask again later.** A short conversation three months after departure is
  often far franker than the one at the door.
- **Read the pattern, not the instance.** One exit is an anecdote. Three
  citing the same manager, the same shift pattern or the same broken
  promise is a finding — and, like every measurement in this curriculum, it
  is only worth collecting if something is done with it.

## Do this at work

1. **Compute your turnover.** Leavers over the year ÷ average headcount.
   Then multiply by a realistic cost per leaver. That is your annual bill.
2. **List who left, not just how many.** Were they your strongest? Same
   team, same manager, same shift? The pattern is the finding.
3. **Hold two stay conversations this month.** Ask the four questions. Then
   act visibly on one thing — otherwise do not ask.
4. **Check your leading indicators.** Who has gone quiet, withdrawn, or
   stopped arguing about things they used to care about?
5. **Find your overloaded reliable person.** From the Team Leadership
   capacity table. They will not complain. Rebalance before you find out the
   other way.
6. **Write a handover template.** What they own, its state, who receives it,
   what only they know. Use it at the next resignation rather than inventing
   it under time pressure.
7. **Re-ask one recent leaver.** Three months on, informally. What you learn
   is usually worth the awkwardness of asking.

## Summary

- Turnover has a price: at ₦900,000 a leaver, ten points of retention on a
  team of twenty is ₦1,800,000 a year, recurring.
- Zero turnover is not the goal, and the average hides the point — losing
  your strongest and your weakest cost the same on paper and are different
  events entirely.
- People leave their manager, a lack of future, and feeling unrecognised
  more often than they leave for pay; pay is the trigger and the
  explanation more often than the cause.
- Stay conversations beat exit interviews because they happen while you can
  still act — but only if you act visibly on something.
- Resignations are visible for months in withdrawal and disengagement; the
  letter is the lagging indicator.
- Counter-offer rarely, handover in writing, and let people leave well —
  everyone staying is watching, and leavers become customers, referrers and
  sometimes returners.
