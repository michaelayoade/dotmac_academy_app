# Discipline & Grievance

Most managers meet formal process at the worst possible moment: something has
gone wrong, feelings are high, and they are improvising a procedure they have
never read. The result is usually an outcome that is *correct and unusable* —
the right conclusion reached in a way that cannot be defended. This chapter
is about fair process: what it consists of, why it protects you as much as
the employee, and how to handle the complaint that comes the other way.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Tell a conduct matter from a capability matter, and why the distinction
  changes everything that follows.
- Run an investigation that establishes facts before anyone is accused.
- Apply the elements of fair process in the right order.
- Choose a proportionate outcome and record it defensibly.
- Receive a grievance — including one about you — without making it worse.

## First: conduct or capability?

These get confused constantly, and the confusion produces unfair outcomes in
both directions.

- **Conduct** is about choice. Someone did something they knew they should
  not: falsified a record, was abusive, ignored a safety rule they were
  trained on.
- **Capability** is about ability. Someone is trying and cannot yet meet the
  standard — through skill, health, or a job that changed under them.

The distinction sets everything downstream. Capability is answered with
training, support, adjustment and time; disciplining someone for being unable
is both unjust and ineffective. Conduct is answered with process and
consequence; coaching someone who made a deliberate choice teaches the team
that the rule is optional.

When you genuinely cannot tell, that is what the investigation is for. Ask
the Decision Making course's question: *facts before theories.*

## Fair process, in order

The specifics are set by law and your organisation's policy — follow those.
The universal shape is remarkably consistent:

1. **Investigate before accusing.** Establish what happened, from records
   and from people who saw it, *including the employee's account*. An
   investigation that begins with a conclusion is not an investigation.
2. **Separate the roles.** Wherever numbers allow, whoever investigates
   should not decide, and whoever decides should not hear the appeal. One
   person doing all three is the procedural failure that most often gets an
   otherwise-correct outcome overturned.
3. **Put the case in writing, in advance.** The allegation, specifically;
   the evidence; the possible consequences; the time and place. Someone
   cannot answer a case they first hear in the room.
4. **Hear the answer properly.** Let them respond, bring their
   representative or companion where entitled, produce their own evidence,
   and be listened to. If what they say changes the picture, the picture
   changes — otherwise the hearing was theatre.
5. **Decide, then explain.** The outcome, the reasons, and the right of
   appeal, in writing.
6. **Hear an appeal from someone new.** Not the person defending their own
   decision.

Two more that carry more weight than they look: **act promptly**, because
delay is itself unfair and memories decay; and **be consistent**, because
the same behaviour treated differently for two people is the single fact
most likely to turn a disciplinary matter into a discrimination claim.

## Proportion, and the point of a sanction

A sanction exists to change behaviour, not to express how the manager feels.
That reframing answers most proportionality questions on its own.

| Consider | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Seriousness and actual harm | A near-miss and an injury may be the same act with different luck — but consequences do count |
| Intent | Careless, reckless, or deliberate are three different matters |
| Record and length of service | Relevant to the sanction, not to whether it happened |
| Consistency | What happened to others for the same thing — check, do not assume |
| Mitigation | The circumstances they offered, weighed honestly |
| Whether the rule was known | An unwritten, unenforced rule is not a rule you can dismiss over |

Most matters end below dismissal, and the point of a first written warning is
that most people correct after one. Warnings should **expire** — a sanction
that never lapses gives someone no route back and removes the incentive to
improve.

## When you get it procedurally wrong

Worth stating plainly, because it is the trap: **a fair outcome reached
unfairly is usually unusable.** Dismiss someone who genuinely did the thing,
but do it without telling them the allegation in advance, or with the same
person investigating and deciding, or without an appeal — and the defensible
part of the case evaporates. The organisation then pays compensation for
conduct nobody disputes.

The lesson is not that process is a legal formality to be endured. It is that
process is *how you make a decision worth relying on.* The sequence exists
because each step catches an error the previous one could not: investigation
catches the wrong facts, the written case catches the wrong allegation, the
hearing catches the missing explanation, the appeal catches the wrong
decision. Skipping steps does not save time; it moves the discovery of the
error to the most expensive possible point.

## Grievances, including about you

A grievance is a complaint an employee raises — about treatment, conditions,
a colleague, or their manager. The instinctive responses are all wrong:
defensiveness, minimising, or a promise to "have a quiet word".

- **Take it seriously and say so.** Most grievances are raised late, after
  private attempts. Someone reaching the formal route has usually been
  unhappy for a while.
- **Follow the process, even when it seems small.** Informal resolution is
  often best and should be offered — but if they want it formal, it is
  formal.
- **Step aside when it concerns you.** You cannot investigate a complaint
  about yourself. Hand it to someone independent, immediately, and without
  visible resentment.
- **Never retaliate, and never appear to.** A change in shifts, tone or
  opportunities after a grievance will be read as retaliation whatever the
  reason — so if a change is genuinely needed, document why it was already
  in motion.
- **Close the loop.** Tell them the outcome and the reasoning. A grievance
  that disappears into silence teaches the whole team not to bother.

The uncomfortable truth: an organisation with no grievances usually does not
have no problems. It has no route, or no confidence that the route is safe.

## Do this at work

1. **Read your policy now.** Before you need it. Ten minutes today against
   an improvised procedure under pressure later.
2. **Classify one live concern.** Conduct or capability? If you cannot tell,
   say so and investigate — do not default to whichever feels easier.
3. **Check who does what.** For any matter in progress, is the same person
   investigating and deciding? Separate them while it is still possible.
4. **Write one allegation properly.** Specific, dated, with the evidence and
   the possible consequences. Read it as though you had to answer it.
5. **Check consistency.** How was the same behaviour handled last time, and
   by whom? Inconsistency is the fact most likely to be fatal.
6. **Check your warnings expire.** Anything permanent leaves no route back
   and removes the reason to improve.
7. **Ask why you have no grievances.** If none have been raised in a year,
   find out whether that is health or silence.

## Summary

- Separate conduct from capability first: one is answered with process and
  consequence, the other with support and time, and confusing them is unjust
  either way.
- Fair process is a sequence — investigate, separate the roles, put the case
  in writing, hear the answer, decide and explain, allow an appeal to
  someone new. Act promptly and consistently.
- Each step catches an error the previous one cannot; skipping steps moves
  the discovery of the error to the most expensive point.
- A fair outcome reached unfairly is usually unusable — the organisation
  pays for conduct nobody disputes.
- Sanctions exist to change behaviour, should be proportionate, and should
  expire.
- Take grievances seriously, step aside when they concern you, never
  retaliate or appear to, and close the loop. No grievances at all is more
  often silence than health.
