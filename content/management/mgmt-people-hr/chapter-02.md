# Onboarding & Probation

The hire is not the finish line; it is the moment the investment starts
paying back or starts leaking. Most organisations spend weeks selecting
someone and then hand them a desk, a laptop if they are lucky, and a
colleague's spare attention. This chapter is about the first ninety days —
what good onboarding actually does, and why probation is a decision rather
than a formality that expires.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Say what onboarding is for, beyond paperwork and a tour.
- Build a first-week, first-month, first-quarter plan a new starter can see.
- Explain why early attrition is usually an onboarding failure rather than a
  hiring one.
- Use probation as a real decision point, with evidence gathered on purpose.
- Hold the conversation when probation is not going to be passed.

## What onboarding is actually for

Paperwork and a building tour are the least of it. Onboarding has three
jobs, and only one of them is administrative:

- **Make them productive sooner.** Every day a new starter spends guessing is
  a day of salary against no output. The fix is unglamorous: access on day
  one, a named person to ask, the five documents that actually matter, and a
  first real task within the first week.
- **Make them certain they chose right.** New starters are re-deciding
  quietly for weeks. The organisation that looks disorganised in week one
  looks risky in week three, and the strong candidate — the one with other
  options — is the one who acts on it.
- **Teach how things actually work here.** Not the org chart: who to ask
  about what, which rules are firm and which are habits, how decisions get
  made, what "good" looks like in this team. This is what a colleague
  transmits in a fortnight and a handbook never transmits at all.

## A plan the new starter can see

The cheapest improvement available to most managers is writing the plan
down and giving it to the person. Not a policy document — one page.

| | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| **Before day one** | Access, equipment, accounts, desk, someone expecting them. Nothing corrodes faster than a first day spent waiting for a login. |
| **Week one** | Who they meet and why; the five things to read; one small real task finished. Meeting the work beats reading about it. |
| **Month one** | The core of the job, with supervision. A named buddy for the questions too small to bring a manager. First feedback conversation. |
| **Month three** | Working at expected standard on the routine parts. The probation decision, against criteria stated on day one. |

Two details do disproportionate work. **A named buddy** — a peer, not the
manager — because most new-starter questions are too small to interrupt a
manager with and too important to guess at. And **a first real task in week
one**, however small: nothing settles a new starter like having done
something useful.

## Early attrition is an onboarding number

When people leave inside six months, the instinct is to blame hiring. Look
at the causes honestly and most of them are downstream of the offer:

- the job differed from what was described (a hiring honesty failure);
- no clarity about what good looked like, so effort went to the wrong things;
- no early feedback, so a small correctable problem became a settled opinion;
- nobody to ask, so competence took twice as long to demonstrate;
- and the quiet one — the manager was busy, so the person concluded they did
  not matter.

This is the Finance course's retention arithmetic wearing a different hat.
Replacing someone costs a multiple of keeping them; the early-tenure period
is where the risk is concentrated; and the fix is upstream, not a
counter-offer at the exit. **Early attrition is the most preventable
turnover you have**, because in the first ninety days you still control
almost every variable.

## Probation is a decision, not a date

Probation exists so both sides can find out. It fails when it is treated as
a period that simply elapses — the manager notices the date passing, signs
the form, and inherits a performance problem that is now much harder to
address.

Used properly it has four parts:

1. **Criteria stated on day one.** What must be demonstrated by month three,
   specifically enough that two people would agree whether it happened.
   Criteria produced at the end are criteria fitted to the outcome.
2. **Evidence gathered as you go.** Notes after the feedback conversations,
   examples of work, what improved after coaching. Judgement assembled from
   memory at month three is memory, not evidence.
3. **Feedback early and repeatedly.** Nobody should be surprised at a
   probation review. If they are, the review is reporting on your
   supervision as much as their performance.
4. **A real decision, made on time.** Pass, extend with stated reasons and a
   short horizon, or end it. Extending indefinitely is not kindness; it is
   deferring a decision at the employee's expense, because they are building
   a life on a job you have privately decided about.

## Worked example: the cost of not deciding

Return to Chapter 1's hire: ₦250,000 a month, clearly wrong by month three.

| | Decide at month 3 | Drift to month 5 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Salary paid | ₦750,000 | ₦1,250,000 |
| Colleague cover at 4 hrs/week | ₦115,200 | ₦192,000 |
| **Sub-total** | **₦865,200** | **₦1,442,000** |

**Two months of not deciding costs ₦576,800** — and that is only the part
that appears in a ledger. It also costs the team, who can see the situation
clearly and are watching what the organisation tolerates, and it costs the
individual, who spends two further months in a job they were never going to
keep and leaves with less time and a worse story.

The decision at month three is not harsher than the decision at month five.
It is the same decision, made while it is still cheap for everybody.

## When probation is not going to be passed

The conversation is uncomfortable and gets worse with delay. What makes it
survivable:

- **No surprises.** If the feedback has been honest and regular, this
  conversation confirms something already discussed rather than announcing
  it.
- **Specifics, not verdicts.** The standard expected, what was observed, the
  support given. Situation, behaviour, impact — the structure from the Team
  Leadership course, in a higher-stakes room.
- **Follow the process exactly.** Notice, the right to be accompanied where
  it applies, the decision in writing. Probation reduces obligations in many
  places; it rarely removes them, and a procedurally sloppy exit converts a
  performance matter into a legal one.
- **Be decent about the ending.** How someone is treated on the way out is
  observed by everyone staying, and it is the version of your organisation
  they will describe for years.

## Do this at work

1. **Write the one-page plan** for the next person joining your team. Before
   day one, week one, month one, month three.
2. **Fix the day-one list.** Access, equipment, accounts, a person expecting
   them. Check it a week ahead, not the morning of.
3. **Name a buddy** — a peer, not you. Ask them to make contact before day
   one.
4. **Give one real task in week one.** However small. Having done something
   useful settles a new starter faster than any induction session.
5. **State probation criteria on day one**, in writing. If you cannot write
   them, you cannot fairly judge them at month three.
6. **Check your early attrition.** How many left inside six months last
   year, and why? That number is an onboarding report card, not a hiring one.
7. **Find the drifting probation.** Anyone whose review date is approaching
   without a decision forming. Decide it on time — the cost of waiting is
   paid by three parties, not one.

## Summary

- Onboarding makes people productive sooner, confirms they chose right, and
  teaches how things actually work — paperwork is the least of it.
- Give the new starter a one-page plan they can see, a named peer buddy, and
  a real task in week one.
- Early attrition is usually an onboarding failure, not a hiring one, and it
  is the most preventable turnover you have.
- Probation is a decision with criteria stated on day one, evidence gathered
  as you go, and feedback that makes the review a confirmation.
- Deciding at month three rather than month five saved ₦576,800 in the
  example — and the ledger is the smallest of the three costs.
- End it properly when you must: no surprises, specifics rather than
  verdicts, exact process, and decency that everyone staying will notice.
