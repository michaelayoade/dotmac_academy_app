# Pay, Records & the Employment File

This is the least glamorous chapter in the curriculum and the one most
likely to save you. Employment records are boring right up to the moment
they are the only thing standing between a disagreement and a dispute — and
by then they cannot be created retrospectively. This chapter covers what
must exist, what pay decisions actually communicate, and how to handle
information about people without causing harm.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Name what an employment file must contain, and why each item is there.
- Write a record at the time that will still be useful in two years.
- Explain what a pay decision communicates beyond its amount.
- Recognise pay compression before your best people do.
- Handle personal data proportionately and confidentially.

## The file: what must exist

Requirements differ by jurisdiction and your organisation's policy governs;
what follows is the common core that a manager is usually responsible for
contributing to:

| Category | What belongs in it |
| --- | --- |
| **The agreement** | Contract or written statement of terms, job description, any variation both sides signed |
| **Identity and eligibility** | Right-to-work evidence, qualifications where the role requires them, licences with expiry dates |
| **Pay** | Rate, changes with dates and reasons, payslips, allowances, deductions authorised in writing |
| **Time** | Leave taken and remaining, absence with reasons, working-time records where hours are regulated |
| **Performance** | Objectives, review notes, feedback conversations that mattered, probation outcome |
| **Conduct** | Investigations, hearings, outcomes, warnings and their expiry dates |
| **Training** | Mandatory training with dates, safety certifications, anything with a renewal cycle |

Two properties matter more than completeness. **Written at the time** — a
note made three months later is a recollection, and it reads like one.
**Factual** — what was said, what was agreed, what was observed. A file full
of adjectives about someone's attitude is worse than an empty one, because
it demonstrates a manager forming conclusions without evidence.

The test for any record: *would this still be understandable, and still be
fair, if read by a stranger in two years with the author unavailable?*

## Writing a record that will hold up

Three lines is usually enough after a conversation that mattered:

> **12 August, one-to-one, Ada.** Discussed the three late job sheets in
> July — dates given. Ada said the tablet has been failing to sync on site.
> Agreed: IT ticket raised by her today; I check on 19 August; if the sync
> is fixed and sheets are still late we treat it as a performance matter.
> Sent to Ada by email the same day.

Notice what it does. It is dated, specific, includes **the employee's
account** rather than only yours, records what was agreed and by when, and
was **shared with the person**. That last habit is the one managers skip and
the one that matters most: a record the employee has seen is evidence, while
a record they have never seen is one person's version.

It also protects the employee. If the tablet really is broken, the file now
shows a manager who investigated rather than assumed — and if the same issue
recurs in six months, nobody is arguing about whether it was raised.

## Pay is a message, not just an amount

Every pay decision communicates something whether or not you intend it.
Four rules keep the message honest:

- **Be able to explain any gap.** Two people doing the same job at
  materially different rates need a reason you would state out loud —
  experience, scope, performance, market at time of hire. "It's what they
  negotiated" is a reason, and it is the one that produces the most
  resentment when discovered, because it rewards confidence rather than
  contribution.
- **Assume pay is not confidential.** People discuss it, in many places have
  the legal right to, and the assumption of secrecy mostly protects
  arrangements that would not survive daylight. Set pay you could defend if
  the whole team saw it.
- **Watch compression.** Market rates rise for new hires while existing
  staff get percentage increases. Left alone, the newcomer eventually earns
  more than the person training them — and that person finds out.
- **Never promise what you cannot authorise.** "I'll see what I can do about
  a raise" is heard as a commitment. Say what you will actually do: raise it
  with whom, by when, and tell them the answer either way.

## Worked example: the raise that was cheaper than the replacement

An experienced engineer on ₦280,000 a month discovers the newest hire is on
₦320,000 — the market moved and their own increases did not track it. They
ask for a correction to ₦320,000.

| | Correct the pay | Let them leave |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Extra cost, 12 months | ₦480,000 | — |
| Recruitment | — | ₦108,000 |
| Cover during the vacancy and ramp | — | ₦307,200 |
| Replacement's 3-month ramp at half output | — | ₦480,000 |
| Replacement's salary at current market | — | ₦480,000 more than the old rate |
| **12-month cost** | **₦480,000** | **₦1,375,200** |

Correcting the pay costs **₦480,000**. Replacing the person costs
**₦1,375,200** — and the replacement is hired at the market rate anyway, so
the salary saving the refusal was protecting does not exist. It was never a
choice between paying more and paying the same; it was a choice between
paying more *with* the experienced engineer and paying more *without* them.

The caveat matters: this arithmetic does not mean every request should be
granted, and a manager who yields to whoever asks creates exactly the
"rewards confidence" problem above. It means the comparison should be to the
real alternative rather than to an imaginary status quo where the person
stays at the old rate and is content. That version is not on the menu.

## Handling information about people

- **Collect only what you need**, keep it only as long as you need it, and
  store it where only those who need it can reach it. A team drive with
  everyone's absence reasons on it is a breach waiting for an audience.
- **Health information is a category apart.** It goes to whoever must act on
  it, at the minimum detail required — usually "adjustments needed" rather
  than a diagnosis. A colleague's condition is not team news.
- **Confidence has limits, and say so early.** "I can't promise to keep that
  to myself if it's a safety matter — but I'll tell you before I take it
  anywhere" is honest, and it is far better than a promise you will break.
- **The file follows employment law, not your convenience.** People
  generally have a right to see what is held about them, which is another
  reason not to write anything you would not show them.

## Do this at work

1. **Audit one file.** Pick a team member and check the core is present:
   agreement, pay history with dates, leave, training and renewals,
   performance notes. Fill the gaps you can fill honestly.
2. **Find an expiring certificate.** Licences, safety tickets, mandatory
   training. Diarise the renewals — an expired ticket is usually discovered
   at the worst possible moment.
3. **Write one three-line record today.** After your next conversation that
   mattered. Include the person's account, and send it to them.
4. **Check your rates against the market.** For every role you manage. If a
   new hire would command more than an existing performer, you have
   compression and a limited amount of time.
5. **Explain one pay gap out loud.** Take two people at different rates and
   say the reason. If it will not survive being said, it will not survive
   being discovered.
6. **Look at where you store people data.** Who can reach it? Reduce it to
   who needs it, this week.
7. **Check for a promise you have not closed.** Anything you said you would
   "look into". Go back with an answer, including the no.

## Summary

- The employment file exists for the day memory is contested, and cannot be
  created retrospectively — write records at the time, factually, and share
  them with the person.
- A record showing the employee's account and an agreed next step protects
  them as much as you.
- Pay communicates: be able to explain every gap out loud, assume nothing is
  secret, and watch compression before your best people find it.
- Compare a raise to the real alternative — in the example, ₦480,000 to keep
  the engineer against ₦1,375,200 to replace them at the same market rate.
- Collect the minimum personal data, restrict access, treat health
  information as a category apart, and never offer confidentiality you
  cannot keep.
