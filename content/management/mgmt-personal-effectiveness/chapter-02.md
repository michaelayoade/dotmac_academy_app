# Priorities & Saying No

A priority list on which everything is high is not a priority list; it is an
inventory. This chapter is about the two skills that make prioritising real:
choosing an order you can defend, and declining work in a way that keeps the
relationship and makes the trade-off visible to whoever is asking.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Separate urgent from important, and say which quadrant your week lives in.
- Rank work in a true order rather than in bands of "high".
- Show what a new commitment displaces, in writing.
- Say no — or "yes, and here is what moves" — without damage.
- Recognise when the real problem is capacity rather than prioritisation.

## Urgent is not important

The distinction is old because it keeps working:

| | **Urgent** | **Not urgent** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Important** | Crises, hard deadlines, live incidents. Unavoidable — but a week made only of these is a week of firefighting. | **Where the returns are.** Planning, prevention, coaching, systems, relationships, the thinking that stops quadrant one recurring. |
| **Not important** | Most interruptions, many meetings and messages. Feels productive, produces little. | Genuine waste — and easy to spot once labelled. |

Almost every manager's week is dominated by the urgent, and almost every
improvement comes from spending more of it on the important-but-not-urgent
box. That box has no deadline, nobody chases it, and it is where prevention,
delegation and systems live — which is to say, it is where tomorrow's
firefighting is either created or avoided.

The practical move is not a philosophy. It is a **defended block in the
calendar**, treated with the seriousness of a meeting with your manager,
because that is the only form of protection that survives a busy week.

## A real order, not bands

"High, high, high, medium" is not an order — it is a way of avoiding the
decision. A real ranking is **1, 2, 3**, and its value is that it answers the
only question that matters when something new arrives: *does this go above or
below number three?*

Three disciplines make it stick:

- **One list, not several.** Work living in your head, your inbox, a
  colleague's expectation and three chat threads cannot be ranked against
  each other.
- **Rank against effort as well as value.** The item worth a great deal that
  needs forty hours may lose to three items worth less that need two hours
  each. Prioritising on value alone is how managers end up with an
  impressive list on which nothing finishes.
- **Re-rank on a rhythm, not continuously.** A weekly pass beats re-arguing
  the order every time someone asks, which is how the loudest request wins.

## Saying no is a management skill

Most managers do not have a prioritisation problem so much as a **declining
problem**: they can see the right order and say yes anyway. The instinct is
about the relationship, so the technique has to protect it.

Four forms that work:

- **The trade-off.** *"Yes, we can — that pushes the Kano survey to the 20th.
  Is that the right swap?"* This is the strongest form, because it is not a
  refusal at all; it hands the decision to the person who owns the priority,
  with the cost attached.
- **The honest no with a reason.** *"No — not this month. We're committed to
  X and adding this means missing it."* Reasons make a no discussable rather
  than personal.
- **The counter-offer.** *"I can't do the full analysis, but I can give you
  the three numbers you actually need by Thursday."* Often what they wanted.
- **The delay with a date.** *"Not now — ask me on the 9th."* Different from
  drifting, and it must be diarised or it is just a soft no.

What does not work: a vague yes intended to buy time, agreeing and then
missing it, or the silent overload where you absorb the work into evenings
and nobody ever learns the real capacity. The last one is the most common and
the most damaging, because **absorbed work is invisible, so the plan that
caused it never gets corrected.**

## Worked example: what a yes actually costs

From the Team Leadership course: a person's forty-hour week holds about
**23.8 hours** of plannable work once meetings, admin and the historical 30%
of unplanned work are removed.

A manager is asked to take on a task needing six hours a week.

| | Before | After the yes |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Plannable hours | 23.8 | 23.8 |
| Already committed | 21.0 | 21.0 |
| New request | — | 6.0 |
| **Committed total** | 21.0 (88%) | **27.0 (113%)** |

The yes did not consume the 2.8 hours of headroom. It consumed the headroom
**and 3.2 hours that do not exist** — which will be found in the unplanned-work
buffer, in quality, or in evenings. And because none of those three appear on
a plan, the overcommitment is invisible to whoever asked.

Now the same conversation with the trade-off made explicit: *"I can take it —
it needs six hours a week and I have 2.8. Which of these three do you want me
to stop?"* Nothing about that is obstructive. It converts an invisible
overcommitment into a visible choice, and it is made by the person who owns
the priorities rather than absorbed by the person who owns the workload.

The uncomfortable part: this only works if you know your real number. A
manager who has never done Chapter 1's log has no 23.8 to point at, and
"I'm quite busy" is not an argument anyone has to accept.

## When it is capacity, not priorities

Sometimes the list is correctly ordered, nothing on it is waste, and it still
does not fit. That is not a personal effectiveness problem and no technique
will fix it. It is a resourcing conversation, and treating it as a discipline
failure — by yourself or by your manager — leads to the same place: the work
absorbed quietly until something gives.

The signal is straightforward: **if the honest plan still exceeds the honest
capacity after you have removed the waste and delegated the delegable**, stop
optimising and escalate with numbers. That is Chapter 5's territory.

## Do this at work

1. **Sort last week into the four quadrants.** The proportion in
   important-but-not-urgent is the number that predicts next month.
2. **Defend one block a week** for that quadrant, and treat it like a meeting
   with your manager — because in effect it is.
3. **Turn your list into 1, 2, 3.** If two things are both "high", decide
   which is higher. That is the whole exercise.
4. **Rank one item against effort, not only value.** Look for the impressive
   item that never finishes.
5. **Use the trade-off sentence once this week.** "Yes — and that pushes X.
   Is that the right swap?" Notice how rarely it is received as a refusal.
6. **Find one absorbed commitment.** Something you took on that never
   appeared in any plan. Make it visible, even retrospectively.
7. **Test whether it is capacity.** If the honest plan exceeds the honest
   capacity after removing waste, stop optimising and escalate with numbers.

## Summary

- Urgent and important are different; the returns are in the
  important-but-not-urgent box, which has no deadline and so needs a defended
  block to survive a busy week.
- A real priority order is 1, 2, 3 — bands of "high" avoid the decision. Rank
  against effort as well as value, on one list, revisited on a rhythm.
- Most managers have a declining problem rather than a prioritising one.
- The strongest no is the trade-off: yes, and here is what moves — which
  hands the decision to whoever owns the priority, with the cost attached.
- A six-hour yes against 2.8 hours of headroom does not consume the headroom;
  it consumes hours that do not exist, invisibly.
- When the honest plan still exceeds honest capacity, it is a resourcing
  conversation, not a technique problem.
