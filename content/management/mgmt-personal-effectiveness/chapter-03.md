# Attention, Focus & the Working Day

Time and attention are different resources, and most managers manage only the
first. A calendar can be full of available hours that produce nothing, because
the hours arrive in fragments too small to think in. This chapter is about
protecting attention: shaping the day around it, defending blocks of it, and
handling the two systems that consume most of it — email and messages.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Explain why fragmented hours are worth less than whole ones.
- Shape a day around your own attention pattern rather than against it.
- Defend a block of concentrated time and survive the objections.
- Process messages in batches instead of monitoring them continuously.
- Recognise the difference between responsiveness and availability.

## Fragmented hours are worth less

Chapter 1 put a number on recovery: roughly twelve minutes to re-enter
concentrated work. That number has a consequence people rarely draw out.

Four hours broken into eight half-hour pieces is not four hours of capacity.
Each re-entry costs recovery, and worse, work requiring sustained thought
never reaches depth at all — you spend each fragment getting back to where
you were and then stop. Some work is simply **not divisible**: a difficult
piece of analysis, a hard piece of writing, an actual plan. It either gets a
long block or it does not get done, and it is exactly the work that slides to
"tomorrow" indefinitely in Chapter 1's diagnosis.

Which means the calendar question is not only *how many hours* but *in what
shape*. Two protected hours can outproduce a fragmented six.

## Shape the day to your own pattern

People differ, and the common advice — "do your hardest work first thing" —
is right for many and wrong for plenty. What matters is that you know your
own pattern and stop fighting it:

- **Find your best two hours.** Most people have a period when difficult
  thinking is markedly easier. Put the only-you work there.
- **Put shallow work in the shallow hours.** Admin, expenses, routine
  approvals and filing belong in the post-lunch trough, not in your best
  window, and they are perfectly suited to it.
- **Batch by type, not by arrival.** Switching between kinds of work costs
  more than doing more of one kind. Group the calls, group the approvals,
  group the reviewing.
- **Protect the same block daily rather than heroically once a week.** A
  recurring, expected, unremarkable block survives; a special arrangement
  gets cancelled the first busy week.

## Defending a block, and the objections

A protected block only exists if it survives contact with other people. Three
things make it hold:

- **Put it in the calendar as busy**, with a real name — "Q3 plan", not
  "focus time". Vague labels get overwritten by anyone who thinks their
  meeting is more concrete than your intention.
- **Tell people what it is and when you are available**, which turns it from
  unavailability into a schedule. "I'm heads-down until eleven; anything
  urgent, phone me" costs nothing and prevents almost all resentment.
- **Have a genuine escape hatch.** A manager who is unreachable is a
  bottleneck, and the whole arrangement collapses the first time something
  real cannot reach you. Name the channel that always works.

The objection worth taking seriously is **"my team needs access to me."** They
do. But access is not the same as constant availability, and the trade the
block makes is usually good for them: two hours of unavailability against a
manager who has actually thought about the thing they are all waiting for.
Where a team genuinely cannot survive two hours, that is a delegation finding
— from the Team Leadership course — not a reason to abandon the block.

## Email and messages: batch, do not monitor

Continuous monitoring is the single most expensive habit in most managers'
days, because it converts every message into an interruption with a recovery
cost. Batching converts the same volume into a fraction of the disruption.

- **Process at set times.** Three passes a day suits most roles. The world
  survives a two-hour wait far more often than the instinct suggests, and
  where it genuinely does not, that is what the phone is for.
- **Turn off the alerts.** A notification is an instruction to context-switch
  issued by whoever happened to type. Deciding when to look is the entire
  point.
- **Handle each item once.** Do it, delegate it, diarise it, or delete it.
  Re-reading the same message four times across a day is the most common
  invisible waste in an inbox.
- **Keep a short reply short.** Length is not respect, and a three-line
  answer today beats a considered essay next week — which the Communication
  course argues at length.
- **Separate responsiveness from availability.** Being reliably responsive
  within a known window is better service than being erratically instant,
  and it is far cheaper to sustain.

One caveat worth stating: **some roles genuinely require monitoring** — an
incident duty, a live escalation, a customer window. The answer there is not
to pretend otherwise but to make it a defined shift with a defined end,
rather than a permanent condition of being employed.

## The end of the day

Two habits, each costing minutes, that change the following day:

- **Close the day deliberately.** Five minutes: what moved, what did not,
  what is first tomorrow. It stops the evening being spent mentally
  re-running an unfinished list, and it means tomorrow starts with a decision
  already made.
- **Leave a thread to pick up.** Finishing mid-task, with a note of the next
  step, makes re-entry much cheaper than starting cold at a blank beginning.

And the one that is not a technique: **stopping.** A manager who works
constantly makes worse decisions, models it for a team who will copy it, and
loses the perspective that made them worth promoting. Fatigue is a
decision-making risk, in exactly the way the Decision Making course means —
and it is the one nobody puts on a risk register.

## Do this at work

1. **Look at last week's calendar for shape, not hours.** How many blocks
   longer than ninety minutes did you have? For most managers the answer is
   none, which explains the sliding work.
2. **Book one recurring two-hour block** with a real project name, marked
   busy, every day if possible. Same time, unremarkable.
3. **Tell people the arrangement** — including the channel that always
   reaches you. That sentence prevents almost all the friction.
4. **Turn off message alerts for one day** and process at three set times.
   Notice what actually broke; it is usually nothing.
5. **Find your best two hours** from Chapter 1's log, and check what is
   currently scheduled in them. Most managers find recurring meetings.
6. **Handle your inbox once per item** for one morning: do, delegate,
   diarise, delete. Count how often you would have re-read something.
7. **Close tomorrow tonight.** Five minutes, three lines. Then stop.

## Summary

- Time and attention are different resources; four fragmented hours are worth
  less than two whole ones, because each re-entry costs recovery and some
  work never reaches depth at all.
- Shape the day to your own pattern: difficult work in your best window,
  shallow work in the trough, batched by type rather than by arrival.
- A block survives if it is calendared as busy with a real name, announced
  along with how to reach you, and has a genuine escape hatch.
- "My team needs access" is true, and access is not constant availability —
  if two hours is impossible, that is a delegation finding.
- Batch messages rather than monitoring them; handle each once; be reliably
  responsive rather than erratically instant.
- Close the day in five minutes and leave a thread to pick up. And stop —
  fatigue is a decision-making risk nobody puts on a register.
