# Where Your Time Actually Goes

Every manager believes they know how they spend their week, and almost every
manager is wrong — usually by a wide margin, and always in the same
direction. This chapter is about measuring before improving, because the
productivity advice that fails is the advice applied to an imagined week
rather than the real one.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Track a week honestly enough to be surprised by it.
- Separate the work only you can do from the work that merely arrived at you.
- Put a figure on what interruption costs you in a year.
- Recognise the difference between being busy and being effective.
- Say what you would stop doing if you had to lose four hours a week.

## Measure first

The instruction is dull and it is the whole chapter: **log a week before
changing anything.** Not in detail — in fifteen-minute blocks, with a
one-word label. Meetings, email, a named piece of real work, interruption,
travel, admin.

Do it for five working days. Two things reliably happen.

The first is that the totals surprise you. Most managers substantially
underestimate time in meetings and email, and substantially overestimate
uninterrupted work — often believing they got three or four hours of it on a
day the log shows forty minutes.

The second is more useful: **the act of logging changes what you do**, because
writing "interruption — 25 min" four times before lunch is a different
experience from vaguely feeling busy. Some of the improvement arrives before
you have analysed anything.

The objection — *"I'm too busy to track my time"* — is the finding, not a
reason to skip it.

## Four categories worth separating

When the log is done, sort it. Not by urgency, and not by project, but by
what the time actually was:

| | What it is | The question it raises |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Only you** | Decisions, direction, difficult conversations, work needing your specific authority or judgement | Are you getting enough of it, and when in the day? |
| **Better by you** | Work you do well and could teach | What would it take to make this "someone else"? |
| **Anyone** | Arrived at you by habit, proximity or history | Why is it still yours? |
| **Nobody** | Reports nobody reads, meetings with no decision, rework | Who would notice if it stopped? |

The fourth row is where the fastest gains are, and it is the one people skip
because it feels like an admission. Everybody has some. A report you produce
monthly that nobody has asked about in a year is not a small thing to find —
it is hours back with no downside at all.

The third row is Chapter 4's subject: work that is yours because nobody ever
decided otherwise, which is the same "deciding by default" the Decision
Making course names.

## Worked example: what interruption costs

The cost of an interruption is not its length. It is the length plus the time
to get back to where you were, which for anything requiring concentration
runs to ten or fifteen minutes.

Take a manager interrupted fifteen times a day — a modest figure for anyone
with an open door and a phone — with twelve minutes of recovery each:

| | |
| --- | ---: |
| Recovery time per day | 15 × 12 min = **3 hours** |
| Per five-day week | **15 hours** |
| Per year, at 48 weeks | **720 hours** |
| Valued at ₦2,400 an hour | **₦1,728,000** |

**Seven hundred and twenty hours a year is more than four months of a working
week's worth of Tuesdays** — spent not on the interruptions themselves, which
are counted separately, but purely on re-entering work you had already
started.

Three honest caveats. Not all interruptions are waste — many are the job, and
a manager who is never interruptible is a bottleneck wearing headphones.
Recovery time varies enormously with the task; answering a quick question
between emails costs almost nothing, while the same question during
concentrated work costs the full amount. And the ₦1,728,000 is not money you
can recover — it is capacity, and it only becomes real if the freed time goes
somewhere that matters.

The point survives all three. The cost is far larger than the interruptions
appear to be, and it is invisible precisely because it is distributed in
two-minute pieces.

## Busy is not effective

The most dangerous state for a manager is a full day that produced nothing
that mattered — and it is dangerous because it *feels* like work. The signals
are recognisable:

- You were reactive all day and could not name a single thing you moved
  forward.
- The important work keeps sliding to "tomorrow", where it has been for a
  fortnight.
- Your working day extends but your output does not.
- You are the bottleneck on several things at once, and each of them is
  waiting for a decision you have not had a clear hour to make.

The Data course's distinction applies exactly: activity is not output. Hours
worked is a vanity metric — it always goes up, it feels virtuous, and no
realistic value of it would change what you do next week.

## Do this at work

1. **Log one week in fifteen-minute blocks.** One word per block. Do not
   change your behaviour while logging — the point is a true baseline.
2. **Total four numbers**: meetings, email and messages, uninterrupted work,
   interruptions. Compare them to what you would have guessed.
3. **Sort everything into the four categories.** Only you, better by you,
   anyone, nobody.
4. **Find one thing in "nobody".** A report, a recurring meeting, a check
   nobody reads. Stop it for a month and see who notices.
5. **Count your interruptions for one day.** Multiply by twelve minutes. The
   annual figure is usually the thing that changes behaviour.
6. **Note when your "only you" work happened.** If it is all after 5pm, your
   day is being spent on other people's priorities and yours is being done on
   your own time.
7. **Answer the four-hour question.** If you had to give up four hours a week
   permanently, what would you stop? Then ask why you have not.

## Summary

- Measure before improving: log a week in fifteen-minute blocks. The totals
  will surprise you, and logging itself changes behaviour.
- "I'm too busy to track my time" is the finding, not a reason to skip it.
- Sort time into only-you, better-by-you, anyone, and nobody. The fastest
  gains are in "nobody", which is the row people avoid looking at.
- Interruption costs recovery, not just duration: fifteen a day at twelve
  minutes is 720 hours a year — capacity rather than cash, and invisible
  because it arrives in two-minute pieces.
- Not all interruption is waste, and a manager who is never interruptible is
  a bottleneck.
- Hours worked is a vanity metric. A full day that moved nothing forward is
  the most dangerous state because it feels like work.
