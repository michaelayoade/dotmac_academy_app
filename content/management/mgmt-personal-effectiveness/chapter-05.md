# Sustaining It

Every technique in this course works for about three weeks. What happens
after that decides whether any of it was worth learning. This chapter is
about the harder part: keeping a working system through a bad month,
recognising overload as a resourcing fact rather than a personal failing, and
escalating it in a way that produces a decision instead of sympathy.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Recover a system after it has lapsed, without starting over.
- Tell the difference between a busy period and sustained overload.
- Escalate capacity with numbers rather than with feelings.
- Recognise the warning signs in yourself and in your team.
- Choose the small number of habits worth keeping when everything else goes.

FIGURE `MGT-PE-05` *The system that survives is the one you can restart in ten minutes after abandoning it.*

## Systems lapse. Plan for the restart.

You will stop doing the weekly review. Something will go wrong, a fortnight
will disappear, and the lists will drift out of date. This is normal and it
is not evidence that the system was wrong.

What matters is that the restart is cheap:

- **Do not rebuild from scratch.** The instinct after a lapse is to start a
  new list, a new tool, a fresh system. That is how people end up on their
  fifth tool with nothing carried forward. Take the old list and correct it.
- **Empty the backlog once, ruthlessly.** After a lapse most of the captured
  items are dead — the moment passed, someone else did it, it stopped
  mattering. Deleting is a legitimate outcome and usually the commonest one.
- **Restart with the smallest viable version.** One list and a weekly review.
  Add nothing else until those two have survived a month.

**A system's real quality is how quickly you can pick it up after dropping
it** — not how well it runs when everything is calm.

## Busy period, or overload?

These need different responses, and confusing them is where damage happens.

| | Busy period | Sustained overload |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duration | Weeks, with a visible end | Months, with no end anyone can name |
| Cause | A specific event — a launch, a season, an absence | The standing shape of the job |
| After it | You catch up | There is no "after" |
| Response | Push, protect the essentials, recover | Change the work, the resourcing, or the expectations |

Pushing through a busy period is right. Pushing through sustained overload
does not fix it — it conceals it, which guarantees continuation, because from
the outside a job being done is a job that is resourced correctly.

This is the same invisibility as Chapter 2's absorbed commitment, at a larger
scale: **absorbed overload is invisible overload, and invisible overload never
gets resourced.**

## Escalating capacity so it lands

"I'm overloaded" invites sympathy and changes nothing, because it is not
actionable and every manager hears it from everyone. What lands is the same
message with arithmetic and a decision attached.

The shape:

- **The capacity, in numbers.** From Chapter 1's log and the Team Leadership
  figure: this is my plannable time, this is what is committed, here is the
  gap.
- **The consequence, named.** Not "things will slip" but *which* things, and
  by when — this is exactly the Communication course's led update, applied to
  your own workload.
- **Options, with costs.** Stop something, move a date, add a person, accept
  lower quality on a named item. Bring the options rather than the problem.
- **A recommendation.** You know the work better than they do; say which
  option you would take and why.
- **A decision request with a date.** "I need a call on this by Friday, and
  here is what I will do by default if I do not have one."

Two things make this work that are not obvious. It is **not a complaint** —
it is a prioritisation question handed to the person who owns the priorities,
which is precisely their job. And it is far more effective *before* the
failure than after: the same conversation held once things have slipped is
about blame, and the same numbers stop being persuasive.

## The warning signs

In yourself: the working day extending without output rising; the important
work sliding for weeks; irritability; decisions deferred because there is no
clear hour to make them; and the tell that arrives first, **stopping doing the
things that keep you effective** — the review, the exercise, the block, the
one-to-ones.

In your team, from the Team Leadership and People courses: the reliable person
quietly absorbing more, disengagement, withdrawal, and leave not taken.
Managers under sustained load stop noticing these, which is what makes
overload a team risk rather than a personal one.

And the honest one: **fatigue degrades judgement before it degrades
performance.** You will still deliver, and the decisions will get worse first,
invisibly. That is why "I'm coping" is a poor test — a manager who is coping
by working every evening is running a decision-making risk that will show up
somewhere other than their own output.

## What to keep when everything goes

When a month goes badly, most of this course goes with it. Keep three things,
in this order:

1. **Capture.** If commitments are still landing somewhere, nothing is
   permanently lost and recovery is a sorting exercise.
2. **One protected block a week.** Not daily — one. It is the difference
   between a bad month and a bad quarter, because it is the only time the
   important-but-not-urgent work gets any oxygen at all.
3. **The one-to-ones.** They are the first thing managers cancel under
   pressure and the most expensive to lose, because everything you would have
   found out early you now find out late.

Everything else — the tagging, the templates, the batching, the review — can
lapse and be restarted in ten minutes. These three cannot be restarted; what
they would have caught is simply gone.

## Do this at work

1. **Write your restart procedure** — three lines, kept with the list. When
   the system lapses, you will not want to think about how to resume.
2. **Classify your current state.** Busy period with an end you can name, or
   sustained overload? Be honest; the responses are opposite.
3. **Build the escalation.** Capacity, gap, named consequences, options with
   costs, a recommendation, a decision date. One page.
4. **Have it early.** Before the slip, while the numbers are still
   persuasive rather than defensive.
5. **Check the first tell.** What have you stopped doing that used to keep
   you effective? That is your earliest warning and it is already showing.
6. **Look at your team for the same signs** — the reliable person absorbing
   more, leave not taken, someone gone quiet.
7. **Protect the three.** Capture, one block, the one-to-ones. Let the rest
   go when it must, and restart it without guilt.

## Summary

- Systems lapse; the quality of a system is how cheaply it restarts, not how
  well it runs when calm. Correct the old list rather than starting a fifth.
- Distinguish a busy period from sustained overload — pushing through the
  first is right, and pushing through the second conceals it, which
  guarantees it continues.
- Absorbed overload is invisible overload, and invisible overload never gets
  resourced.
- Escalate with capacity numbers, named consequences, options with costs, a
  recommendation and a decision date — before the slip, not after.
- Fatigue degrades judgement before performance, so "I'm coping" is a poor
  test.
- When a month goes badly, keep capture, one protected block, and the
  one-to-ones. Everything else restarts in ten minutes; what those three
  would have caught does not come back.
