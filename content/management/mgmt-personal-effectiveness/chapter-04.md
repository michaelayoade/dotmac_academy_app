# Systems That Carry the Load

Willpower is a poor scheduling mechanism. Everything a manager holds in their
head — commitments, follow-ups, half-promises, the thing they must remember to
check on the 9th — occupies attention whether or not it is being worked on,
and leaks at exactly the wrong moment. This chapter is about the small number
of systems that hold it instead, and about the habit that decides whether any
of them survive.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Get commitments out of your head and into something trusted.
- Run one list rather than several partial ones.
- Track what you are waiting on from other people.
- Build routines that make good practice the default rather than a decision.
- Say why a system you do not trust is worse than none.

## Capture everything, decide later

The head is a bad storage device and a good processor. Anything held only in
memory does two damaging things: it consumes background attention, and it
surfaces at random — usually at 3am, or in the meeting where you cannot act on
it.

So: **capture immediately, decide later.** One place, always to hand, where a
commitment goes the moment it is made — a notebook, an app, a card, it does
not matter, provided it is single and always present. The friction of getting
something in must be near zero, because a capture system you have to go and
find is a capture system you will bypass while busy.

Then process the captures on a rhythm, deciding what each one is: an action
with an owner and a date, something delegated, something diarised for a future
date, a note, or nothing at all.

## One list, and what it costs to have several

Most managers have four or five partial lists: an app, a notebook, flagged
emails, a whiteboard, and a set of things they simply remember. That is not a
system, and it fails in a specific way — **nothing can be ranked against
anything else**, so priority collapses to whatever is in front of you. Chapter
2's 1, 2, 3 is impossible across five surfaces.

Consolidating is usually a one-hour job and disproportionately valuable. What
the single list needs:

- **Everything on it**, including the things that feel too small and the
  things you are avoiding, which are usually the same things.
- **One owner per item** — you, or a name if delegated. The Team Leadership
  rule applies to your own list.
- **A date where a date matters**, not on everything, or dates stop meaning
  anything.
- **A next action, phrased as an action.** "Kano project" is not an action
  and will be skipped every time you read it; "call Musa about the Kano
  permit" gets done.

## The waiting-on list

The item most managers never track is the one that costs the most: **things
you are waiting for from other people.** Untracked, they fail silently — you
discover on the deadline that the supplier never replied, the approval never
came, the colleague forgot.

A waiting-on list is three columns: what, who, since when. Reviewed weekly, it
turns a whole class of failure into a five-minute chase. And it is where
Chapter 3 of the Project Management course's external dependencies live at
personal scale — the approval that "takes two weeks" takes two weeks from
submission, and nobody but you is counting.

## The weekly review

This is the habit that makes every system above survive, and the one people
drop first. Thirty minutes, same time each week:

1. **Empty the captures.** Everything in the notebook, the inbox, the notes —
   processed into actions, delegations, diary entries or discarded.
2. **Look at the waiting-on list.** Chase anything older than it should be.
3. **Re-rank.** Chapter 2's order, once, deliberately, rather than
   continuously under pressure.
4. **Look at the week ahead.** Where are the protected blocks? What arrives
   that needs preparation? What will realistically not happen?
5. **Look back one week.** What did you not get to, and why? The pattern
   across a month is more informative than any single week.

Without this, the lists silently become inaccurate, and an inaccurate list is
worse than no list — because you stop trusting it, and a system you do not
trust you will quietly bypass while continuing to maintain it. **That is the
worst of both: the overhead of a system and the anxiety of not having one.**

## Routines and defaults

The highest-return systems are the ones that remove a decision entirely.

- **Recurring blocks** for the work that otherwise slides — the one-to-ones,
  the planning hour, the review. Decided once rather than weekly.
- **Templates** for anything you write repeatedly: the status update, the
  handover, the brief. The Communication course's skeletons are exactly this.
- **Checklists for anything with consequences and steps** — a site visit, a
  new starter's first day, a month-end. Checklists are not for people who
  forget; they are for people who are experienced enough to be confident and
  busy enough to skip step four.
- **Defaults that make the right thing automatic**: a standing agenda, a
  filing convention, a rule that every meeting ends with who-what-by-when.

Each of these converts a repeated decision into a decided-once. That is the
whole mechanism, and it is why a modest routine beats an ambitious system
nobody sustains.

## When the system is the problem

A caution, because productivity systems attract a particular failure: **it is
possible to spend more time maintaining the system than the system saves.**
Elaborate tagging, nested categories, a tool migration every quarter,
re-organising the list instead of doing the work on it. That is
procrastination with good posture.

The test is simple. **Does the system reduce the number of things you are
holding in your head, and does it survive a genuinely bad week?** If it needs
a calm week to maintain, it will fail exactly when you need it. Simpler and
sustained beats sophisticated and abandoned, every time.

## Do this at work

1. **Pick one capture place** and put it where you already are. Anything
   you have to go and find will be bypassed.
2. **Consolidate your lists into one.** An hour, once. Include the small
   things and the ones you have been avoiding.
3. **Rewrite five items as actions.** If an item is a noun, it will be
   skipped every time you read it.
4. **Start a waiting-on list.** What, who, since when. Populate it from
   memory now — most people find something already overdue.
5. **Book the weekly review** as a recurring thirty minutes. It is the habit
   that keeps the others alive.
6. **Build one checklist** for something with consequences and steps. Not
   because you forget, but because you are confident and busy.
7. **Delete one piece of system overhead** — a category, a tag, a tool you
   maintain but do not use.

## Summary

- Capture immediately and decide later; anything held only in memory consumes
  attention and surfaces at the wrong moment.
- One list, not five — across several surfaces nothing can be ranked against
  anything, so priority collapses to whatever is in front of you.
- Phrase items as actions with owners and dates where dates matter.
- Track what you are waiting on from other people; untracked, it fails
  silently and is discovered on the deadline.
- The weekly review is what keeps every other system honest, and an
  untrusted list is worse than none — the overhead of a system with the
  anxiety of not having one.
- Routines, templates, checklists and defaults convert repeated decisions
  into decided-once. Simpler and sustained beats sophisticated and abandoned.
