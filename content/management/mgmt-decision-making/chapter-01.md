# How Decisions Go Wrong

Managers are paid, more than anything, for the quality of their decisions. Yet
most of us have never examined *how* we decide — we trust judgement, and
judgement is quietly shaped by biases, pressure, and habit. Before learning
frameworks for deciding well, it pays to study the standard ways deciding
goes wrong, because you will recognise every one of them in your own recent
history.

## Decision quality is not outcome quality

First, a distinction that underpins this whole course: **a good decision and
a good outcome are different things.** You can decide well and get unlucky;
you can decide recklessly and get away with it. If you judge decisions only
by results, you will learn the wrong lessons — punishing sound reasoning
that ran into bad luck, and repeating gambles that happened to pay off.
Evaluate decisions by what was known *at the time*: the information gathered,
the options considered, the risks weighed. That is also the fair way to judge
your team's decisions, and it is what makes honest reporting of bad news
possible.

## The standard biases

Human judgement takes shortcuts. Four of them cause most managerial damage:

- **Anchoring.** The first number or option on the table drags every later
  estimate toward it. The first quote you receive frames the negotiation;
  the first diagnosis in the room frames the investigation. Defence: generate
  your own estimate *before* looking at anyone else's, and treat the first
  option as one candidate, never the baseline.
- **Confirmation bias.** Once you suspect an answer, you notice evidence that
  supports it and discount evidence that doesn't. It is why investigations
  "confirm" their first theory so often. Defence: ask explicitly, "what would
  I expect to see if this theory were *wrong*?" — and go look for that.
- **Sunk cost.** Money and effort already spent argue for continuing —
  "we've invested too much to stop now." But spent resources are gone under
  every future option; only future costs and benefits differ between choices.
  Defence: ask "knowing what we know now, would we *start* this today?" If
  no, continuing needs a better argument than momentum.
- **Availability.** Recent and vivid events feel more likely than they are.
  Last month's dramatic failure dominates planning while the slow routine
  risk that occurs weekly goes unmanaged. Defence: check records, not
  memories, when estimating how often things happen.

Two more deserve honourable mention: **overconfidence** (our estimates are
routinely tighter than reality — widen the range) and **status quo bias**
(the current arrangement gets a free pass no new option would survive).

## The urgency trap

Pressure creates a false binary: *decide right now, or fail*. Real situations
usually offer more room than they advertise, and the first casualty of
manufactured urgency is option generation — under pressure, people compare
the two options in front of them instead of asking whether better ones exist.

Discipline for hot moments:

- Ask "what actually happens if we decide tomorrow instead of now?" Often
  the honest answer is "nothing", and one day buys real information.
- Distinguish **decision deadlines** (after which an option expires) from
  **discomfort deadlines** (the pressure to make the anxiety stop). Only the
  first is real.
- When time truly is short, shrink the decision instead of rushing it —
  make the smallest commitment that keeps options open.

## Deciding by default

The most invisible failure mode is the decision nobody made. Not renewing a
contract review, not reassessing a struggling project, not choosing a
priority — each is a decision made by the calendar rather than by you, and
"we never actually decided that" is its signature phrase. Deferral is
sometimes right; *unconscious* deferral never is. If you are postponing,
postpone on purpose: say what you are waiting for and when it will be
revisited.

## Decision debt

Every unmade or badly-made decision accrues interest. The team that never
chose between two conflicting priorities pays daily in friction; the
ambiguous ownership question deferred in January becomes the turf war in
June. Like financial debt, some decision debt is a sensible loan — deferring
a choice until information arrives. The toxic kind is unacknowledged debt:
nobody knows the decision is pending, so nobody is collecting the
information, and the cost compounds silently.

Keep a visible list of open decisions the way you keep a task list: the
decision, its owner, what it is waiting on, and its revisit date. Chapter 5
returns to this as the decision log.

## Summary

- Judge decisions by the process at the time, not the outcome — otherwise
  you learn the wrong lessons and punish honesty.
- Know the big four biases — anchoring, confirmation, sunk cost,
  availability — and their specific defences.
- Urgency is often manufactured: separate real decision deadlines from
  discomfort, and shrink decisions when time is genuinely short.
- Never defer unconsciously. A postponed decision needs a named reason, an
  owner, and a revisit date.
- Track open decisions as visible debt before the interest compounds.
