# Group Decisions & Buy-In

Most significant decisions at work involve other people — as sources of
information, as stakeholders, or as the ones who must execute what's chosen.
Groups can make decisions better: more perspectives, more error-catching,
more ownership of the result. They can also make them worse: slower, blander,
and dominated by the loudest voice. The difference is structure. This chapter
covers running decisions *with* people — and landing them so they stick.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Name the standard group-decision failures and design meetings against
  them.
- Run a meeting that ends in decisions rather than in another meeting.
- Use disagree-and-commit honestly, including what it asks of the person who
  lost.
- Keep a decision log whose entries save time six months later.
- Communicate a decision to the people it went against.

## Groups: the promise and the standard failures

Use a group when the decision needs knowledge spread across several heads,
or when execution depends on the group owning the outcome. Know the three
standard failure modes going in:

- **Groupthink.** Cohesion becomes agreement-pressure; doubts go unspoken
  and the group is more confident than any member. Counter it by *assigning*
  dissent — someone's explicit job is to argue the best case against the
  favourite — so scepticism is a role, not a rebellion.
- **Anchoring on the first or loudest voice.** Once a senior or confident
  person speaks, later contributions orbit their position. Counter: collect
  views *silently and in parallel* first (everyone writes their answer
  before anyone speaks), and if you are the senior person, **speak last**.
- **Diffusion of responsibility.** "The group decided" means nobody
  answers for it. Groups inform and advise; per Chapter 2, the decision
  still has one named decider — or an explicitly named voting rule agreed
  in advance. Ambiguity about *how the call gets made* poisons more meetings
  than the call itself.

## Meetings that actually decide

A decision meeting is a different animal from a discussion meeting, and it
goes wrong mostly by being unprepared or unbounded:

1. **Frame before the meeting.** Circulate a short note: the decision to be
   made, the criteria, the options with their honest pros and cons, and a
   recommendation. People who arrive having read a page argue about
   substance; people who arrive cold spend the hour reconstructing context.
   The written note also disciplines *you* — half-formed proposals collapse
   on paper before they waste a room.
2. **Separate the phases.** Clarify facts first, then debate merits, then
   decide. Groups that blur these loop endlessly: every new fact reopens the
   debate, every opinion smuggles in a fact-claim.
3. **End with the sentence.** "We are deciding X. The call is Y. Ada owns
   the first step by Friday. We revisit if Z happens." If that sentence
   can't be said, the meeting discussed but did not decide — schedule the
   decision, not another discussion.

## Disagree and commit

Consensus — everyone genuinely agreeing — is lovely and rare. Waiting for it
on every decision hands a veto to the most reluctant person in the room and
produces compromise options that nobody believes in. The workable standard
is **disagree and commit**: everyone gets heard, the decider decides, and
then everyone — including the people who argued otherwise — executes
wholeheartedly.

Two conditions make it legitimate rather than authoritarian. First, the
disagreement must have been *genuinely heard*: engaged with on its merits,
reflected in the stated reasoning, not just permitted as a speech. Second,
commitment must be real in both directions: the dissenter doesn't slow-walk
or "I told you so" the decision — and the decider doesn't punish the
dissent. A team where yesterday's dissenter executes today's decision hard
is healthy; a team where dissent quietly disappears is not, and the silence
will cost you exactly when you most need a warning.

Explicitly saying "we're in disagree-and-commit; I've heard the case against
and here's why we're proceeding" is not weakness. It marks that dissent was
processed, and it gives the disagreement dignity while ending it.

## Write decisions down: the decision log

Chapter 1 introduced decision debt; the **decision log** is its ledger. For
every significant decision, record five lines:

- **What we decided** — the sentence.
- **When, and who decided.**
- **Why** — the reasoning and the main alternative rejected, in two or
  three sentences.
- **What would change our minds** — the revisit trigger or date.
- **Who was told.**

The payoff compounds. Six months later, "why do we do it this way?" has an
answer, so the decision isn't re-litigated from scratch. New team members
inherit reasoning, not just rules. And when circumstances change, the
recorded trigger tells you which decisions deserve reopening — versus the
constant low-grade renegotiation of everything that teams without a log
suffer.

A filled-in entry is shorter than people expect — this is a real one's worth
of detail, not a project document:

| Field | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| **Decision** | Move to Vendor B for cable supply from 1 September |
| **When / who** | 12 August · Operations Manager (decider) · Finance and Field Lead consulted |
| **Why** | Weighted matrix put B ahead of C by 0.10 — effectively a tie — but B holds regional stock, and the two stock-outs this year both cost more than the price difference. A was cheapest and failed the reliability gate. |
| **What would change our minds** | Two late deliveries in any quarter, or C opening a regional depot. Review at the 12-month renewal regardless. |
| **Who was told** | Field team briefed 13 August; A and C informed 14 August |

Four things that entry does which memory cannot. It records the **rejected
alternative and why**, so next year's "why aren't we using the cheap one?"
takes thirty seconds rather than a meeting. It names a **decider**, so the
decision has an owner rather than a committee. It states a **trigger**, so
reopening is evidence-driven instead of mood-driven. And it captures the
*reasoning* — the stock-out costs — which is the part that actually transfers
to the next decision.

Note too what it honestly admits: the matrix was effectively a tie. Logging
that is more useful than pretending the analysis was decisive, because it
tells a future reader the decision was finely balanced and is worth
revisiting on new evidence rather than defending on principle.

## Communicating the decision

A decision isn't finished until the people affected know what was decided,
why, and what it means for them. Tell the people it went against *first* and
where possible personally — hearing it second-hand adds insult to
disappointment. Communicate the reasoning, not just the verdict; per Chapter
2, people accept decisions that engaged their objections far better than
verdicts from a black box. And keep the reasoning honest: if the deciding
factor was cost, say cost — a diplomatic fiction discovered later costs more
trust than the true reason ever would.

## Do this at work

1. **Audit your last meeting.** How many items ended in a decision with an
   owner and a date? Items that ended in "let's discuss further" are the
   ones to redesign.
2. **State the decision type up front.** Before the next contested item, say
   whether you are consulting or deciding by consensus, and who decides if
   it stalls. Most meeting resentment is role confusion.
3. **Start your decision log.** Five fields, one table. Backfill the three
   most-questioned decisions in your area — those are the ones being
   re-litigated.
4. **Record a rejected alternative.** For one recent decision, write down
   what you did not choose and why. That single line prevents most repeat
   arguments.
5. **Set one revisit trigger.** Name the evidence that would reopen a
   decision you have made. Then you can hold the line calmly, because
   reopening has a condition rather than a mood.
6. **Tell someone it went against.** Explain the reasoning to a person whose
   preferred option lost. Consultation survives being overruled only when
   the overruled hear why.

## Summary

- Use groups for spread-out knowledge and needed ownership; structure
  against groupthink (assign dissent), anchoring (silent parallel input,
  senior speaks last), and diffusion (one named decider or explicit rule).
- Decision meetings: framed in writing beforehand, phases separated, ended
  with the who-does-what sentence.
- Replace consensus-hunting with disagree-and-commit — legitimate only when
  dissent is genuinely engaged and commitment runs both ways.
- Keep a five-line decision log: what, who/when, why, revisit trigger, who
  was told. It ends re-litigation and preserves reasoning.
- Land decisions deliberately: affected people first, reasoning included,
  true reasons only.
