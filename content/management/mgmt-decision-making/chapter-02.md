# Structured Decision Frameworks

Judgement improves when it has scaffolding. This chapter gives you four
pieces: sizing the decision before choosing a method, setting criteria before
comparing options, two workhorse tools (the decision matrix and cost–benefit
thinking), and clarity about *who* decides. None of them removes judgement —
they organise it, and they make your reasoning explainable to others, which
is half of what a manager's decisions must be.

## First, size the decision

The most useful early question is not "what should we choose?" but "**how
much deciding does this deserve?**" Two properties size a decision:

- **Reversibility.** A *reversible* decision (a two-way door) can be undone
  at modest cost: a trial arrangement, a process change, most purchases. An
  *irreversible* decision (a one-way door) cannot: signing a long contract,
  dismissing someone, public commitments, spending the reserve.
- **Stakes.** What is the size of the impact if it goes wrong — trivial,
  painful, or existential?

The rule: **decide reversible, low-stakes questions quickly — with about 70%
of the information you'd like — and correct course as you learn.** The cost
of deciding slowly exceeds the cost of the occasional wrong-but-fixable
call. Reserve the heavy machinery — analysis, consultation, deliberate
delay — for irreversible or high-stakes decisions. Teams that agonise over
two-way doors and rush through one-way doors have the sizing exactly
backwards, and it is a common pattern.

## Criteria before options

When people compare options directly ("A feels stronger than B"), the
argument loops, because each person is silently weighing different things.
Break the loop by writing down **what matters, before debating what wins**:

1. List the criteria this decision should be judged on — cost, speed, risk,
   quality, effect on the team, whatever genuinely applies.
2. Mark which criteria are *requirements* (an option failing them is out)
   versus *preferences* (better is better).
3. Weight the preferences roughly — not with false precision, just "cost
   matters about twice as much as speed here."

Criteria-first has a second benefit: it exposes disagreements early and at
the right level. Arguing "is speed or cost more important?" is a productive
disagreement; "I just prefer option B" is not.

## The decision matrix

For multi-option, multi-criteria choices — vendors, candidates, locations,
tools — the decision matrix is the workhorse:

1. Options as rows; weighted criteria as columns.
2. Score each option against each criterion (1–5 is plenty).
3. Multiply by weights, sum, and compare totals.

Treat the total as a **conversation-starter, not a verdict**. Its real value
is what it surfaces: an option that wins on points but fails a requirement; a
"favourite" that loses everywhere except one overweighted criterion; two
close totals that tell you the decision matters less than the argument
suggested. If the result offends your intuition, don't override it silently —
find the criterion or weight your intuition says is wrong and argue *that*.
Either the matrix updates or your intuition does; both are wins.

## Cost–benefit, honestly

Cost–benefit thinking is simple to state — will this be worth more than it
costs? — and routinely done dishonestly. The traps:

- **Counting benefits but not all costs.** Include the ongoing costs
  (maintenance, training, attention) and the **opportunity cost**: what the
  same money, people, or months would earn doing the next-best thing.
  "Worth doing" is not the bar; "worth doing *instead*" is.
- **Certainty asymmetry.** Costs are usually concrete and immediate;
  benefits are estimated and future. Discount the benefits for optimism, not
  the costs.
- **Ignoring the do-nothing option.** "Compared to what?" Every proposal
  should beat an honestly-assessed status quo, including the status quo's
  real deterioration if that's what's happening.

## Who decides — and who doesn't

Half of slow, resentful decision-making is role confusion: everyone believes
they are a decider. For any significant decision, name four roles explicitly:

- **The decider** — exactly one person (or one named forum) who makes the
  call.
- **Consulted** — people whose input is gathered *before* the call. Being
  consulted means being heard, not holding a veto; say that out loud.
- **Informed** — people told after, promptly, with reasoning.
- **Delegated-to** — sometimes the right move is recognising the decision
  belongs a level down, with you consulted instead.

The discipline matters most on the boundary: when you consult your team and
then decide differently from the majority view, explain the reasoning. People
accept decisions that went against them far better than decisions that
ignored them — being overruled with reasons is respect; being unheard is not.

## Summary

- Size decisions first: fast with ~70% information through two-way doors;
  slow and deliberate through one-way doors. Most teams have this backwards.
- Fix criteria — requirements, preferences, rough weights — before
  comparing options, so disagreements surface at the right level.
- Use a decision matrix to organise multi-option choices, and treat
  intuition-versus-matrix conflicts as information, not embarrassment.
- Do cost–benefit honestly: all costs including opportunity cost, optimism-
  discounted benefits, against a real status quo.
- One named decider per decision; consulted means heard, not veto; explain
  the call to the people it went against.
