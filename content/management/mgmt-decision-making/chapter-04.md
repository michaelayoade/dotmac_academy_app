# Deciding Under Uncertainty

Every interesting decision is made with incomplete information — if the facts
fully determined the answer, it wouldn't need a decider. The skill is not
eliminating uncertainty (you can't) but deciding *well inside it*: knowing
how much information is enough, weighing outcomes by likelihood, protecting
against ruin, and recognising when waiting has quietly become the most
expensive option on the table.

## How much information is enough?

Information has a cost curve. Early facts are cheap and change your decision;
late facts are expensive and mostly confirm it. Three questions locate the
stopping point:

- **Would more information actually change the choice?** If every realistic
  answer to the outstanding question leads to the same decision, the
  question is entertainment. Decide.
- **What does waiting cost?** Delay is never free: options expire, problems
  compound, teams idle, competitors move. Name the cost of a week explicitly
  — "waiting is safe" is an assumption, not a fact.
- **Is the missing information even gettable?** Some uncertainty resolves
  with a phone call; some only resolves by acting. Distinguish *research
  problems* (go find out) from *irreducible uncertainty* (no one knows —
  deciding is the only way to learn).

Chapter 2's sizing rule extends here: through two-way doors, act at roughly
70% information; the last 30% costs more than the occasional reversal. For
one-way doors, buy more certainty — but even there, 100% is not on sale.

## Think in expected value — with a ruin clause

Weigh outcomes by their likelihood. An option with a 70% chance of a good
outcome and bearable downside usually beats one with certainty of mediocrity
— and repeated over a career of decisions, the arithmetic compounds. You
don't need precise probabilities; even rough ones ("more likely than not",
"maybe one in five") beat the unspoken default of treating vivid outcomes as
certain and dull ones as impossible.

But expected value has one override: **never accept a small chance of ruin
for a routine gain.** A bet that loses everything 5% of the time is not a
"95% good bet" — repeated, it is a guaranteed loss; it just hasn't happened
yet. Before choosing, ask *"what is the worst case, and can we survive it?"*
If the answer is no, the expected value is irrelevant — change the option
until the worst case is survivable (smaller commitment, staged rollout,
explicit fallback), or decline it.

The practical habit: for any significant option, write three lines —
probable case, good case, worst case — and check you can live with the
third before being seduced by the second.

## Make waiting a decision

Waiting feels neutral; it is actually a choice of the status quo, made
hourly, usually without its costs being counted. Two disciplines convert
drift back into deciding:

- **Timebox the decision.** "We decide by Friday with whatever we know
  then." An honest deadline forces the information-gathering to focus and
  prevents the discomfort-driven deferral loop of Chapter 1.
- **Set tripwires.** Decide *in advance* what evidence triggers action:
  "if the numbers drop again next month, we switch suppliers"; "if the
  trial isn't showing X by the 15th, we stop." Tripwires beat vigilance
  because they are set while you are calm and specific, then fire even when
  the moment is busy and awkward.

When waiting *is* chosen, it should look like Chapter 1's conscious
deferral: named reason, what we're waiting for, revisit date.

## Small bets before big ones

When uncertainty is high and the decision is shrinkable, shrink it. A pilot
with one team before the org-wide rollout; a month's trial before the annual
contract; a manual version of the process before the expensive automation.
The point of a small bet is not timidity — it is **buying information with
the smallest possible stake**, converting irreducible-looking uncertainty
into experience. Design small bets honestly: define what result would
convince you *not* to proceed, or the pilot is a ceremony, not a test.

## Escalation is a decision skill

Part of deciding under uncertainty is knowing which decisions are no longer
yours. Escalate when the worst case exceeds your authority to accept, when
the decision commits people or money beyond your remit, or when you're
genuinely stuck between options with major consequences. Escalate *well*:
bring the decision, not the mess — "here's the situation, the options, my
recommendation, and what I need from you" (the options-paper discipline that
Chapter 5 develops). And escalate *early*: your manager can help cheaply at
the fork in the road, expensively after three exits have passed.

## Summary

- Stop gathering information when it would no longer change the choice, when
  waiting costs more than knowing, or when the uncertainty is irreducible.
- Weigh options by rough likelihood — but never accept a small chance of
  ruin for routine gain; make the worst case survivable first.
- Waiting is a decision: timebox it, set tripwires in advance, and defer
  only consciously.
- Shrink big uncertain decisions into small bets that genuinely could say
  "stop".
- Escalate when the stakes exceed your authority — early, and carrying a
  recommendation, not a mess.
