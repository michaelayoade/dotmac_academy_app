# From Metric to Action

Everything so far — the chosen few, the baselines, the honest reading —
earns nothing until a number changes what somebody does. The gap between
measurement and action is where most programmes quietly die: the dashboard
is reviewed, lamented, and filed, month after month, while operations
continue exactly as before. This chapter closes the gap: the operational
review that converts numbers into owned actions, the follow-through that
makes actions stick, and the honest management of measurement's shadow
side — gaming.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Run an operational review with a fixed shape, where every signal ends in
  an owner and a date.
- Check whether last review's actions *worked*, not merely happened.
- Diagnose before prescribing when a metric signals trouble.
- Predict how a target will be gamed, and read the guard metric that
  exposes it.
- Respond to discovered gaming as system feedback rather than as a
  character problem.

## The operational review

The engine of metric-driven management is a short, regular review — weekly
or monthly to match the tempo of the work — with a fixed shape:

1. **The same few numbers, as lines.** The team's KPIs (Chapter 2)
   against baseline and target path (Chapter 3), read with Chapter 4's
   discipline. Same order every time; familiarity is what makes change
   leap out.
2. **Explain pattern, not wobble.** Only genuine signals get discussion
   time. The chair's job is enforcing that distinction — the review that
   interrogates every downtick teaches people to dread and then decorate
   the numbers.
3. **Every signal ends in one of two sentences.** *"We understand it and
   accept it"* (a reasoned decision, recorded) or *"an owner will do X by
   date Y."* Nothing else — no thirds, no "we should look into it"
   without a name. This is the who-does-what-by-when discipline from the
   Leading a Team course, applied to numbers.
4. **Last review's actions come first.** Before any new discussion: did
   the things we said get done, and did the metric respond? This single
   habit — checking whether actions *worked*, not just happened — is what
   separates improvement loops from activity theatre, and it is the same
   closed loop the Customer Experience course builds for service signals
   and the Finance course builds for business cases.

Kept honestly, the rhythm compounds: actions accumulate into fixed causes,
fixed causes move baselines, moved baselines justify new targets. The
review is where the Decision Making course's tools get their weekly
workout — root-cause pursuit for recurring misses, evidence before
theories for surprising moves.

## Corrective action that actually corrects

When a metric signals trouble, the quality of the response decides whether
measurement was worth it:

- **Diagnose before prescribing.** The metric says *that* something
  changed, never *why*. The why lives on the floor — in the cases, the
  sites, the conversations behind the number. Walk from the chart to the
  work (Chapter 4's last instinct) before choosing a fix.
- **Fix classes, not instances.** The root-cause discipline (Decision
  Making, Chapter 3): the metric that dipped because of one bad week
  needs nothing; the metric that keeps dipping for the same category of
  reason needs that category killed, once.
- **One owner, one date, one expected effect.** State what the fix should
  do to the number by when — that prediction is what next month's review
  checks. Fixes without predicted effects can never fail, which means
  they can never teach.
- **Mind the interactions.** Corrective pressure on one family squeezes
  the others (Chapter 2). The fix for speed that quietly spends quality
  will be visible — but only because you kept the guard metric on the
  wall.

## Gaming: the shadow of every measure

State it as a law: **people optimise what you inspect, by the cheapest
available route.** Sometimes the cheapest route is better work — that's
the system succeeding. Sometimes it's redefining, reclassifying, timing,
cherry-picking, or quietly not-recording — the same energy, spent on the
scoreboard instead of the game. Gaming is not a villain problem; it is
rational behaviour under pressure, and the design response matters more
than the moral one:

- **Design against it.** Paired guard metrics (families disciplining each
  other), written definitions with owned edges (Chapter 2), and
  occasional audits of the number against raw reality — read the actual
  tickets, visit the actual site, call the actual customer.
- **Keep stakes proportionate.** The Customer Experience course's rule
  generalises: metrics wired directly to individual pay or punishment
  corrupt fastest. Numbers should carry *decisions* — where to invest,
  what to fix — and inform performance conversations as one input among
  several, not as an automatic verdict.
- **Praise the goal, not only the score.** Chapter 3's proxy warning,
  lived: managers who ask "is the thing itself better?" — and who
  publicly value an honest bad number over a decorated good one — set
  the incentive that keeps data truthful. The first time a team watches
  a manager punish the messenger of a real number, the dashboard begins
  its drift toward permanent green.
- **Treat discovered gaming as system feedback first.** Ask what pressure
  made the fake cheaper than the real, fix that, and reserve discipline
  for dishonesty that survives a fixed system.

## Worked example: the target that was met without improving anything

Return to Chapter 2's dispatch team. They adopted Definition A — *on time
means meeting the current promised date* — which, as noted then, means
re-promising resets the clock. Chapter 3's baseline put them at 74%. A
target of 85% arrives, tied to the quarterly bonus, with no guard metric.

Two quarters later:

| | Baseline | After the target | |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| On-time (Definition A) | 74% | **86%** | ✅ target met |
| Jobs rescheduled by us | 8 per 100 | **24 per 100** | ⚠️ tripled |
| On-time against the *first* promised date | 71% | **68%** | ⚠️ worse |
| Reopen rate | 6% | **11%** | ⚠️ nearly doubled |
| Satisfaction | 4.2 | **3.8** | ⚠️ fell |

The headline KPI improved by 12 points and the bonus was paid. The customer
experience got worse in every respect that was not being inspected.

Nobody falsified anything. The team found the cheapest available route to a
number they were judged on: re-promise the job that is about to run late,
and close cases faster to keep the queue moving. Definition A permitted the
first; the absence of a quality guard permitted the second. Both were
*designed in* the moment the target was set — the team merely read the design
correctly.

Notice that the failure is visible only because someone kept measuring the
other four rows. A dashboard showing on-time alone would have reported a
triumph, indefinitely. This is why Chapter 2 insists on drawing from five
families and Chapter 3 insists on pairing every push with a guard: not
because teams are dishonest, but because **an unguarded target is an
instruction, and it will be followed.**

The fix here is not a disciplinary conversation. It is to publish on-time
against the first promised date alongside Definition A, put reschedule
volume on the same page, and keep the reopen rate next to both. Then the
cheapest route to a good number is the one you actually wanted.

## A measurement culture, in the end

Zoom out and the five chapters assemble into something simpler than a
toolkit: a team where a few honest numbers are known by everyone, read
calmly, argued about rarely, and acted on relentlessly — where "what does
the data say?" is the reflex before "what does everyone think?", and
where a worsening line triggers curiosity instead of cover. That culture
is built the only way cultures are: by what the manager does on rhythm —
the review held every week, the action checked every review, the honest
miss thanked, the gamed number quietly made impossible. Measurement, done
this way, stops being surveillance and becomes what it was always for:
the team, seeing its own performance clearly, getting better on purpose.

## Do this at work

1. **Give your review a fixed shape.** Same numbers, same order, every
   time. Put last review's actions first on the agenda — before any new
   discussion.
2. **Audit your last ten actions.** For each, record whether it was done
   *and* whether the metric responded. The second column is usually much
   emptier than the first, and that gap is what separates an improvement
   loop from activity theatre.
3. **Find a signal that ended in neither sentence.** Look back through
   recent reviews for a discussed metric that produced no owner and no
   recorded decision. That is where measurement leaks into lament.
4. **Predict the gaming.** Take a target your team currently carries and
   write down the cheapest route to hitting it *without* doing the
   underlying work. You will usually think of it in under a minute — which
   tells you the team already has.
5. **Check that route.** Now find the metric that would reveal it. If no
   such metric is published, add it before the next cycle rather than after
   the next bonus.
6. **Audit one number against reality.** Read the actual tickets, visit the
   actual site, call an actual customer. Compare what you find to what the
   dashboard says. Do this occasionally and visibly; it is the cheapest
   defence measurement has.
7. **Thank one honest bad number.** Publicly. The first time a team watches
   a manager punish the messenger, the drift toward permanent green begins.

## Summary

- Run a fixed-shape review on rhythm: same lines, pattern only, every
  signal ending in either an accepted-and-recorded reason or an owner
  with a date — and start each review by checking whether last time's
  actions moved the number.
- Correct causes, not instances: diagnose on the floor, fix the class,
  predict each fix's effect so the loop can teach.
- Expect gaming as rational behaviour: design against it with guard
  metrics, owned definitions, and reality audits — and keep individual
  stakes proportionate.
- Praise honest bad numbers over decorated good ones; ask for the thing
  itself, not just the score.
- The end state is cultural: few numbers, known by all, read calmly,
  acted on relentlessly — measurement as the team seeing itself clearly.
