# Targets & Baselines

A metric tells you where you are; a target says where you intend to be.
Between the two sits the most quietly abused number in management: the
target picked from air, imposed from above, or copied from last year's
wish. Targets done well focus effort and make progress visible. Done
badly they demoralise, distort, and teach a team that numbers are
politics. The difference is mostly one discipline: **baseline before you
promise.**

## Baseline first

A **baseline** is what the metric actually does now, measured honestly
over enough periods to see its normal range. Before any target
conversation, run the metric for several weeks or months and learn three
things about it:

- **Its level** — not one good week, the boring average.
- **Its spread** — how much it bounces naturally between periods. This
  matters more than managers expect: a metric that swings ±20% in normal
  operation cannot support a target judged weekly, and Chapter 4 returns
  to why reacting to that bounce does harm.
- **Its shape** — trend and seasonality already in motion. A number
  already drifting up will hit a modest target with no effort at all; a
  seasonal dip will "miss" any flat target every year, on schedule.

Promising before baselining is how impossible targets get made in good
faith — and how easy ones get celebrated. Both corrode: the impossible
target teaches helplessness, the accidental one teaches theatre. When a
number has never been measured before, the honest first "target" is
simply: *measure it for a quarter.* That is not stalling; it is the
difference between aiming and gesturing.

## Setting the stretch

With a baseline in hand, a target is a *reasoned* stretch beyond it. The
craft:

- **Anchor to the baseline, reach with a reason.** "From 74% on-time to
  80% this quarter, because the two delay causes we found account for
  roughly that much" is a target with a theory. "95%, because it sounds
  like excellence" is a wish with a deadline. The reason matters beyond
  arithmetic: a team that can see *how* the gap closes will chase it; a
  team handed a number with no path will negotiate it or fake it.
- **Stretch without breaking.** The useful zone is reachable-with-effort:
  beyond current performance, within the team's influence (Chapter 1's
  test), achievable by better work rather than heroics. Targets that need
  permanent overtime aren't ambition; they're a plan to burn the team or
  bend the number.
- **Stage the distance.** When the honest gap is large — say the baseline
  is half of where the business needs it — set the destination *and*
  waypoints: quarterly steps, each consolidated before the next. Teams
  climb stairs; they balk at cliffs.
- **Pair every push with its guard.** Chapter 2's families discipline
  each other; targets must too. Push speed, and quality gets a
  hold-the-line floor alongside; push cost down, and satisfaction stands
  guard. An unguarded target announces exactly which corner may be cut —
  and Chapter 5 shows how reliably that announcement is heard.
- **Involve the people who must hit it.** Not as a courtesy — as
  calibration and commitment. The team knows where the slack and the
  walls are; a target they helped reason toward is theirs, one imposed
  is management's. (The Decision Making course's consulted-means-heard
  rule applies squarely.) Guard the opposite failure too: targets set
  purely bottom-up drift comfortable. The honest version is a
  negotiation between the baseline's evidence and the business's need.

## Living with targets

A target's real life begins after it's set:

- **Review against the path, not just the endpoint.** If the quarter's
  target implies steady gains, a flat first month is information now —
  not a surprise in week thirteen. (This is the budget-variance habit
  from the Finance course, applied to operations: the gap and its story,
  early enough to act.)
- **Misses are diagnosed, not punished — and not ignored.** A missed
  target triggers the root-cause question (Decision Making, Chapter 3):
  wrong theory, changed conditions, or execution? Each has a different
  fix, and only one of them is "try harder." Equally, a miss quietly
  waved through teaches that targets are decorative. The productive
  posture is the blameless review: the target was a prediction; what did
  reality teach?
- **Beware the target that becomes the ceiling.** Teams that hit a
  target early often coast — the number said "done." Where that pattern
  appears, review the *metric's* trend, not just target attainment, and
  celebrate beating the path.
- **Retire and reset deliberately.** Conditions change; a target set in
  January can be nonsense by August (re-forecasting, again from the
  Finance course). Changing a target mid-period is legitimate when the
  world changed — and corrosive when it's quietly done to convert a miss
  into a hit. The difference is daylight: reset openly, with the reason
  stated, or not at all.

## The target is not the goal

A last calibration, carried straight into Chapters 4 and 5: the target is
a *proxy* for the real goal — the faster service, the lower waste, the
happier customer. Proxies drift. When hitting the number and serving the
goal come apart — the deliveries marked on-time by redefining "on-time,"
the resolved cases that weren't — the number has stopped indicating and
started lying. Managers who praise only the number get more number;
managers who keep asking "and is the *thing itself* better?" keep the
proxy honest. Hold targets firmly and proxies lightly, and never confuse
beating the metric with doing the job.

## Summary

- Baseline before promising: level, natural spread, and existing
  trend/seasonality — several periods of honest data. A brand-new
  metric's first target is "measure it for a quarter."
- Set targets as reasoned stretches: anchored to the baseline, backed by
  a theory of how the gap closes, staged into waypoints when large.
- Pair every pushed metric with a guard metric, and set targets with the
  team — negotiated between evidence and business need.
- Review against the expected path; diagnose misses (theory, conditions,
  or execution) rather than punishing or ignoring them; reset targets
  only in daylight.
- The target is a proxy. When number and goal diverge, believe the goal
  — and ask for the thing itself, not just the score.
