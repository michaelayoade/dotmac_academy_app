# Closing & Follow-Through

Closing has accumulated more mythology than any other part of selling —
magic phrases, pressure tactics, "always be closing." The universal truth is
plainer: **if the earlier stages were done honestly, closing is simply
asking a ready buyer to decide** — and most sales that die at the end die
because nobody asked. This chapter covers asking well, and the follow-through
that turns one sale into a stream of them.

## Ask for the decision

Sellers avoid asking for the order out of fear of "no", so conversations end
warmly and vaguely — "think it over!" — and the deal quietly evaporates.
Asking is not pressure; a buyer whose problem you've understood, whose
concerns you've answered, is *helped* by a clear invitation to decide:

- **Make the ask simple and direct.** "Shall we go ahead?" "Would you like
  this one?" "If the terms work, we can start Monday — shall I send the
  agreement?" No tricks required; clarity is the technique.
- **Read readiness.** Buyers signal it: asking about delivery timelines,
  implementation details, "what happens after we sign," narrowing to one
  option, bringing in the colleague who'd use it. When the signals appear,
  stop presenting — every additional feature past readiness re-opens
  deliberation — and ask.
- **A useful test when unsure:** "Is there anything still standing between
  you and a decision?" Either you surface the remaining concern (Chapter 4
  territory) or you learn there is none — in which case, ask.

**And treat the answer as an answer.** "Yes" means confirm and move to
action the same day. "No" means learn why, part well, and log it — a
respectful no earns future business surprisingly often. The one outcome to
refuse is the eternal maybe: if a deal cannot say yes, your job is to help
it say something.

## Confirm and deliver the first days well

The close is not the finish line; it's a handover. Universal disciplines:

- **Confirm the specifics in writing** — what was bought, at what price,
  delivered when, including whatever was promised verbally along the way.
  Misremembered promises are the top source of early disputes, and the
  seller's memory is not the one that counts.
- **Buyer's remorse is real.** Between the yes and the delivery, doubt
  creeps in — the bigger the purchase, the more it creeps. A prompt
  confirmation, a fast first step, a check-in call: momentum is the
  antidote.
- **Own the handover.** If delivery passes to colleagues, make the
  transfer explicit (the buyer told the seller things the delivery team
  needs to know). To the customer, *you* are the company; "that's another
  department" is never an answer a buyer should hear from the person who
  took their money.

## The follow-through engine: repeat, referral, review

Here is the arithmetic that separates career sellers from transaction
chasers: **selling again to a satisfied customer costs a fraction of finding
a new one.** The customer who has bought, been treated well, and been
remembered is your highest-probability pipeline — and most sellers abandon
them the moment the invoice clears. The follow-through habits:

- **Check in after delivery** — not to sell, to confirm it's working.
  Problems you find are problems you fix cheaply while goodwill is high;
  the check-in call *is* the complaint-prevention system.
- **Ask for referrals at the moment of satisfaction.** "Glad it's working —
  who else do you know dealing with the same problem?" A warm introduction
  converts at many times the rate of a cold approach, and satisfied
  customers are usually happy to give one. They just need asking.
- **Stay usefully in touch** at a rhythm that serves the customer —
  renewal reminders, relevant new options, a visit when you're nearby.
  Present without pestering: each contact should carry something of value
  to *them*.

## Pipeline discipline: the habits that make numbers predictable

Chapter 1 promised that a pipeline makes results predictable. That's only
true if the records are honest. Three closing habits, universal across
markets and tools (a CRM, a notebook — the tool doesn't matter, the habit
does):

- **Record every prospect and every commitment** — who, state, what was
  said, and always a **next action with a date**. A deal with no next
  action isn't paused; it's abandoned without the honesty of saying so.
- **Keep stage labels truthful.** "Proposal out" means a proposal is out —
  not "mentioned pricing once." Inflated stages produce fictional
  forecasts, and fictional forecasts produce real droughts.
- **Review your own numbers regularly.** Conversations started, quotes
  sent, win rate, time-to-close. Not for the boss — for you: they tell you
  which chapter of this course to reread.

## Summary

- Most lost closes were never asked for: read readiness, stop selling past
  it, and invite the decision plainly.
- Take yes to action same-day, take no with grace and a lesson, and refuse
  the eternal maybe.
- Confirm everything in writing, counter buyer's remorse with momentum,
  and own the handover — to the customer, you are the company.
- Work the after-sale engine: check-ins, referrals asked for at the moment
  of satisfaction, useful ongoing contact.
- Keep honest pipeline records with a dated next action on everything —
  predictable numbers are made of truthful small entries.
