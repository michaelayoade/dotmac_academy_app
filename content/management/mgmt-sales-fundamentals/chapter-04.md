# Objections & Negotiation

An objection is not rejection — it is engagement. Buyers who don't care
don't argue; they smile and disappear. This chapter covers the universal
craft of handling concerns honestly, negotiating without giving the margin
away, and knowing when the right move is to stand firm or walk.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Treat an objection as engagement rather than rejection, and diagnose which
  family it belongs to.
- Answer each family with the response it needs instead of a reflex
  discount.
- Prepare a list of tradeable concessions and price what each is worth.
- Fund a discount the buyer asks for out of things you receive in return.
- Recognise the deal that should be declined rather than won.

## Welcome the objection

The instinctive responses to an objection — arguing back, talking faster,
caving immediately — all fail, because they treat the objection as an
attack. Treat it as information. The universal sequence:

1. **Hear it out fully.** Don't interrupt with your rebuttal; half-heard
   objections get half-relevant answers, and interruption confirms the
   buyer's suspicion that you're not listening.
2. **Clarify before answering.** "When you say it's risky — what's the
   scenario you're worried about?" Most objections arrive as headlines; the
   real concern is underneath, and answering the headline misses it.
3. **Acknowledge the legitimate part.** "You're right that switching has a
   cost" builds more credibility than pretending the concern is silly.
4. **Answer the real concern, with evidence** — then check: "does that
   settle it, or is there something else behind it?"

That last check matters because of the **smokescreen pattern**: the stated
objection often stands in for an unstated one (no authority, no budget, a
competitor preferred, fear of change). If you answer three objections and a
fourth keeps appearing, stop answering and ask directly: "It feels like
something else is holding this back — what would need to be true for you to
go ahead?" That question, asked kindly, saves weeks.

## The common objection families

Across every market, objections cluster into families, each with its honest
response:

- **Price** ("too expensive") — diagnose first, per Chapter 3: value not
  shown yet? Comparing unequal scopes? Genuine budget gap? Negotiation
  gambit? Each has a different answer; a reflex discount answers none of
  them and teaches the buyer that your first price is fiction.
- **Risk/trust** ("what if it doesn't work / we don't know you") — answer
  with evidence and structure, not reassurance: references, trials,
  guarantees, phased starts, exit terms.
- **Inertia** ("what we have is fine") — revisit the trigger and the cost
  of the status quo you found in discovery. If there genuinely is no pain,
  this was a qualification miss, not an objection to overcome.
- **Timing** ("not now") — real constraint, or soft no? Ask what changes by
  the named date. A parked deal with a concrete revisit date and reason is
  fine; "call me next quarter" repeated twice is a no wearing a coat.

## Negotiation: trade, never give

When the buyer wants better terms, the difference between negotiating and
crumbling is one universal rule: **every concession gets something in
return**. Discount for a longer commitment. Better price for a larger
volume, a referral, payment up front, a lighter service tier. Freely given
concessions don't just cost margin — they teach the buyer that pushing
produces gifts, which invites more pushing, and they quietly confirm the
price was padded all along.

Supporting principles, valid in any market:

- **Know your floor before the conversation.** Decide the walk-away point
  coolly in advance (your Decision Making course will recognise a tripwire
  when it sees one); a floor negotiated live, under pressure, always sinks.
- **Widen the field beyond price.** Delivery timing, payment schedule,
  scope, service level, duration — a deal blocked on one axis usually has
  room on another, and buyers value some things that cost you little.
- **Trade in small steps and slow down.** Big fast concessions signal
  hidden room and invite the next demand; small, reluctant, reciprocated
  ones signal a real floor nearby.
- **Protect the margin arithmetic.** Know what a discount actually does to
  the profit of the deal — at typical margins, a small percentage off the
  price is a large percentage off the profit, and volume rarely fills the
  hole. Sellers who haven't done this arithmetic give away in seconds what
  the business spends months earning.

## Worked example: funding a 15% discount without paying for it

A buyer asks for 15% off the ₦16,200 deal. Given plainly, that is a **30% cut
to margin** — ₦8,200 down to ₦5,770 — for nothing in return, and it teaches
the buyer that the list price was never real.

Instead, price the things you could receive. Each is worth something concrete,
and each therefore *funds* a slice of discount:

| What you ask for in return | What it is worth to you | Discount it funds |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 24-month commitment instead of 12 | Acquisition cost spread over twice as long: ₦1,042/month | **6.4%** |
| Drop the quarterly on-site visit (remote support instead) | ₦900/month of delivery cost removed | **5.6%** |
| Payment annually in advance | Removes the cash gap of Finance ch4; valued conservatively | **3.0%** |
| | | **15.0%** |

The buyer gets exactly the 15% they asked for. You get a customer who stays
twice as long, costs less to serve, and pays up front — and your margin is
intact. That is a trade.

Three things to notice. First, **you cannot construct this in the room**;
the values have to be worked out beforehand, which is why "know your floor"
and "widen the field" are preparation instructions rather than techniques.
Second, the buyer may refuse all three — which is useful information, because
a buyer who wants the discount but will not commit, will not change how they
are served, and will not pay early is telling you the price is the only thing
they value. Third, if they accept even one, you have learned what they
actually care about, and the negotiation has become a conversation about fit
rather than a contest over a number.

The failure mode this replaces is familiar: the seller, under pressure,
splits the difference at 7% and gets nothing for it. That is not a compromise.
It is a 14% margin cut and a lesson to the buyer that the next push will work
too.

## When standing firm wins

Some deals should not be won. The customer demanding a price below your
floor doesn't become profitable later; the buyer who bullies at the
negotiating table bullies through delivery and payment too. Declining
gracefully — "we can't do it at that number and do it properly; if things
change, I'd be glad to revisit" — protects margin, self-respect, and
market price expectations, and a surprising fraction of firmly-declined
buyers return at the real price once alternatives disappoint. The pipeline
discipline of Chapters 1–2 is what makes this possible: a seller with one
prospect can't afford a floor; a seller with a full pipeline negotiates
from calm.

## Do this at work

1. **Sort your last ten objections into families.** Price, risk, inertia,
   timing. The family that dominates tells you which earlier stage is
   underperforming — inertia usually means qualification, price usually
   means discovery.
2. **Write the diagnosis question for each.** Before responding to "too
   expensive", what will you ask? Have the sentence ready; inventing it
   under pressure is how discounts happen.
3. **Price your tradeables.** List everything you could ask for — longer
   term, prepayment, reduced scope, a reference, a case study, flexible
   scheduling — and work out what each is worth per month. This is the
   preparation that makes trading possible.
4. **Build one discount ladder.** For a live deal, show how the discount
   being requested could be funded entirely by concessions you receive.
   Take it into the meeting.
5. **Write your floor down before the next negotiation.** The number and
   the reason. A floor decided live, under pressure, always sinks.
6. **Identify one deal you should decline.** A buyer below your floor, or
   one who bullies at the table. Practise the courteous no — it is a skill,
   and it protects the delivery team who would have inherited the problem.

## Summary

- Objections are engagement: hear fully, clarify the headline, acknowledge
  what's legitimate, answer with evidence, and check for the concern
  behind the concern.
- Know the four families — price, risk, inertia, timing — and diagnose
  before responding; reflex discounts answer nothing.
- Never give a concession without getting one; set the floor in advance,
  widen the negotiation beyond price, concede small and slow.
- Do the margin arithmetic before you discount — and walk away, politely,
  from deals below the floor. A full pipeline is what buys you that calm.
