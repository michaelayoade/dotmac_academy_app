# Presenting Value

If discovery was done well, presenting is almost easy: you show the buyer
that what you learned about their situation connects to what your offer
does. This chapter covers the universal mechanics — benefits over features,
tailoring, proposals that get answered, and handling the price conversation
with confidence instead of apology.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Translate a feature into the benefit the buyer actually described wanting.
- Build a proposal around the criteria discovery uncovered, not around your
  product's structure.
- State a price without apologising, framed against the real alternative.
- Compute what any discount costs in margin, and the extra volume it
  demands.
- Offer something other than price when price is pushed.

## Features tell, benefits sell

A **feature** is a fact about the offer; a **benefit** is what that fact does
for *this buyer*. "This model has a dual pump" is a feature. "So if one pump
fails you keep running until the technician arrives — no stopped production"
is a benefit. The translation is the seller's job, not the buyer's, and the
bridge is always the phrase *"which means that…"* completed with something
the buyer told you they care about.

The discipline behind this is using discovery. Generic benefits ("saves you
money!") are wallpaper — every competitor says them. A benefit tied to the
buyer's own words ("you said month-end downtime is what hurts; here's what
happens at month-end with this in place") is proof you listened and proof
the offer fits. This is also why presenting before discovery fails: without
their criteria, you're guessing which benefits matter, and buyers hear the
guessing.

Two supporting universals:

- **Prioritise ruthlessly.** Present the two or three benefits that map to
  their top criteria, not the full catalogue. Every extra claim dilutes the
  ones that matter and invites objections you didn't need.
- **Prove what you claim.** A demonstration, a reference customer, a trial,
  a guarantee, a specification sheet — match the evidence to the claim and
  the stakes. Confidence without evidence is just volume.

## Tailor the proposal to the discovered need

Whether "the proposal" is a formal document, a quotation, or a sentence at a
counter, the same structure works because it mirrors how buyers decide:

1. **Their situation and need** — one or two sentences proving you
   understood. (In a written proposal, this section going first is what
   separates you from every template the buyer received that week.)
2. **The proposed answer** — what you're offering, framed against their
   criteria, benefits first, features as support.
3. **The evidence** — why they can believe it: proof points, references,
   guarantees.
4. **Price and terms** — clear, complete, no surprises buried in footnotes.
5. **The next step** — specific and easy to take: what happens if they say
   yes, by when, requiring what from them.

Written quotes deserve one extra discipline: **send them fast, and follow
them up**. A quote that arrives in a day says something about how you'll
deliver; one that arrives in two weeks says something too. And a quote
without a follow-up call is a message in a bottle — agree the follow-up
("I'll call Thursday once you've had a look") *when you send it*.

## Talking about price with confidence

Price is where sellers wobble, and buyers can hear the wobble. The
universals:

- **Never apologise for the price.** State it plainly, next to the value it
  buys. "It's ₦-whatever, and that includes…" beats a wince and a discount
  offered before anyone asked. If *you* don't act like it's worth it, why
  would they?
- **Sequence value before price.** A price with no established value is
  always too high. If the conversation reaches price before you've been
  able to connect the offer to their problem, that's discovery calling —
  loop back briefly, don't just answer and hope.
- **Compare against the real alternative.** Price feels different next to
  the cost of the problem continuing, the cheaper option's gaps, or the
  total cost over the product's life. Give the buyer an honest frame, not
  a naked number.
- **"Too expensive" is the start of a conversation, not the end.** It
  usually means one of: value not yet shown, comparing different scopes, a
  budget mismatch, or an opening negotiation move. Chapter 4 handles each —
  the point here is that the correct response is a question, not an
  immediate discount.

## Worked example: what a discount actually costs

A seller is asked for "just 10% off" on a ₦16,200 deal. It sounds like
giving away a tenth. It is not — the discount comes out of the *margin*, not
the price, and the margin is the smaller number.

Using the Finance course's customer: price ₦16,200, variable cost ₦8,000, so
margin ₦8,200.

| Discount | New price | New margin | Margin lost | Extra volume to stand still |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0% | ₦16,200 | ₦8,200 | — | — |
| 5% | ₦15,390 | ₦7,390 | −10% | **+11%** |
| 10% | ₦14,580 | ₦6,580 | −20% | **+25%** |
| 15% | ₦13,770 | ₦5,770 | −30% | **+42%** |

Ten percent off the price is **twenty percent off the profit**, and it takes
a quarter more volume just to end up where you started. A seller who
discounts 10% across their year has to sell 25% more to earn the same — which
is roughly a working day a week, given away at the moment of least
resistance.

The general shape, for any margin:

| Your gross margin | 5% off | 10% off | 15% off | 20% off |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 30% | +20% | +50% | +100% | +200% |
| 40% | +14% | +33% | +60% | +100% |
| 50% | +11% | +25% | +43% | +67% |

Read the bottom-left against the top-right. On a 30% margin, a 20% discount
requires **triple** the volume; on a 50% margin, the same discount needs
two-thirds more. The thinner your margin, the more violent the arithmetic —
which is exactly backwards from how discounting instinct works, since
thin-margin businesses feel the most price pressure.

None of this says never discount. It says know the number before you offer
it, and reach for the alternatives first: a smaller scope at the lower price,
a longer commitment, faster payment, a reference, a bundled extra that costs
you less than the discount would. Chapter 4 makes trading rather than giving
the central negotiation skill; this table is why it matters.

## Honesty scales

One more universal, easily lost in the craft of persuasion: **never
oversell**. The buyer who discovers after purchase that the claims were
inflated becomes your most expensive customer — refunds, complaints, bad
word of mouth — and in the age of reviews, one oversold sale can cost ten
future ones. Underclaim slightly and overdeliver: it converts buyers into
the referring, returning kind that Chapter 5 shows are the cheapest revenue
you will ever get.

## Do this at work

1. **Rewrite three features as benefits.** Take your product sheet and, for
   each feature, write "which means you can ___". If the blank will not
   fill, the feature does not belong in a proposal.
2. **Audit one proposal against discovery.** Find a recent proposal and mark
   every section that answers something the buyer actually said. The
   unmarked sections are yours, not theirs.
3. **Compute your own discount table.** Take your gross margin and work out
   the extra volume needed at 5, 10 and 15% off. Carry it with you — the
   arithmetic is unmemorable and the number is decisive.
4. **Price one deal against the alternative.** For a live opportunity, write
   what the problem costs the buyer per month if nothing changes. That
   figure, not your price list, is the frame.
5. **List five things you can trade instead of price.** Scope, timing,
   payment terms, commitment length, references, bundled extras. Have them
   ready before you are asked, because inventing them under pressure is how
   discounts get granted.
6. **Find one oversell.** Look for a claim your team makes that delivery
   struggles to meet. That gap is a future complaint, a refund, or a
   review — and it is cheaper to fix in the pitch than after the sale.

## Summary

- Translate every feature into a benefit with "which means that…",
  completed in the buyer's own terms — that's what discovery was for.
- Present few benefits, matched to their top criteria, each with evidence
  proportionate to the claim.
- Structure proposals as: their need, your answer, the evidence, clear
  price, easy next step. Send quotes fast and pre-agree the follow-up.
- State price plainly after value, framed against the real alternative;
  treat "too expensive" as information to explore, never a cue to discount.
- Never oversell — slightly underclaim and overdeliver; reviews and repeat
  business are the long game.
