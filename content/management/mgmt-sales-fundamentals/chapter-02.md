# Understanding the Customer

Discovery is where sales are actually won — everything after it merely
collects what discovery earned. This chapter covers the universal skills of
finding out what a buyer really needs: asking questions that open people up,
listening for what matters, qualifying honestly, and having the discipline to
walk away early from deals that will never close.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Open a conversation with questions that make the buyer describe their own
  problem before you describe your product.
- Listen for the decision behind the stated need — who decides, on what
  criteria, against what alternative.
- Score a prospect on need, means, authority and timing, and turn each weak
  answer into a next action.
- Put an hours figure on a dead deal, and convert it into the sales it
  displaced.
- Walk away in a way that leaves a referral behind.

## Ask before you tell

The single most common selling error, in every market, is pitching too soon.
The moment you start presenting, two things stop: you stop learning, and the
buyer starts defending. Reverse the order. Whether the conversation is five
minutes or five meetings, begin with genuine questions:

- **Open questions** get the buyer talking: "What are you using at the
  moment?" "What prompted you to look at this now?" "Walk me through how
  this happens today."
- **Follow the pain.** When they mention a frustration, go deeper before
  moving on: "How often does that happen?" "What does it cost you when it
  does?" "What have you tried already?" The difference between a mild
  annoyance and a burning problem *is the sale* — and only questions reveal
  which one you're looking at.
- **Confirm what you heard.** "So the real issue is less the price and more
  that deliveries are unpredictable — is that right?" Playback proves you
  listened, corrects your misreadings, and often makes the buyer sharpen
  their own understanding of what they want.

The "why now" question deserves special respect. Something moved this buyer
to talk to you *today* — a failure, a growth spurt, a contract ending, a
frustration finally boiling over. That trigger is the engine of the sale;
find it, because a buyer with no trigger has no urgency, whatever their
interest.

## Listen for the decision, not just the need

Buyers tell you the product they want; fewer volunteer how the decision will
actually be made. Both matter. Alongside the need, listen and probe for:

- **Criteria** — what will they judge options on? Price, reliability,
  speed, ease, appearance, service? (You'll present against these in
  Chapter 3, so collect them precisely.)
- **Alternatives** — what else are they considering, including the ever-
  present competitor: doing nothing?
- **The decision path** — who else weighs in (a spouse, a boss, a
  committee, a purchasing department), and what does the process look like?
- **History** — have they bought this kind of thing before? A burned buyer
  needs different reassurance than a first-timer.

## Qualification: not every prospect is a deal

Qualifying is deciding — honestly, on evidence — whether this prospect can
become a sale worth your effort. The universal checklist is four questions:

1. **Need** — do they have a problem we genuinely solve? Not "could they
   theoretically use it" but "does it hurt enough to act on?"
2. **Means** — can they realistically afford it? Establishing budget range
   early is a kindness to both sides, not rudeness.
3. **Authority** — can this person decide, or are you performing for
   someone who can't say yes? If a decider exists elsewhere, your real job
   is reaching them or equipping this person to sell inward on your behalf.
4. **Timing** — is there a reason to act now (the trigger again)? "Someday"
   interest produces "someday" revenue.

A prospect strong on all four is worth serious investment. Weak on one, you
know what to work on. Weak on most, be honest with yourself — hope is not a
qualification.

Used as a scorecard rather than a memory aid, the four questions tell you
what to *do* next rather than just how hopeful to feel:

| | Strong looks like | Weak looks like | Your next move if weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need** | They describe the problem before you name it, with a cost attached | "Interesting, we might use that someday" | Find the pain or park the deal — you cannot install urgency |
| **Means** | A budget range is discussed without discomfort | Price avoided, or a figure far below your floor | Establish the range now; a proposal into an unknown budget is a lottery ticket |
| **Authority** | You are speaking to whoever signs, or know exactly who does | "I'll have to run it past some people" — unnamed | Reach the decider, or equip your contact to sell inward with the material they need |
| **Timing** | A dated trigger: contract ending, expansion, failure, regulation | "Sometime this year" | Ask what would have to happen for this to become urgent, and diarise that |

Weak on one is a work item. Weak on three is not a prospect, however pleasant
the conversation — and pleasantness is precisely what makes these deals
survive in a pipeline long after they should have left it.

## Worked example: what a dead deal actually costs

Sellers resist walking away because the cost of continuing is invisible. Make
it visible by tracking one deal that never closes:

| Activity | Hours |
| --- | ---: |
| Discovery meeting | 2.0 |
| Travel to and from site | 3.0 |
| Writing and revising the proposal | 4.0 |
| Three follow-up calls | 1.5 |
| Internal pricing approval | 1.0 |
| **Total per dead deal** | **11.5** |

Now suppose a seller carried **eight** of these through a quarter — deals
that were never going to close, kept alive by politeness and hope. That is
**92 hours**, or eleven and a half working days: most of a month, spent
producing nothing.

Convert it back into pipeline. At roughly two hours per new conversation,
those 92 hours would have started **46 conversations** — and at Chapter 1's
ratio of 17 conversations per sale, that is **about 2.7 additional sales**.

So the eight dead deals did not cost nothing. They cost nearly three real
sales, taken quietly from the seller's own year. That is the number to hold
in mind the next time a prospect is pleasant, responsive, and never going to
buy.

## The discipline of walking away

Sellers hate abandoning prospects: effort is invested, the person is
pleasant, and "you never know." But every hour spent on a deal that cannot
close is an hour taken from one that could — the opportunity cost is
invisible but real, and it is the biggest hidden tax on most sellers' time.
Recognise the signatures of a dead deal honestly:

- No trigger and no timeline, only politeness ("send me some information").
- Repeatedly postponed decisions with new reasons each time.
- No access to the real decision-maker after genuine attempts.
- A need your offer honestly doesn't fit.

Walk away *well*: courteously, leaving the door open, sometimes pointing
them somewhere that fits better. A graceful exit costs one deal that was
already lost and often earns a referral or a return visit when circumstances
change. And when the mismatch is your product's fit, saying so plainly —
"honestly, what you need is X, and we don't do X well" — builds more
long-term revenue than any forced sale ever did.

## Summary

- Ask before you tell: open questions, follow the pain, play back what you
  heard, and always find the trigger — the "why now".
- Discover the decision, not just the need: criteria, alternatives, who
  decides, and the buyer's history.
- Qualify on need, means, authority, and timing — evidence, not hope.
- Drop dead deals early and gracefully; eight dead deals in a quarter is
  ~92 hours, which at ordinary conversion is nearly three real sales taken
  from your own year. The hours you recover are where your real revenue
  comes from, and honest "this isn't for you" builds the
  reputation that fills next year's pipeline.

## Do this at work

1. **Score your live pipeline.** Take every open deal and mark it strong or
   weak on need, means, authority and timing. Deals weak on three are not
   a pipeline; they are a list of people you like.
2. **Find the unnamed decider.** For each deal where authority is weak,
   write the name of whoever actually signs. If you cannot, that is the
   next call, and it is more valuable than another follow-up with your
   contact.
3. **Time one dead deal.** Reconstruct the hours a recently lost deal
   consumed — meetings, travel, proposal, follow-ups, approvals. Most
   sellers underestimate this by half.
4. **Convert those hours to sales.** Divide by your hours per conversation,
   then by your conversations per sale from Chapter 1. That is what the
   deal cost you, and it is the honest argument for qualifying harder.
5. **Retire three deals this week.** Pick the three oldest deals with no
   trigger and no timeline. Close them out courteously, point them
   elsewhere if you can, and note how much calendar returns.
6. **Ask one buyer what would make it urgent.** For a "someday" prospect,
   ask directly what would have to happen for this to become a priority.
   Then diarise that, and stop spending weekly effort in the meantime.
