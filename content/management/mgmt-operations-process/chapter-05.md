# Running the Operation Day to Day

Improvement projects get attention; the daily discipline that keeps an
operation stable does not, and it is what decides whether last quarter's
improvements are still there. This chapter is about the routine: the short
review that catches problems in hours, the small number of numbers worth
watching, and the escalation that reaches someone before the customer does.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Run a daily operational review that takes minutes and changes something.
- Choose the handful of numbers an operation should watch daily.
- Set escalation triggers that fire before a customer notices.
- Plan for the demand you can predict and absorb the demand you cannot.
- Keep improvement alive without a project.

## The daily review

Short, standing, same time, same questions. Fifteen minutes is enough for most
operations:

1. **What happened yesterday that we should know about?** Exceptions only —
   not a recital of normal work.
2. **What is at risk today?** Jobs, deliveries, coverage, equipment.
3. **What is blocked, and who is unblocking it?** A name and a time.
4. **Anything from yesterday's list still open?**

Two rules keep it from becoming the thing everyone dreads. **It is not a status
parade** — if each person is reciting what a shared board already shows, you
have rebuilt the meeting the Communication course tells you to delete. And
**every problem raised ends in an owner and a time**, or the review teaches
people that raising things achieves nothing.

The value is speed. A problem found in the morning has a day to be fixed; the
same problem found in a weekly review has already spent four days becoming
customer-visible.

## The numbers to watch daily

Chapter 2 of the Data course argues for three to six per team, drawn from
different families so they discipline each other. For an operation, the
practical set is:

| Family | Typical daily number |
| --- | --- |
| Output | Jobs completed against plan |
| Quality | First-visit completion, or rework raised today |
| Speed | Oldest open job, and the size of the queue at the constraint |
| Cost | Overtime hours, or cost per job this week |
| Satisfaction | Complaints raised today |

**The queue at the constraint is the operational number most teams do not
watch and should.** It moves before anything else does — before output falls,
before dates slip, before a customer complains — which makes it the earliest
warning available.

And watch them as lines against a normal range, not as bare figures. Chapter 2
of this course, and the Data course's baseline work, are the same instruction:
react to pattern, not to yesterday's wobble.

## Escalation that arrives before the customer

The failure mode is universal: a job quietly stalls, nobody notices, and the
first person to raise it is the customer. The fix is a set of **triggers agreed
in advance** — the tripwires from the Decision Making course, applied to
operations.

- **Age-based.** Any job older than *n* days is raised automatically,
  regardless of whether anyone is worried about it. The age threshold catches
  what judgement misses, because nobody is watching a job that is merely
  sitting there.
- **Event-based.** A failed second visit, a missed delivery, a part that
  cannot be sourced, a job returned twice — each raises immediately without
  waiting for a review.
- **Named owner and named recipient.** "Escalate to operations" is not an
  escalation route; a person is.
- **A response commitment.** An escalation that disappears into someone's
  inbox teaches the frontline to stop escalating, and after that you learn
  about problems from customers permanently.

The test of an escalation system is not how many escalations it produces. It
is **what share of your customer complaints arrived about something you already
knew about.** If most complaints are news to you, the triggers are not firing.

## Demand: the part you can predict

Most operations treat all demand as unpredictable and are surprised in the same
way every year. Some of it is genuinely predictable and can simply be planned
for:

- **Seasonal and calendar patterns** — month-end, paydays, holidays, rainy
  season, school terms.
- **Consequences of your own actions** — a marketing push, a price change, a
  new product, a migration.
- **Known events** — a contract starting, a site opening, a large customer
  onboarding.

Plan capacity against those explicitly, and keep the buffer for what is
genuinely unforeseeable — which is exactly the Team Leadership capacity point:
committing 100% of capacity to planned work is a decision to miss deadlines,
because the unplanned share is historically reliable even when its content is
not.

## Keeping improvement alive without a project

Large improvement projects are episodic and stop when the project does. The
durable version is smaller and continuous:

- **One improvement at a time, owned by the team**, chosen from what annoys
  them most — which is usually also a real inefficiency, because friction and
  waste tend to sit in the same place.
- **A visible list of irritations** anyone can add to. Most entries are small
  and cheap, and the act of collecting them changes how a team looks at its
  own work.
- **Time protected for it**, however little. An hour a fortnight that
  genuinely happens beats a quarterly workshop that gets cancelled.
- **Close the loop visibly.** The single strongest driver of further
  suggestions is people seeing one of theirs implemented and named.

And check the old improvements occasionally. Chapter 4's 30- and 90-day checks
are the mechanism; without them you will re-solve the same problem in two
years and not recognise it.

## Do this at work

1. **Start a fifteen-minute daily review** with four fixed questions, and end
   every problem with an owner and a time.
2. **Pick five daily numbers** across output, quality, speed, cost and
   satisfaction. Fewer than you want; more than one.
3. **Add the constraint's queue** to the board. It is the earliest warning
   most operations are not watching.
4. **Set one age-based trigger** — any job older than *n* days raised
   automatically — and name the person it reaches.
5. **Check your last twenty complaints.** What share were about something you
   already knew? That is your escalation system's real score.
6. **List your predictable demand** for the next quarter and plan capacity
   against it, keeping the buffer for the genuinely unforeseeable.
7. **Start the irritations list** and implement one item this month, visibly.

## Summary

- A short daily review with fixed questions catches problems in hours rather
  than days — provided it is not a status parade and every problem ends with
  an owner and a time.
- Watch a handful of numbers across output, quality, speed, cost and
  satisfaction, read as lines against a normal range rather than as bare
  figures.
- The queue at the constraint moves before anything else and is the earliest
  warning most operations ignore.
- Escalation triggers must be agreed in advance, age-based as well as
  event-based, addressed to a person, and answered — and their real score is
  the share of complaints that were news to you.
- Plan for the predictable share of demand and keep the buffer for the rest;
  committing all capacity to planned work is a decision to miss dates.
- Continuous small improvement owned by the team outlasts projects — with
  protected time, a visible irritations list, and loops closed where people
  can see them.
