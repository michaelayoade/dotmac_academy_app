# Seeing the Process

Every organisation runs on processes, and most of them were never designed.
They accumulated: a step added after an incident, a check inserted for a
customer who complained once, an approval nobody can trace. This chapter is
about making a process visible enough to manage — which is almost always the
first improvement, because a process nobody has drawn is a process nobody can
argue about accurately.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Map a process end to end, as it actually runs rather than as described.
- Separate value-adding steps from the rest without contempt for either.
- Find the waiting time that dominates most end-to-end durations.
- Identify handoffs and the queues that form at them.
- Say what a process is *for*, in the customer's terms.

## Start with what it is for

Before drawing anything, answer one question: **what does this process produce,
for whom, and what would "good" look like to them?** An installation process
exists to get a working service to a customer, correctly, on the promised day,
without them chasing. A month-end process exists to produce numbers people can
act on, in time to act on them.

That sentence matters because process improvement without it optimises the
wrong thing. A team can make a broken process wonderfully efficient, and the
faster production of something nobody needed is the most expensive outcome
available.

## Map it as it runs

Two rules make a process map useful rather than decorative:

- **Walk it, do not describe it.** Follow one real job from beginning to end.
  Talk to the people at each step. Watch what actually happens, including the
  workaround everyone uses and nobody mentions.
- **Map the current state before the ideal one.** The temptation is to draw
  how it *should* work. That map is a proposal, not a diagnosis, and it hides
  precisely the steps you need to see.

Record for each step: what happens, who does it, how long the work takes, how
long the job *waits* before that step, and what can send it backwards.

That last one — the rework loop — is where most processes leak, and it is the
one people forget to draw because it feels like an exception. If it happens to
one job in eight, it is not an exception; it is a step.

## Value, necessary, and waste

Sort the steps honestly into three groups:

| | What it is | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Value-adding** | Changes the thing in a way the customer would pay for | Would they notice if it stopped? |
| **Necessary but not value-adding** | Compliance, safety, records, controls | Required by law, safety, or a real risk — keep, but make it cheap |
| **Waste** | Everything else | Who would notice if this stopped? |

Two cautions, because this framing is often used badly.

**The middle column is not waste.** Safety checks, regulatory records and
financial controls do not add customer value and must not be removed for that
reason — the Vendor & Procurement and Health & Safety courses both explain
what removing them costs. The goal is to make them efficient, not absent.

**A step you cannot explain is not automatically waste.** It may be a control
whose original incident nobody remembers. Ask why it exists before removing
it — and if genuinely nobody can say, that is worth knowing before you find
out the hard way.

## Waiting is most of the duration

Here is the finding that surprises managers most consistently. Take an
end-to-end process — enquiry to installation, request to delivery, complaint
to resolution — and add up the time when someone is actually *working* on it,
then compare that to the total elapsed time.

The working time is usually a small fraction. Most of the duration is the job
sitting in a queue: waiting for an approval, waiting for a part, waiting for
someone to notice it arrived, waiting because it is Thursday and that team
processes on Mondays.

The consequence is practical: **if you want a process to be faster, attack the
waiting, not the working.** Asking people to work faster targets the small
number and is the option that damages quality. Removing a queue targets the
large one and usually costs nothing but a decision.

## Handoffs are where things stop

Every point where work passes between people or teams is a point where it can
wait, be dropped, or lose information. The signatures of a bad handoff will be
familiar from the Team Leadership course: no fixed format, no explicit
acceptance, and no single owner across the boundary.

Count them. A process with nine handoffs has nine queues and nine chances to
drop something, and the count alone often makes the case for redesign more
effectively than any analysis of the steps themselves.

## Do this at work

1. **Write the purpose sentence.** What this process produces, for whom, to
   what standard. If the team would write different sentences, that is your
   first finding.
2. **Walk one real job end to end.** Not a description, an actual job. Note
   what you were told versus what you saw.
3. **Record waiting separately from working** at every step. The ratio is
   usually the most surprising number in the exercise.
4. **Draw the rework loops.** Anything that sends a job backwards. If it
   happens to one job in eight, draw it as a step, not an exception.
5. **Count the handoffs.** Each is a queue and a chance to drop something.
6. **Sort steps into value, necessary and waste** — and before removing
   anything in the third column, ask why it exists.
7. **Find one queue you can delete with a decision** rather than with effort.
   Most processes have one.

## Summary

- Start with what the process is for, in the customer's terms; efficiency
  without that optimises the wrong thing.
- Map the current state by walking a real job, including the workaround
  everyone uses and nobody mentions. An ideal-state map is a proposal, not a
  diagnosis.
- Draw the rework loops — if it happens to one job in eight it is a step, not
  an exception.
- Sort into value-adding, necessary, and waste. The necessary column is not
  waste, and an unexplained step may be a control whose incident nobody
  remembers.
- Waiting, not working, is most of the elapsed time. Attack the queues rather
  than asking people to work faster.
- Every handoff is a queue and a chance to drop something. Counting them
  often makes the case on its own.
