# Standard Work & Variation

Two teams can produce the same average and be in completely different states —
one predictable, one a lottery. Averages hide that, and managing to an average
is how organisations end up with a good headline number and customers who
cannot rely on them. This chapter is about variation: why it matters more than
the mean, and what a standard method does about it.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Read spread as well as average, and say which one the customer feels.
- Explain what standard work is and what it is not.
- Write a standard that improves rather than freezes a process.
- Distinguish common-cause from special-cause variation.
- Say why reacting to every fluctuation makes a process worse.

## Worked example: the same mean, two different operations

Two crews, jobs completed per week over eight weeks:

| | W1 | W2 | W3 | W4 | W5 | W6 | W7 | W8 | Mean | Range |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Crew A** | 42 | 45 | 44 | 43 | 46 | 44 | 45 | 43 | **44.0** | **4** |
| **Crew B** | 28 | 61 | 39 | 52 | 35 | 58 | 41 | 49 | **45.4** | **33** |

On a monthly report Crew B looks marginally better. In practice they are not
comparable operations at all.

**Crew A can be planned around.** Promise 42 and you will keep the promise.
Materials, scheduling and staffing can all be set against a number that holds.

**Crew B cannot.** To promise safely you must promise 28 — their worst week —
which throws away a third of their apparent capacity. Promise their average
and you will miss roughly half the time. So the *usable* capacity of the
higher-performing crew is lower, and the difference is caused entirely by
variation.

There is a second cost that does not appear in either row. Crew B's bad weeks
generate chasing, rescheduling, apologies and complaint handling — work that
exists only because the output was unpredictable, and that lands on people who
were not involved.

**The customer never experiences your average.** They experience the week they
happened to arrive in. This is the same argument the Data course makes about
distributions and tails, applied to operations.

## Standard work, and what it is not

Standard work is the current best-known way to do a task: the sequence, the
timing, the tools, the checks. Its purpose is not conformity for its own sake
— it is that **you cannot improve a process that is done differently every
time.** Without a baseline, a change cannot be evaluated, because you cannot
tell whether the result came from the change or from which person happened to
do it.

What it is not:

- **Not a script that removes judgement.** It covers the routine path and
  says explicitly what to do when the situation departs from it — usually
  *stop and ask*, which is the Health & Safety course's point about methods
  surviving bad conditions.
- **Not permanent.** A standard is the current best-known method, and it
  should change whenever someone finds better. A standard nobody has revised
  in three years is either perfect or ignored.
- **Not written by people who do not do the work.** Standards written at a
  desk get quietly bypassed, and then you have variation *plus* a document
  that lies.
- **Not a substitute for capability.** A precise standard handed to someone
  untrained produces precisely wrong work.

The strongest argument for it is the one teams accept fastest: **standard work
is what makes a new person useful in weeks rather than months**, and what
stops the whole operation depending on the two people who know how things
really work.

## Common cause and special cause

Not all variation is the same, and the distinction decides your response.

| | Common cause | Special cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | The normal noise of the process itself | A specific, identifiable event |
| Examples | Small differences in jobs, weather, minor delays | A vehicle broke down; a supplier missed a delivery; a new starter's first week |
| Right response | Change the process | Investigate the instance |
| Wrong response | Investigating each fluctuation | Assuming it is normal |

Getting this backwards is expensive in both directions, and one of them is
worse.

**Reacting to common-cause variation actively harms the process.** The Friday
interrogation about a dip that reverses on Monday teaches the team to explain
randomness, produces false causes that then get believed, and generates
adjustments that add variation of their own. The Data course names this as
reacting to noise; in an operation it has a physical cost, because each
"correction" changes a process that was working.

**Treating special cause as normal** wastes the free information — the vehicle
that broke down was a maintenance finding, not bad luck.

The practical test is the one from the Data course's baseline work: know your
process's normal range, mark it, and react only to points outside it or to a
run of points on one side.

## Making a standard that improves

Four habits keep standard work alive rather than laminated:

1. **Write it with the people who do it.** They know the real sequence, and
   they will follow a document they helped write.
2. **Keep it where the work is** — on the machine, in the van, in the hand.
3. **Make improving it easy and visible.** If changing the standard requires
   three approvals, people will improve the work and not the document, and
   within a year the two will have nothing to do with each other.
4. **When someone finds a better way, change the standard and tell everyone.**
   That single act is what converts one person's improvement into the
   organisation's — and it is the difference between a team that gets better
   and a team where a few individuals do.

## Do this at work

1. **Plot eight weeks of one measure** and look at the range, not just the
   mean. The spread is what your customers and your planners experience.
2. **Ask what you could safely promise** based on the worst recent week. The
   gap between that and the average is capacity variation is costing you.
3. **Find your rescheduling work.** Chasing, apologising, re-booking — it
   exists only because output was unpredictable, and it lands on people who
   were not involved.
4. **Watch two people do the same task.** The differences are either
   improvements one of them found, or variation nobody intended. Both are
   worth acting on.
5. **Write one standard with the crew**, on one page, kept where the work is.
6. **Check the last three "corrections" you made** to a process. Were they
   responses to common-cause noise? If so, they added variation.
7. **Change one standard because someone found better** — and tell everyone
   you did it. That is the habit that makes standards live.

## Summary

- Two operations with the same mean can be entirely different: the customer
  experiences the spread, never the average.
- Variation destroys usable capacity — a crew you can only safely promise at
  its worst week has less effective capacity than a steadier crew with a
  lower mean.
- Unpredictability generates its own second workload: chasing, rescheduling
  and apologies that land on people who were not involved.
- Standard work exists because you cannot improve a process done differently
  every time — and it makes a new person useful in weeks.
- It is not a script, not permanent, not written at a desk, and not a
  substitute for capability.
- Change the process for common-cause variation and investigate the instance
  for special cause. Reacting to noise adds variation of its own.
