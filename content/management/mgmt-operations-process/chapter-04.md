# Changing a Process Without Breaking It

Knowing what to improve is the easy half. The hard half is changing a live
process that people depend on, without introducing a worse problem than the
one you fixed, and without the change quietly reverting three weeks later.
This chapter is about making improvement stick.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Choose which improvement to make first, on evidence rather than enthusiasm.
- Test a change small before rolling it out.
- Predict what a change will do, so you can tell whether it worked.
- Bring the people who run the process into the change.
- Recognise a change that reverted, and know why it did.

## Choose on evidence, not on irritation

Most improvement effort goes to whatever annoyed a senior person most
recently. Better filters:

- **Attack the constraint first.** Chapter 3's rule: improvements elsewhere
  produce no additional output. This alone redirects most misplaced effort.
- **Rank by frequency × cost**, the Pareto check from the Decision Making
  course. A small number of causes usually accounts for most of the pain, and
  the fixing budget belongs there.
- **Prefer removing steps to optimising them.** A step deleted needs no
  training, no monitoring, and cannot decay. A step made 20% faster needs all
  three.
- **Prefer changes that eliminate a queue** over changes that speed up work,
  because Chapter 1 showed the queue is where the duration lives.

And apply the Finance course's honesty: **count the cost of the change**,
including the disruption, the retraining and the period where the process runs
worse while people learn. A change worth doing is still worth costing.

## Predict before you change

The single most useful discipline, and the one most often skipped: **write down
what you expect to happen, and how you will know.**

> "We expect first-visit completion to rise from 88% to about 93% within six
> weeks. We will know from the weekly first-visit rate. If it has not moved by
> the end of week six, the theory was wrong and we stop."

Three things that sentence buys. It converts an opinion into a testable claim.
It stops the change being declared a success on the basis that it was
implemented. And it defines failure in advance, which is what makes stopping
possible — the tripwire from the Decision Making course, in an operational
setting.

Without a prediction, every change looks successful, because the people who
made it are the people evaluating it and something always improves somewhere.

## Pilot small

Change one crew, one site, one shift, one product line — for a defined period,
with the measure agreed beforehand.

- **You find the unforeseen problems cheaply.** Every process change has some;
  the question is only whether you meet them on one crew or on twelve.
- **You get a genuine comparison.** The unchanged part of the operation is
  your control group, which is worth more than a before-and-after that
  coincided with a seasonal change.
- **The pilot crew becomes the teacher.** People adopt a change from peers who
  have run it far more readily than from a manager who has read about it.

The trap to avoid: **piloting with your best crew and generalising the
result.** Everything works with your strongest people. If the change only
survives with them, it is not a process change, it is a staffing arrangement.

## Involve the people who run it

The reason this matters is practical rather than sentimental. The people doing
the work know which step the map got wrong, which control is routinely
bypassed and why, and which of your three proposed improvements will collapse
in the rain. Skipping them buys a change that fails in the first week for a
reason someone could have told you in a sentence.

The Team Leadership course's consulted-means-heard rule applies exactly: ask
genuinely, act on at least some of it visibly, and explain the decisions that
went the other way. A team that has been consulted and overruled with a reason
will run your change. A team consulted decoratively will run it until you stop
watching.

## Standardise, or it reverts

A change that is not written into the standard work of Chapter 2 will revert.
Not through resistance — through drift. New people are trained on the old
method by someone who never heard about the change. The busy week arrives and
the old habit is faster under pressure. The one person who understood why it
changed moves teams.

So the last step of every improvement is unglamorous and non-optional:

1. **Update the standard**, and the checklists, templates and forms that
   embody it.
2. **Retrain**, including the people who were not there and the ones on
   nights.
3. **Update the training material for new starters**, which is the step most
   often forgotten and the one that guarantees reversion within a year.
4. **Check it is still running at 30 and 90 days.** Improvements decay
   quietly, and the check is what tells you whether you improved a process or
   held one up for a fortnight.

## When a change makes things worse

It happens, and the response decides whether anyone proposes a change again.

- **Revert quickly and without drama.** A pilot that fails is the pilot
  working — that was the point of running it small.
- **Say what you learned**, publicly. A theory disproved is information, and
  the team is watching how you treat a failed idea of your own.
- **Do not quietly keep it** because reverting is embarrassing. That is sunk
  cost from the Decision Making course, with the added cost that everyone can
  see it.

The organisations that improve fastest are not the ones whose changes always
work. They are the ones that run more small changes, discover the failures
cheaply, and are honest enough to stop them.

## Do this at work

1. **Check what you are about to improve is the constraint.** If it is not,
   the output will not move and the effort is spent.
2. **Rank three candidate improvements by frequency × cost.** The answer is
   often not the one being discussed loudest.
3. **Look for a step to delete** before looking for one to speed up.
4. **Write the prediction before the change** — the measure, the expected
   size, the date, and what result means stop.
5. **Pilot on one crew or site**, and not your best one.
6. **Ask the people who run it what will break.** Then act visibly on at
   least one thing they say.
7. **Update the standard, the checklist and the new-starter material** — then
   diarise a check at 30 and 90 days.

## Summary

- Choose improvements on evidence: attack the constraint, rank by frequency ×
  cost, prefer deleting steps and removing queues, and cost the change
  honestly including its disruption.
- Write the prediction first — the measure, the expected movement, the date,
  and what result means stop. Without it every change looks successful.
- Pilot small, on a defined scope, with a control — and not with your best
  crew, because everything works with your strongest people.
- Involve the people who run the process for practical reasons: they know
  what will break, and consultation is what makes the change survive your
  attention moving elsewhere.
- Changes revert through drift, not resistance. Update the standard, the
  checklists and the new-starter training, then check at 30 and 90 days.
- Revert failures quickly and say what you learned; the fastest-improving
  organisations run more small changes and stop the bad ones honestly.
