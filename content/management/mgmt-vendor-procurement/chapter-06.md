# Procurement Ethics & Controls

Procurement is where an organisation's money meets people who want it. That
makes it the function most exposed to conflict of interest, quiet favouritism,
and outright fraud — not because the people in it are worse, but because the
opportunity is concentrated there and the damage is easy to hide inside
ordinary paperwork. This chapter covers the standards a buyer holds
themselves to, the controls that make those standards enforceable, and the
patterns that indicate something has gone wrong.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Recognise a conflict of interest in yourself and declare it before it
  decides something.
- Apply a defensible line on gifts and hospitality.
- Say why facilitation payments are a different category from ordinary
  commercial pressure.
- Name the four separations of duty that make procurement fraud difficult.
- Read the standard red-flag patterns in your own purchase records.

## Why this chapter is not about bad people

The instinctive framing of procurement ethics is character: hire honest
people, and the problem is solved. It is a comforting model and a poor one,
for two reasons.

First, most procurement damage is not theft. It is **drift** — the supplier
who became a friend and stopped being compared, the specification quietly
written around one vendor's product, the renewal nobody questioned because
the relationship is pleasant. Nobody in that chain did anything they would
describe as wrong, and the organisation still overpays for years.

Second, controls exist to protect the honest as much as to catch the
dishonest. A buyer who selects a supplier their cousin works for, with no
declaration and no competing quotes, may have chosen entirely on merit — and
has no way to prove it. The control is not an accusation. **It is the
mechanism by which an honest decision remains visibly honest to someone
looking at it two years later, in a worse mood, with an auditor.**

Hold that framing throughout. "We trust our people" is not an alternative to
controls; it is what controls make possible.

## Conflicts of interest

A conflict of interest exists when you have an interest that could
reasonably be seen to influence a decision you are making for the
organisation. Note the standard: **could reasonably be seen to**, not *did*.
The test is not whether you would have decided differently — you are the
worst-placed person to judge that — but whether a reasonable outsider,
knowing the facts, would wonder.

The common shapes:

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Personal relationship | A supplier owned or staffed by family, a friend, a partner |
| Financial interest | You hold a share in the supplier, or are owed money by them |
| Outside employment | You or a household member work for, or consult to, the bidder |
| Future interest | The supplier has hinted at a job, or you are negotiating one |
| Reciprocal | The supplier buys from your side business, or employs someone at your request |

The obligation is not to have no relationships — in a market the size of most
Nigerian cities, a buyer with no connections to any supplier is a buyer who
has just arrived. The obligation is threefold: **declare it in writing,
before the decision; step out of the decision itself; and let someone without
the interest make the call.** A declared conflict handled that way is a
non-event. The same conflict discovered later is a scandal, regardless of
whether the decision was sound.

Write the declaration even when you are confident nothing turns on it.
Declarations cost minutes and are the only thing that distinguishes you from
the person who concealed the same fact.

## Gifts and hospitality: where the line goes

Suppliers give things. Most of it is ordinary courtesy; some of it is
purchase of influence, and the two look identical at the moment of offering.
The workable test has three questions:

1. **Would you be comfortable if it were published** — in an internal list,
   or in a newspaper? Discomfort is information.
2. **Is it timed near a decision?** A calendar in December is one thing; the
   same gift arriving during a tender is another entirely.
3. **Is it proportionate and occasional?** A working lunch is hospitality. A
   weekend away is not, and neither is a modest gift that arrives every month.

Most organisations set a value threshold, a register, and a rule that
anything above the threshold is declined or declared and surrendered. If
yours has one, follow it exactly; if it does not, the safe personal standard
is: **accept nothing you would not tell your manager about in passing, and
register anything you accept.**

Cash, or anything convertible to cash, is never acceptable at any value. That
is not a threshold judgement — it is a different category, and it is where
hospitality stops and the next section begins.

## Bribery and facilitation payments

A bribe is anything of value given to improperly influence a decision. Two
things about it are worth stating plainly, because both are routinely
misunderstood.

**"Facilitation payments" are bribes.** The small payment to make a routine
approval move, the something-for-the-boys to release goods already cleared —
these feel different because the outcome was owed to you anyway, and because
refusing has an immediate cost while paying has a deferred one. They are
nonetheless illegal under Nigerian law and under the extraterritorial regimes
(UK Bribery Act, US FCPA) that reach any organisation with international
customers, banking, or investors. An organisation that pays them routinely is
building a liability that arrives all at once.

**Your supplier's conduct can become your liability.** Engaging an agent or
intermediary who pays on your behalf is not distance; in most regimes it is
exposure. That is why supplier due diligence and anti-bribery clauses exist
in the contracts of Chapter 3, and why an intermediary whose commission is
unexplained is a genuine warning sign rather than a commercial detail.

The practical position for a manager: **you are never authorised to pay a
bribe, and you are never expected to.** If you are being extorted — a
shipment held, a permit stalled, a threat made — that is an escalation, not a
decision. Escalate it the same day, in writing, and let the organisation take
the delay. A manager who quietly paid to keep the schedule has protected the
schedule and transferred the risk to themselves.

## The four separations that make fraud hard

Most procurement fraud requires one person to control more than one step. The
controls are therefore mostly about who may do what:

| Separate | So that |
| --- | --- |
| **Requesting** from **approving** | Nobody authorises their own spending |
| **Approving** from **purchasing** | The approved thing is what gets ordered |
| **Purchasing** from **receiving** | The person who ordered cannot also confirm it arrived |
| **Receiving** from **paying** | Payment follows verified receipt, not assertion |

Alongside those, three supporting controls do most of the remaining work:

- **The supplier master file is controlled.** Adding a new supplier, or
  changing an existing supplier's bank details, is the single highest-risk
  action in the whole process — and it is where the most common modern fraud
  lands. Changes require verification by a separate person, through contact
  details you already held, never through the details in the requesting
  message.
- **Three-way matching.** Purchase order, delivery note, invoice — compared
  before payment. Errors and inventions both fail this check, and it is
  mostly mechanical.
- **Thresholds with real competition.** Above a stated value, a minimum
  number of genuine quotes; above a higher one, a formal process. Thresholds
  with no enforcement teach everyone the number below which nothing is
  checked.

In a small business, one person genuinely does several of these jobs. The
answer is not to pretend otherwise but to **break the chain where it is
cheapest**: the owner reviews bank-detail changes and the monthly supplier
list, someone other than the buyer opens the deliveries, and payments above a
threshold need a second signature. Three cheap separations remove most of the
opportunity.

## Worked example: reading the red flags

None of the patterns below proves anything on its own. Each is a reason to
look, and the reason they are worth memorising is that every one of them is
visible in records you already have.

| Pattern in the records | What it can indicate |
| --- | --- |
| One supplier wins repeatedly with no competing quotes | Specification written around them, or a relationship nobody declared |
| Purchases sit just under an approval threshold | Splitting: one purchase divided to avoid the gate above it |
| A supplier's bank details changed shortly before a payment | Mandate fraud, usually by email impersonation |
| Invoices with sequential numbers from a supplier who serves many clients | You may be their only customer, which raises what they are |
| Deliveries confirmed by the person who ordered them | The separation has collapsed |
| Prices drift up between quote and invoice, unchallenged | Nobody is checking, and the supplier has learned it |
| A supplier address that matches an employee's | Undeclared interest |
| Unusually round or repeated amounts | Fabricated invoices |

Take **splitting**, because it is the one honest managers do accidentally. A
₦400,000 approval threshold, and a manager who needs ₦620,000 of equipment
raises two orders of ₦310,000 a week apart. Sometimes that is fraud. Far more
often it is a competent person routing around a gate they experience as an
obstacle — and the *effect* is identical: a purchase that should have had a
second pair of eyes did not get them.

Which is why the finding is a conversation before it is an accusation. If
splitting is common in your records, you have learned two things: that the
threshold is being avoided, and quite possibly that the approval process
above it is slow enough to be worth avoiding. **Fix both.** A control that
honest people route around is a control that has already failed, and
tightening it without fixing the delay simply moves the workaround somewhere
less visible.

## When you find something

Finding a possible problem is uncomfortable, and the instinct — to have a
quiet word, or to wait and see — is the one that causes the most damage.

- **Do not investigate it yourself.** You will alert the person, and you may
  destroy the record trail that would have proved it either way.
- **Preserve rather than confront.** Keep the documents and the dates. Do not
  challenge the supplier or the colleague.
- **Report it through the stated route** — your manager, or the escalation
  path if your manager is involved. Organisations that lack a route are
  telling you something, and the answer is to create one before it is needed.
- **Report suspicion, not certainty.** You are not required to have proved
  anything. The standard is a reasonable concern held in good faith, and a
  concern raised honestly and disproved costs nothing but a conversation.

Whistleblower protections exist in Nigerian public-sector policy and in most
organisations' own codes; the practical protection is a written report to a
named person, kept.

## Do this at work

1. **Write one declaration.** If any supplier you deal with has a connection
   to you — family, friendship, a past employer, an approach about a job —
   declare it in writing this week, even if nothing turns on it. It costs
   minutes and it is the only thing separating you from someone who hid it.
2. **Check for splitting.** Sort last quarter's purchases by value and look
   just below each approval threshold. If there is a cluster, ask why the
   gate is being avoided before assuming who is avoiding it.
3. **Test the bank-detail control.** Ask how a supplier's account number gets
   changed in your systems, and who verifies it. If the answer is "we update
   what the email says", you have found the highest-risk gap in the process.
4. **Find a sole supplier nobody re-tested.** One that has won repeatedly
   without competition. Get two genuine quotes — not to replace them, but so
   the file shows they were still the right choice.
5. **Map your own separations.** Requesting, approving, purchasing,
   receiving, paying. Where two sit with one person, name the cheapest break.
6. **Check the gift register exists.** If your organisation has one, read
   what was declared last year. If it does not, start one with your own
   entries — a register with three honest entries is worth more than a policy
   with none.
7. **Find the route.** Know today who you would tell about a suspected
   irregularity, and what you would do if that person were the subject.

## Summary

- Controls protect the honest as much as they catch the dishonest: they are
  how a good decision stays visibly good to someone reviewing it later.
- Most procurement damage is drift, not theft — the unexamined relationship,
  the specification written around one product, the unquestioned renewal.
- Declare conflicts in writing before the decision, step out of it, and let
  someone uninterested decide. The test is what a reasonable outsider would
  wonder, not what you know about your own motives.
- Gifts: publishable, not near a decision, proportionate and occasional.
  Cash is a different category at any value.
- Facilitation payments are bribes, your intermediary's conduct can be your
  liability, and extortion is an escalation rather than a decision.
- Separate requesting, approving, purchasing, receiving and paying; control
  the supplier master file and bank-detail changes above all else.
- Red flags are reasons to look, not proof. Splitting is usually a competent
  person routing around a slow gate — fix the gate as well as the splitting.
- On finding something: preserve, do not investigate, report suspicion in
  good faith through a named route.
