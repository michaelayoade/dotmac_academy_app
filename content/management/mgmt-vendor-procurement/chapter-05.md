# Supplier Performance & Relationships

The contract set the standard; daily reality decides whether you got it.
Suppliers, like employees, perform to what is measured, noticed, and
discussed — and drift when nothing is. This chapter closes the course with
the ongoing disciplines: scorecards that turn impressions into facts,
escalation that fixes problems without burning bridges, and the strategic
calls — consolidate, diversify, or switch — that shape your supply base
over years.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Turn impressions of a supplier into a scorecard of facts.
- Escalate a failing supplier professionally and early.
- Decide when to consolidate, diversify, or switch.
- Keep the relationship honest without making it adversarial.

## The scorecard: impressions become facts

Ask three colleagues about a supplier and you'll get three adjectives.
Scorecards replace adjectives with a short factual record, kept per
significant supplier, reviewed on a rhythm:

- **Measure the few things that hurt when they slip** — typically:
  on-time delivery rate, quality (rejects/returns at receiving, Chapter
  4's records feeding directly in), SLA performance where one exists
  (Chapter 3), pricing movement against the market, and responsiveness
  when something went wrong.
- **Source from records, not recollection.** The receiving log, the
  complaint trail, the invoice history — the data already exists if
  Chapters 1–4 are being lived. A scorecard assembled from memory is the
  loudest recent incident wearing a table.
- **Score, then talk.** Share the scorecard with the supplier at a
  regular review — quarterly for important ones. Suppliers respond to
  being measured exactly as the Customer Experience course says customers
  respond to being heard: the relationship where performance is discussed
  factually improves; the one where grievances accumulate silently
  explodes at renewal.

The Managing with Data course's warning applies here too: measure few
things honestly rather than many things decoratively, and let the trend,
not the single bad week, drive conclusions.

## When suppliers fail: escalate like a professional

Every supplier fails sometimes. What separates a supply base you can rely
on from a rolling crisis is how failure is handled:

1. **Raise it factually, at working level, fast.** The delivery was two
   days late; here's the impact. Most failures die here — and per the
   Sales course's objection-handling mirror, the supplier hearing specific
   facts responds better than one hearing accumulated temper.
2. **Patterns escalate with evidence.** Repeats go up a level — your
   manager to their manager — carrying the scorecard, not the anecdote.
   The contractual consequences (Chapter 3's credits and remedies) get
   invoked as written: consequences applied predictably read as fairness;
   consequences applied in anger read as war.
3. **Serious failures trigger the contract's machinery** — formal notice,
   cure periods, and genuine consequences up to termination. By this
   stage your paper trail (receiving records, notices, reviews) is either
   your strength or your regret.
4. **Always leave the repair path open.** The goal is performance, not
   victory. A supplier who recovers well after a hard, fair escalation
   often becomes the most reliable one you have — they've seen that you
   notice, and that you're worth keeping.

And the quiet counterpart: **pay well-performing suppliers on time**. The
Finance course said your payables are someone's receivables; a business
known to pay promptly gets priority when supply is short, favours when
schedules are tight, and honesty when problems loom. Payment behaviour is
the supplier relationship lever most businesses never think to pull.

## Portfolio calls: consolidate, diversify, switch

Above individual suppliers sits the shape of the whole base:

- **Consolidate** when fragmentation is costing you: many small suppliers
  for the same category means no volume leverage, many relationships to
  manage, and inconsistent quality. Fewer, deeper relationships earn
  better pricing and attention — this is a genuine trade.
- **Diversify** when dependence threatens you: any critical input with a
  single source is a single point of failure — the supply version of the
  Team Leadership course's cross-training rule. For items that stop your
  business when absent, a qualified second source (even at slightly worse
  price for a minority share) is insurance you buy deliberately.
- **The tension between these is real** and resolved per category, not
  per slogan: consolidate the routine, diversify the critical. Know which
  of your inputs are which — the one-page exercise of listing what stops
  the business if it fails to arrive is worth an afternoon.
- **Switch** when the scorecard, the market check, and the repair
  attempts all point the same way — and switch like a project, not a
  tantrum: qualify the replacement first, overlap deliveries through the
  transition, protect stock levels (Chapter 4) through the cutover, and
  exit per the contract's terms. The decision itself is a Decision
  Making course case: criteria, evidence, reversibility assessed — and
  switching costs counted honestly, because they're the moat every
  incumbent hides behind.

## The relationship, honestly

Good supplier relationships are not friendship and not warfare; they are
**aligned incentives, factually maintained**. Share forecasts so suppliers
can plan for you; tell them the truth about problems and priorities; let
them earn margin — a supplier squeezed to breaking rationally cuts the
corners you can't see. And keep the relationship institutional, not
personal: records, reviews, and files that survive any individual's
departure, so the knowledge belongs to the business — which is, in the
end, this whole course in one sentence.

## Do this at work

1. **Score one supplier on facts.** On-time rate, quality issues, responsiveness,
   price movement. Impressions are how bad suppliers survive good reputations.
2. **Take the scorecard to the review.** A conversation about recorded
   performance goes differently from one about how you feel it has been.
3. **Escalate one drift early.** Specifics, a date, and what needs to change.
   Suppliers rarely recover after the relationship sours; they often recover
   after an early, unemotional conversation.
4. **Check your single points of failure.** Which suppliers could stop you
   operating? That list is a risk register, and it should be short and
   deliberate.
5. **Review one consolidation.** Fewer suppliers means better prices and more
   exposure. Say which trade you are making rather than drifting into it.

## Summary

- Keep short factual scorecards per significant supplier — delivery,
  quality, SLA, pricing, responsiveness — built from records and reviewed
  with the supplier on a rhythm.
- Escalate failures factually and fast, patterns with evidence, and
  contract machinery predictably — always leaving the repair path open.
- Pay good suppliers on time; payment behaviour buys priority, favours,
  and honesty that money can't otherwise reach.
- Shape the base per category: consolidate the routine for leverage,
  diversify the critical for resilience, and switch as a planned project
  with switching costs counted honestly.
- Aim for aligned incentives, factually maintained — institutional
  relationships that survive individuals, with margin enough on both
  sides that nobody needs invisible corners.
