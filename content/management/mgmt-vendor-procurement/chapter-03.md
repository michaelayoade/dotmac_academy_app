# Contracts & Supplier SLAs

A contract is not a weapon you draw when things go wrong; it is the shared
memory of what both sides agreed while they were still friends. Most
supplier disputes are not battles between crooks — they are two honest
parties with two different recollections and nothing written down. This
chapter covers the terms that matter, service levels with teeth, and the
renewal discipline that stops yesterday's good deal from quietly becoming
today's bad one.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Identify the contract terms that actually decide outcomes.
- Attach consequences to service levels so they mean something.
- Read the supplier's paper before signing it.
- Argue the uplift clause once, at signature, where it is worth arguing.
- Diarise notice dates so renewals are chosen rather than suffered.

## The terms that actually matter

Long contracts intimidate, but for most purchases a handful of clauses do
nearly all the work. Whatever the format — formal contract, signed
quotation, or exchange of letters — make sure these are explicit:

- **Scope and specification.** What exactly is being supplied, to what
  standard, referencing the spec from Chapter 2. The phrase "as discussed"
  is a dispute with a delivery date.
- **Price and price-change rules.** Not just today's price but *when and
  how it can move*: fixed for the term, indexed to something visible, or
  changeable only with notice. Suppliers remember the price flexibility
  you never capped.
- **Payment terms.** When payment is due, against what evidence (delivery,
  acceptance, milestones), and the Finance course's reminder applies in
  both directions: your payment behaviour is part of the deal you're
  making.
- **Delivery and risk.** Where, when, who transports, and — easily missed
  — *when responsibility transfers*. If goods are damaged in transit,
  whose loss is it? The answer should come from the contract, not the
  argument.
- **Warranty and remedies.** What happens when the thing fails: repair,
  replace, refund, and within what period. A warranty without a response
  time is a promise without a clock.
- **Exit and term.** How long the agreement runs, how either side leaves,
  and what notice is required. The exit clause is read once at signing and
  studied hard exactly when the relationship sours — write it for that
  second reading.

## Service levels with consequences

For ongoing services — maintenance, logistics, support, anything where the
supplier performs repeatedly — the contract needs a **service-level
agreement**: the measurable standard the supplier commits to. The
discipline mirrors the customer-facing SLAs of the Customer Experience
course, from the other side of the table:

- **Measurable, few, and meaningful.** Response time, resolution time,
  delivery punctuality, quality reject rate — pick the handful that map to
  what actually hurts you when it slips, and define how each is measured
  and by whom. An SLA nobody measures is decoration.
- **Consequences that escalate.** Service credits or penalties for misses,
  escalating with severity and repetition, with termination rights for
  persistent failure. The purpose is not revenge income — it's making the
  supplier's incentives point at your uptime. A penalty too small to
  change supplier behaviour is a licence fee for failing.
- **Review rhythm.** A standing meeting — monthly or quarterly by stakes —
  where measured performance is reviewed against the SLA. Chapter 5 builds
  the scorecard; the contract is what makes the conversation contractual
  rather than optional.

## Reading before signing: the supplier's paper

Often the supplier presents *their* standard contract. Read it as a
negotiator, not a passenger — their template was written to protect them,
which is fair, and amendable, which is the point. Watch particularly for:
auto-renewal clauses with long notice windows (diarise them or they decide
for you); limitation-of-liability caps set below the damage a failure
could actually cause you; exclusivity or minimum-purchase commitments
hiding in schedules; and price-review clauses that move only upward. None
of these are outrages; all of them are negotiable — but only before
signing. The Sales course's rule works on this side too: every concession
you grant should earn one back.

## Renewals: the silent margin leak

Contracts age badly when unattended. The market price fell; your contract
price didn't. Volumes doubled; the discount tier didn't. The service is
now routine; the premium-support fee survives. Renewal discipline is
simple and pays every year:

- **Diarise every contract's end and notice dates** the day it is signed.
  An auto-renewal missed by a week can cost a year.
- **Re-compete periodically, even happy relationships.** Not every year,
  and not as a bluff — but a genuine market check every few renewal
  cycles keeps pricing honest and your knowledge of alternatives current.
  Tell the incumbent honestly; good suppliers expect it, and per the
  Decision Making course, the do-nothing option deserves costing too.
- **Renegotiate on facts.** Volumes, performance record, market prices —
  the scorecard from Chapter 5 walks into the renewal meeting with you.
  Loyalty is worth something; unexamined loyalty is worth precisely what
  the supplier decides to charge for it.

### Worked example: the 8% that nobody argues with

A ₦2,000,000 annual contract carries an "annual adjustment" clause of 8%.
Each year it arrives as a single-line notice, and each year 8% is small
enough that challenging it costs more attention than it seems to be worth.

| Year | Annual price |
| ---: | ---: |
| 1 | ₦2,000,000 |
| 2 | ₦2,160,000 |
| 3 | ₦2,332,800 |
| 4 | ₦2,519,424 |
| 5 | **₦2,720,978** |

| | |
| --- | ---: |
| Five-year total as signed | ₦11,733,202 |
| Five-year total if held flat | ₦10,000,000 |
| **Paid for never having the conversation** | **₦1,733,202** |

By year five the price is **36% higher** than year one, and the cumulative
overpayment is nearly a full year's contract value. No single increase was
unreasonable. That is precisely the mechanism: a leak survives because each
individual instance is beneath the threshold at which anyone escalates.

The lesson is not that uplifts are illegitimate — costs genuinely rise, and a
supplier holding prices flat for five years while their own inputs inflate is
a supplier heading for trouble you will inherit. The lesson is that the clause
should be **argued once, at signature**, where it is worth ₦1.7m of attention:
cap it, tie it to a published index rather than the supplier's discretion, or
trade accepting it for something you want. Arguing it at each renewal is where
it always wins, because by then it is only ever "8%".

And diarise the notice date the day you sign. An auto-renewal missed by a week
does not cost you a negotiation — it costs you a year of the number above.

## Do this at work

1. **Find your uplift clauses.** Any contract with an annual adjustment.
   Compute five years of it compounded, and see what never having the
   conversation costs.
2. **Diarise every notice date.** Today, for every live contract. An
   auto-renewal missed by a week costs a year.
3. **Check one SLA for teeth.** What actually happens when the supplier
   misses it? A service level with no consequence is a wish.
4. **Read one contract you signed on their paper.** Look specifically at
   termination, liability, and price change. Those three decide most
   disputes.
5. **Market-test one relationship.** Not as a bluff — a genuine check, told
   honestly to the incumbent. Unexamined loyalty is worth exactly what the
   supplier decides to charge for it.

## Summary

- A contract is shared memory: scope, price-change rules, payment terms,
  delivery and risk transfer, warranty with a clock, and an exit clause
  written for the day the relationship sours.
- Ongoing services need SLAs: few measurable standards that map to real
  pain, consequences that escalate enough to steer supplier behaviour, and
  a standing review rhythm.
- The supplier's standard contract protects the supplier — read for
  auto-renewals, liability caps, hidden commitments, and one-way price
  clauses, and negotiate before signing.
- Diarise end and notice dates at signing; missed auto-renewals decide for
  you.
- Renew on facts — volumes, performance, market checks — because
  unexamined loyalty is priced by the other side.
