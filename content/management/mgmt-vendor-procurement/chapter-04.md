# Receiving, Inventory & Asset Custody

The purchase order was clean, the contract solid — and none of it matters
if what arrives is short, wrong, or wanders off a shelf three weeks later.
Between the supplier's truck and the moment of use lies the least glamorous
and most leak-prone stretch of the whole buying cycle. This chapter covers
receiving goods properly, keeping inventory records that match reality, and
custody — knowing, at all times, who holds what.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Treat receiving as the last cheap moment to catch a problem.
- Keep records that match what is actually on the shelf.
- Attach names to assets so custody is real.
- Measure shrinkage rather than absorbing it.

## Receiving: the last cheap moment to catch a problem

The moment of delivery is the last point where a discrepancy is easy to
fix. After the driver leaves, every shortfall becomes your word against
theirs, and after the goods disperse, it becomes nobody's word at all. The
receiving discipline:

- **Check against the PO, not the delivery note.** The delivery note says
  what the supplier *sent*; the PO says what you *ordered*. Count actual
  items against the PO: quantity, type, condition. Chapter 1's three-way
  match starts its second leg here.
- **Inspect before signing.** The signature on a delivery note is
  evidence of acceptance. Damaged carton? Short count? Note it on the
  document before signing, photograph it, and notify the supplier the
  same day per the contract's window (Chapter 3). "We noticed later"
  is the weakest sentence in procurement.
- **Partial and substituted deliveries get recorded as exactly that.**
  Suppliers under pressure deliver what they have and true-up later — fine
  when tracked, a silent shortfall when not. The PO stays open until it is
  actually filled.
- **Separate the roles.** The person receiving should not be the person
  who ordered, where headcount allows — the same separation-of-duties
  logic from Chapter 1. In a small team, a second pair of eyes at
  delivery time for high-value items is the affordable version.

Only after a clean receipt does the invoice earn payment: PO, receiving
record, invoice — three documents telling one story.

## Inventory: records that match the shelf

Inventory records exist to answer two questions instantly: *what do we
have?* and *where is it?* Everything else is technique. The essentials:

- **One record system, updated at the moment of movement.** Goods in,
  goods out, and transfers recorded when they happen, not reconstructed on
  Friday. A ledger book maintained at the moment beats software updated
  from memory — the discipline, as with Chapter 1's spine, outranks the
  tooling.
- **Named issue, not open shelf.** Stock leaves the store against a
  record: who took it, for what job, when. This isn't suspicion — it's
  what makes usage visible (which jobs consume what), reorder points
  honest, and shrinkage detectable while it's still small.
- **Count reality against records regularly.** Full counts periodically,
  and small rolling spot-counts of a few items continually. The point of
  counting is the *variance*: records and shelves drifting apart is the
  early warning for process failure, theft, or breakage being hidden —
  and per the Decision Making course, the variance is a symptom whose
  root cause deserves the five-whys treatment, not just a corrected
  number.
- **Know your reorder points — and your dead stock.** Minimum levels
  trigger reordering before stockouts halt work; and the reverse audit
  matters too, because shelves full of items nobody has issued in a year
  are the Finance course's cash-flow chapter gathering dust. Inventory is
  money in a less useful shape.

## Custody: assets with names attached

Beyond consumable stock, businesses hand people durable things — tools,
laptops, vehicles, test equipment, keys. Custody is the discipline of
every such asset having, at all times, a **named holder**:

- **An asset register** — what the business owns, identified specifically
  (serial numbers, tags), with purchase date and value. Unregistered
  assets are unloseable in the worst way: nobody can miss what nobody
  listed.
- **Issue and return records** — signed out to a person, condition noted,
  signed back in. Transfers between people go through the record, not the
  corridor; the chain of custody breaks at exactly the handoffs nobody
  wrote down.
- **Periodic verification** — does the register match reality? Same
  variance logic as stock counts, same escalating attention when it
  doesn't.

The cultural point matters more than the paperwork: custody records
protect the *holder*. The technician with a signed, condition-noted issue
record can hand back a worn tool without suspicion; the one who took it
informally inherits every doubt. As with approval discipline, the controls
are what let honest people prove they're honest.

## Shrinkage: the quiet tax

Shrinkage — stock that disappears through theft, damage, miscounting, or
informal "borrowing" — is a percentage tax on everything you buy, and it
thrives on exactly the informality this chapter removes: unchecked
deliveries, open shelves, unnamed custody, uncounted variance. You will
never reach zero; you can make it visible, small, and boring. The pattern
to watch hardest is normalisation — the site where "some loss is normal"
has stopped measuring, and what is unmeasured grows.

## Do this at work

1. **Check one delivery properly.** Against the order, not just the
   delivery note. Signing for what you did not verify is how disputes are
   lost.
2. **Count one shelf.** Compare it to the record. The gap is your data
   quality, and it is usually worse than assumed.
3. **Name the custodian of five assets.** Vehicles, tools, devices. Assets
   owned by "the company" walk away; assets owned by a person do not.
4. **Measure shrinkage once.** Put a number on what goes missing annually.
   Unmeasured, it is absorbed; measured, it becomes a decision.
5. **Find the reconciliation nobody does.** Ordered, received, invoiced,
   paid. Where those four are never compared, errors survive indefinitely.

## Summary

- Receive against the PO with inspection before signature; discrepancies
  noted, photographed, and reported same-day are recoverable — noticed
  later, they're donations.
- Keep one inventory record updated at the moment of movement, issue stock
  to named people for named purposes, and treat count variances as
  symptoms deserving root-cause attention.
- Watch both ends: reorder points prevent stockouts, dead-stock audits
  recover cash sleeping on shelves.
- Every durable asset has a register entry and a named holder, with signed
  issue, transfer, and return — custody records protect the honest.
- Shrinkage is a tax on informality: make it measured, visible, and
  boring, and be most suspicious wherever loss has become "normal".
