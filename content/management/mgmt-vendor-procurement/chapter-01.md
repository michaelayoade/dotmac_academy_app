# Procurement Basics

Every business buys things — goods, services, equipment, spare parts — and
most buy them badly, not through dishonesty but through informality: the
urgent phone order nobody recorded, the supplier chosen because they always
answer, the invoice that arrives for more than anyone remembers agreeing.
Procurement is simply purchasing made deliberate. This chapter covers the
basic machinery — requisition, approval, purchase order — and why the
discipline exists even when it feels like paperwork slowing you down.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Follow the request–approve–purchase spine and say what each step protects.
- Explain why approval discipline exists beyond bureaucracy.
- Say who may commit the organisation to spend, and to how much.
- Apply the same spine proportionately in a small business.

## The three-step spine

Whatever the size of the business, deliberate purchasing runs through the
same three steps:

1. **Requisition** — someone states the need, in writing: what, how many,
   for what purpose, needed by when. The requisition is not bureaucracy; it
   is the moment a vague want ("we need more cable ties around here")
   becomes a specific, costable request that someone owns.
2. **Approval** — someone with the authority to spend agrees the purchase
   should happen, *before* it happens. Approval limits are set in advance:
   small amounts approved locally, larger amounts higher up. The point is
   not distrust; it's that the person spending and the person accountable
   for the budget (see the Finance course's budget chapter) are often
   different people, and the money leaves only once.
3. **Purchase order (PO)** — the formal instruction to the supplier: items,
   quantities, agreed prices, delivery terms. The PO is the contract's
   everyday cousin, and its superpower is simple: **when the invoice
   arrives, there is something to check it against.** No PO means the
   supplier's memory of the price wins by default.

Three steps, three documents, one thread: the requisition says *we need
it*, the approval says *we may buy it*, the PO says *we are buying exactly
this*. Later, the delivery is checked against the PO (Chapter 4) and the
invoice is checked against both. That chain — often called three-way
matching — is what makes overbilling, duplicate invoices, and phantom
deliveries visible instead of quietly paid.

## Why approval discipline exists

Approval steps are where informal businesses push back hardest: "it slows
us down", "we trust each other", "it was urgent." Each objection deserves
an honest answer:

- **Speed.** A right-sized approval ladder is fast — small purchases need
  one quick yes, and genuinely urgent categories can have pre-approved
  arrangements (standing orders, small local floats) designed *in advance*.
  What actually slows businesses down is the aftermath of unapproved
  spending: the surprise invoice, the budget hole discovered at month end,
  the argument about who authorised what.
- **Trust.** Approval protects the trusted more than it polices them. The
  storekeeper who can buy anything, from anyone, with no counter-signature
  is one grievance away from an accusation they cannot disprove. Separation
  of duties — the person who requests is not the person who approves is
  not the person who receives — is what lets honest people *demonstrate*
  they are honest.
- **Urgency.** Real emergencies exist, so design for them: an emergency
  path with after-the-fact review, not a culture where "urgent" is the
  password that switches all controls off. Watch the ratio — when a large
  share of purchases travel the emergency lane, you don't have emergencies,
  you have a planning problem wearing a siren.

The deeper reason for the discipline is the one from the Finance course:
**every purchase is a business decision about scarce cash**, and decisions
(per the Decision Making course) deserve process proportionate to their
stakes. Approval is simply decision sizing applied to spending — trivial
buys glide, large commitments get real scrutiny before the money is gone.

## Who may buy: the quiet rules that prevent loud problems

A few standing rules do most of procurement's protective work:

- **Named authority, written limits.** Who can approve what amount is
  written down and known. Ambiguity here is where both delays and abuses
  live.
- **No self-approval.** Nobody approves their own requisition, however
  senior. Not because seniors are suspect, but because the rule only
  protects anyone if it protects everyone.
- **Declare interests.** If the supplier is your cousin's company, you say
  so and step out of the choice. The purchase may still be right; the
  undeclared version of it is always wrong.
- **One buying doorway.** Suppliers learn quickly who can be talked into
  informal orders. Routing purchases through the defined path isn't just
  internal tidiness — it changes how suppliers treat you, because pricing
  games and favour-trading need side doors to work.

## Small business, same spine

None of this requires software or a department. A duplicate book of
requisitions, a manager's initials, a numbered PO pad, and a folder where
PO, delivery note, and invoice are stapled together *is* a procurement
system — the discipline is in the sequence, not the tooling. Grow the
tooling when volume demands it; the three-step spine and the separation of
duties stay identical from market stall to multinational, and the business
that learns them small avoids learning them expensively large.

## Do this at work

1. **Trace one purchase end to end.** Who requested it, who approved it, who
   ordered it, who confirmed it arrived. Any step performed by the same
   person as another is where control is missing.
2. **Check your own authority.** What may you commit without approval? If
   you are not certain, neither is anyone else.
3. **Find a purchase made outside the spine.** There is usually one that was
   urgent. Ask what would have happened if it had gone wrong.
4. **Separate one duty.** Ordering, receiving and paying should not all sit
   with one person. Where they do, fix it before anything goes wrong rather
   than after.
5. **Write the small-business version.** Three lines, proportionate. A spine
   nobody can follow is the same as no spine.

## Summary

- Deliberate purchasing runs requisition → approval → purchase order: the
  need stated, the spend authorised in advance, the order specified so the
  invoice can be checked against it.
- Three-way matching — PO, delivery, invoice — is what makes overbilling
  and phantom deliveries visible instead of quietly paid.
- Approval discipline is decision sizing for money: fast lanes for small
  and genuinely urgent buys, real scrutiny before large ones, and an
  emergency path designed in advance rather than a culture of exceptions.
- Separation of duties and no-self-approval protect honest people; declared
  interests and one buying doorway starve the games that need side doors.
- The spine scales down as well as up — paper and initials are a real
  system; the sequence, not the software, is the control.
