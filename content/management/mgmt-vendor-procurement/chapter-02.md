# Sourcing & Quotations

Once the business knows what it needs, someone must decide *who* supplies
it and *at what real cost* — and both halves of that sentence are harder
than they look. This chapter covers writing specifications that get you
comparable quotes, comparing those quotes on total cost of ownership rather
than sticker price, and running the sourcing conversation so that you end
up with a supplier, not just a discount.

## The specification: decide before you shop

The single biggest cause of bad purchases is a vague specification. "Get us
a generator" produces three incomparable quotes, a choice made on price
alone, and a machine that turns out too small for the load. Before
approaching any supplier, write down:

- **What it must do** — the requirement in performance terms: capacity,
  output, duty cycle, environment. State the *need*, and let suppliers
  propose how to meet it; over-specifying a brand or model narrows the
  field and imports someone else's markup.
- **Requirements versus preferences** — exactly as in the Decision Making
  course's criteria discipline: what disqualifies an offer (must-haves,
  safety standards, delivery deadline) versus what earns extra credit
  (nice-to-haves, longer warranty). Written before quotes arrive, this
  stops the shiniest proposal from quietly rewriting your priorities.
- **The context a supplier needs** — quantities, delivery location and
  constraints, timing, quality standards, after-sales expectations. Every
  ambiguity you leave becomes either a padded price (the supplier prices
  the risk) or a dispute later (you assumed; they didn't).

A good specification does one more quiet job: it makes quotes
**comparable**. Three quotes against the same written spec can be judged
side by side; three quotes against three phone conversations cannot.

## Total cost of ownership: the price is not the cost

The sticker price is where the cost *starts*. What the purchase actually
costs the business over its life includes:

- **Running costs** — fuel, power, consumables, spare parts. A cheap
  machine that drinks expensive consumables loses to a dearer one that
  sips, usually within the first year or two.
- **Maintenance and downtime** — service intervals, parts availability,
  and what it costs *you* when the thing is out of action. A supplier with
  no local parts turns every breakdown into a long outage; that outage is
  part of the price.
- **Life and residual value** — how long it lasts, and what it's worth at
  the end. Two purchases at different prices with different lifespans need
  comparing per year of service, not per invoice.
- **The soft costs** — training, installation, integration with what you
  already run, and the switching costs if this purchase locks you to a
  supplier's ecosystem of parts and service.

This is the Finance course's unit-economics honesty applied to buying:
count everything the purchase consumes, not just the number on the quote.
The practical tool is a simple side-by-side table over a realistic horizon
— purchase, running, maintenance, disposal — for each serious option. It
takes an hour, and it routinely reverses the ranking the sticker prices
suggested.

## Comparing quotes without fooling yourself

With comparable quotes in hand, discipline still matters:

- **Get enough quotes, not maximal quotes.** For routine purchases, two or
  three genuine quotes keep everyone honest. Reserve the wide competitive
  net for large or unfamiliar spends. (And a "quote" from a supplier who
  was told the target price is theatre, not competition.)
- **Check what's excluded.** The lowest quote is often lowest because
  something is missing — delivery, installation, taxes, warranty. Force
  every quote onto the same scope before comparing; the sticker-price
  winner frequently stops winning.
- **Weigh the supplier, not just the offer.** Capacity to actually deliver
  at your volume, financial stability, references from customers like you,
  after-sales behaviour. A brilliant price from a supplier who fails to
  deliver is the most expensive option on the table — and Chapter 5's
  scorecards are where these judgements get grounded in evidence rather
  than charm.
- **Beware the suspiciously low bid.** It usually means the supplier
  misunderstood the spec, plans to recover margin through extras and
  variations, or intends to win you cheap and raise prices once switching
  is painful. Ask the low bidder to walk through how they'll meet the spec
  at that price; the answer is always informative.

## Sourcing is a relationship decision

The quote decides today's purchase; the sourcing decision shapes the next
three years. For anything you'll buy repeatedly, you are choosing a
*counterparty*, and the negotiation principles from the Sales course apply
in mirror image: the supplier who trades concessions transparently, quotes
realistic numbers, and says "no, we can't do that well" is showing you how
they'll behave when something goes wrong. Take character evidence as
seriously as price evidence — you'll be living with both.

## Summary

- Write the specification first: performance requirements, must-haves
  versus preferences, and full context — vagueness becomes padded prices
  or disputes.
- Compare total cost of ownership over a realistic horizon — running
  costs, maintenance, downtime, lifespan, soft costs — not sticker prices;
  the ranking often reverses.
- Force quotes onto identical scope, check exclusions, and treat
  suspiciously low bids as questions to investigate, not bargains to bank.
- Evaluate the supplier as well as the offer: delivery capacity,
  stability, references, after-sales behaviour.
- For repeat purchases you're choosing a counterparty, not a price — how a
  supplier behaves in the sourcing conversation is how they'll behave in
  the dispute.
