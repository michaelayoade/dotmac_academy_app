# Risk Assessment That Is Actually Used

Most risk assessments are written once, filed, and never read by anyone doing
the work. They satisfy an auditor and change nothing, which is worse than
useless — it produces a documented belief that the risk is managed. This
chapter is about the version that works: short, specific, produced with the
people who do the job, and revisited when something changes.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Run the five steps of a risk assessment on a real task.
- Score likelihood and severity, and read what the score is for.
- Distinguish inherent risk from residual risk, and say why only one of them
  tells you whether to proceed.
- Recognise the assessment that exists to be filed rather than followed.
- Say when an assessment must be redone.

## The five steps

1. **Identify the hazards.** Walk the job, watch it being done, and ask the
   people who do it — they know the shortcuts, the awkward step, and the
   thing that nearly went wrong last month. An assessment written at a desk
   finds the hazards you already knew about.
2. **Decide who might be harmed, and how.** Not just the operator: the
   passer-by, the cleaner who comes at night, the contractor unfamiliar with
   your site, the young or new worker, the pregnant colleague, the lone
   worker with no one to notice.
3. **Evaluate the risk and decide the controls.** Score it, then apply the
   hierarchy from Chapter 3 — eliminate before you manage.
4. **Record it, and make it reachable.** A control the crew cannot see is a
   control that does not exist.
5. **Review it.** Not annually because the calendar said so, but when
   something changes — and after anything that went wrong.

## Scoring: what the number is for

A simple matrix multiplies **likelihood** by **severity**, each on a scale of
1 to 5:

| | 1 · Negligible | 2 · Minor | 3 · Moderate | 4 · Major | 5 · Fatal |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **5 · Almost certain** | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | **25** |
| **4 · Likely** | 4 | 8 | 12 | **16** | **20** |
| **3 · Possible** | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | **15** |
| **2 · Unlikely** | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
| **1 · Rare** | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |

The number is **not** a measurement. It is a way of arguing about priority
with a shared vocabulary — and its real value is comparative: this job is
worse than that one, this control moved us further than that one.

Two disciplines stop the scoring becoming theatre:

- **Severity is what could *reasonably* happen, not the average outcome.**
  A fall from height is scored on the injury it can cause, not on the fact
  that most falls end in bruises. Scoring by average outcome is how fatal
  hazards end up rated "moderate".
- **Anything with a credible fatal outcome gets attention regardless of
  likelihood.** A 5×1 scores the same as a 1×5 on this grid and they are not
  the same situation. The multiplication is a starting point, not the
  decision — which is exactly the ruin clause from the Decision Making
  course, in a different uniform.

## Inherent and residual risk

The score before controls is the **inherent** risk. The score with the
controls actually in place — not the controls you intend, or the ones written
in the file — is the **residual** risk.

Only the residual number answers the question you care about: *is it
acceptable to do this job today, as it is actually set up?*

This is where most assessments quietly fail. The controls are listed, the
residual score is calculated as though they were all present, and on site
two of them are not: the guard is off, the second person never arrived, the
harness is in the other van. The paperwork says 4; the job is running at 16.

The habit that closes it: **score the residual against what you can see, not
what is written.** And if a control is missing, the job's score has changed
and the decision must be made again — which is what a stop-work
authority is for, and Chapter 4's subject.

## Worked example: the same job, twice

Working at height to replace a fixture, assessed honestly:

| | Likelihood | Severity | Score |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Inherent** — ladder, one person, no restraint | 4 | 5 | **20** |
| **After controls** — tower scaffold, two people, harness, ground-level pre-assembly | 2 | 4 | **8** |
| **As found on site** — tower scaffold, one person, no harness | 3 | 5 | **15** |

Three readings, and the third is the one that matters.

The first row is why the job needs controlling at all. The second row is what
the file says, and it justifies proceeding. **The third row is what is
actually happening**, and it is nearly twice the assessed residual risk — not
because anyone was reckless, but because the second person was pulled to
another job and the harness was in a van that went elsewhere.

Note also what the controls did and did not do. Severity barely moved — a
fall from that height is still severe — because most of the gain came from
*likelihood*. That is typical, and it is why the biggest single win in the
list is the one that changes the job rather than protecting the person:
**pre-assembling at ground level removes time spent at height altogether.**

## The assessment that exists to be filed

You can spot it quickly. It is generic rather than about this job. It lists
controls in the future tense. It has no date, or a date years old. Nobody who
does the work has read it, and several of them would disagree with it. Its
final page has signatures collected in a room.

The fix is not a better template. It is producing it *with* the crew, keeping
it to one page they will actually read, and treating the review trigger as
real:

- the method, equipment or materials change;
- the people change — a new starter, a contractor, someone returning from
  long absence;
- the environment changes — night work, rain, a busier site;
- something goes wrong, or nearly does;
- or the work is being done differently from the assessment, which is
  information about the assessment as much as about the crew.

## Do this at work

1. **Pick one routine task and assess it with the crew**, not for them. Ask
   what nearly went wrong recently. The first answer is usually the finding.
2. **Score inherent and residual separately.** If your assessment has only
   one number, you cannot tell whether the controls are doing anything.
3. **Go and check a residual score against the site.** Are all the listed
   controls present today? The gap between the file and the ground is the
   real risk position.
4. **Find one generic assessment** covering a job it was not written for.
   Rewrite it for the actual task, on one page.
5. **Look for a fatal-outcome hazard scored as moderate.** Severity scored on
   average outcome rather than credible outcome is the commonest scoring
   error.
6. **Check who else could be harmed.** Cleaners, contractors, visitors, lone
   workers, the public. The list of people is usually shorter than it should
   be.
7. **Set a real review trigger** on one assessment — an event, not a date.

## Summary

- Five steps: identify hazards with the people who do the work, decide who
  could be harmed, evaluate and control, record reachably, review on change.
- The likelihood × severity score is a shared vocabulary for priority, not a
  measurement — and its value is comparative.
- Score severity on the credible outcome, not the average, and treat a
  credible fatality as demanding attention whatever the likelihood.
- Inherent risk justifies controlling; only **residual** risk, scored against
  what is actually present today, tells you whether to proceed.
- In the example the file said 8 and the site was running at 15 — because two
  controls were absent, not because anyone was reckless.
- An assessment that is generic, undated, future-tense and unread by the crew
  is a documented belief that the risk is managed. That is worse than none.
