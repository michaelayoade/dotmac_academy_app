# Incidents, Investigation & Culture

Something will go wrong. What a manager does in the hours and weeks after
decides two things: whether the same event happens again, and whether anyone
tells you about the next one. This chapter covers the immediate response, an
investigation that finds causes rather than culprits, and the culture that
determines how much you get to know.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Act correctly in the first hour of an incident.
- Preserve the scene and the evidence without obstructing care.
- Investigate to root cause without stopping at a person's name.
- Write findings and actions that actually get done.
- Read your reporting rate as a measure of trust rather than of safety.

## The first hour

In order, and the order matters:

1. **Make people safe.** Casualty care first, then remove or isolate the
   continuing danger so nobody else is hurt — including responders, who are
   regularly the second casualty.
2. **Secure the scene.** Once care allows, stop the area being cleared,
   repaired or tidied. The instinct to restore normal operation destroys the
   evidence that would explain the event.
3. **Preserve what will disappear.** Photographs from several angles,
   equipment as found, settings, readings, the actual damaged item retained
   rather than binned. Names of everyone present — memory decays within
   hours and converges on a shared story by the next day.
4. **Notify.** Internally per your procedure, and to the regulator where the
   event is reportable. Reporting thresholds and deadlines are specific;
   know yours before you need them, because the clock starts at the incident
   and not at the moment you look it up.
5. **Look after the people.** The injured person, the witnesses, and often
   the person who was operating. Practical support, and a clear statement
   that the investigation is about causes.

What not to do in the first hour: assign blame, take detailed formal
statements while people are shaken, or promise an outcome.

## Investigation that finds causes

The purpose is to prevent recurrence, which means the investigation must not
stop at the first human error it finds. Chapter 3 of the Decision Making
course applies directly: **if your root cause is a name, you have a symptom
with feelings.**

Run it in this order:

- **Establish the timeline first.** What happened, in sequence, with times,
  from records and from people separately. Most confusion resolves once the
  order is fixed.
- **Separate observation from inference** in every account. "The guard was
  off" is an observation; "he removed the guard to work faster" is a theory
  that needs testing.
- **Ask why the error was possible, and why it was not caught.** Two
  different questions, and both have system answers: what allowed this action
  to be taken at all, and what should have detected it before harm?
- **Look for the conditions.** Fatigue, time pressure, missing equipment,
  unclear method, a defeated control, an untested assumption about
  competence. These are the findings that prevent recurrence, because they
  are the ones present for everyone else too.
- **Check whether the plan made the safe method impractical** — Chapter 4's
  point. It is a common finding and an uncomfortable one, because the
  investigator often reports to whoever set the plan.

A useful discipline: **investigate near misses with the same method**, at
lower intensity. The causes are identical and the cost of learning is zero.

## Findings that get acted on

Investigations fail at the end at least as often as in the middle. A report
with twenty recommendations, no owners and no dates produces nothing.

| Weak finding | Strong finding |
| --- | --- |
| "Operator failed to follow procedure" | "The procedure required a tool that has not been on this van since June" |
| "Reinforce training" | "Two-hour practical reassessment for the six crew doing this task — Ada, by 30 September" |
| "Improve supervision" | "Second person required for this task; scheduling changed to allow it — Ops manager, from next rota" |
| "Remind staff to wear PPE" | "On-tool extraction fitted so the mask is no longer the primary control — by 15 October" |

The right-hand column shares three properties: it names a **system change**
rather than a person's effort, it has **one owner and a date**, and it would
have prevented *this* event. Test each recommendation against that last
question — a recommendation that would not have prevented the incident it
came from is a good idea in the wrong document.

Then close the loop, in the sense the Data course means: check on the date
whether the action happened *and* whether it worked. Actions that are never
verified teach an organisation that investigation is a ceremony.

## Reporting rate is a trust measure

Here is the counter-intuitive finding a manager must be ready for: **a team
that starts reporting more near misses has usually become safer, not more
dangerous.**

Reporting reflects two things multiplied together — how much is happening,
and how safe people feel telling you. When the second rises, the count rises,
and a manager who reacts to the number rather than the cause will crush the
thing they just built.

So read reporting as the Data course reads any metric: the count alone is
uninterpretable. Watch instead:

- **the ratio of near misses to injuries** — a healthy system reports many
  near misses per injury; a system reporting almost none has an information
  problem, not a hazard-free workplace;
- **who reports** — if reports come only from supervisors, the frontline does
  not believe it is safe;
- **what happened to the last ten reports** — the honest measure of whether
  reporting is worth anyone's time.

**Silence is the most dangerous reading on any safety dashboard.** A team with
no incidents, no near misses and no reports is either extraordinary or not
telling you, and the second is far more common.

## Just culture: the line that has to be drawn

Blame-free does not mean consequence-free, and pretending otherwise loses the
workforce's respect. The workable distinction:

- **Error** — a slip, a lapse, a genuine mistake. Console and fix the system.
- **At-risk behaviour** — a shortcut where the risk was not appreciated,
  usually normalised across the team. Coach, and fix what makes the shortcut
  attractive.
- **Reckless behaviour** — a conscious, unjustifiable disregard for a known
  serious risk. This is a conduct matter, per the People course's chapter 4.

The test that keeps it fair is the **substitution test**: would another
competent person, in the same situation, with the same pressures and the same
information, plausibly have done the same? If yes, you are looking at a
system problem wearing an individual's name.

And the rule that makes reporting survive: **however the conduct is handled,
the person who reports is protected.** An organisation that disciplines
someone for the thing they voluntarily disclosed will not be told anything
twice.

## Do this at work

1. **Know your reportable thresholds now.** Which events must go to the
   regulator, within what time. The clock starts at the incident.
2. **Write your first-hour list** and put it where it will be found — in the
   van, on the wall. Nobody recalls a procedure during an incident.
3. **Investigate one near miss properly** this month, with the full method.
   Zero cost, identical causes.
4. **Re-read your last investigation.** Does any finding stop at a person's
   name? Ask why the error was possible and why it was not caught.
5. **Audit your last ten actions.** Done? Verified? Did the thing they were
   meant to change actually change?
6. **Check your near-miss to injury ratio.** If you report almost no near
   misses, you have an information problem rather than a safe workplace.
7. **Apply the substitution test** to the last incident someone was blamed
   for. Would another competent person, under the same pressures, plausibly
   have done the same?

## Summary

- First hour, in order: make people safe, secure the scene, preserve
  perishable evidence, notify within your thresholds, support the people.
- Investigate to causes: timeline first, observation separated from
  inference, and two questions — why was the error possible, and why was it
  not caught?
- If the root cause is a name, keep going. Look for fatigue, pressure,
  missing equipment, defeated controls and plans that made the safe method
  impractical.
- Findings must name a system change, carry one owner and a date, and would
  have prevented *this* event — then be verified on the date.
- A rising near-miss count usually means reporting became safe. Silence is
  the most dangerous reading on a safety dashboard.
- Just culture distinguishes error, at-risk behaviour and recklessness, uses
  the substitution test to stay fair, and protects the reporter regardless.
