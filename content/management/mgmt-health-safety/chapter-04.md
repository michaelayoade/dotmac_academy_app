# Safe Systems, Competence & Stopping Work

Between a risk assessment and a job actually done safely sit three things: a
method the crew can follow, people competent to follow it, and permission to
stop when the conditions are not what the method assumed. This chapter is
about those three — and about the pressures that quietly remove all of them.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Write a safe system of work the crew will actually use.
- Say what competence consists of, beyond having attended a course.
- Run permits and lone-working arrangements that mean something.
- Give and honour a genuine stop-work authority.
- Recognise when scheduling pressure has become a safety control failure.

## A safe system of work people can follow

A safe system of work is the agreed method: the sequence, the controls at
each step, the equipment, and who does what. It fails for predictable
reasons.

- **It is too long.** A twelve-page method for a two-hour job will not be
  read. One page, or a card that fits a pocket, is read.
- **It was written by someone who has not done the job.** Crews spot this
  instantly, and it costs the document its authority permanently.
- **It describes the ideal conditions.** Real methods must survive rain,
  darkness, a missing part and a difficult customer — or say explicitly what
  to do when those arrive, which is usually *stop and call*.
- **It is not where the work is.** Filed centrally rather than in the van, on
  the machine, or in the person's hand.

The test is simple and uncomfortable: **ask someone doing the job to talk you
through the method without looking at it.** What they say is the actual
system of work. The document is a description of it, and where the two differ
the document is wrong more often than the crew is.

## Competence is four things, not one

"Trained" is routinely used to mean "competent" and they are not the same.
Competence is:

| | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Knowledge** | They understand the hazard and why the control exists — not just the step |
| **Skill** | They can actually perform the task to standard, demonstrated |
| **Experience** | They have done it, in real conditions, enough times |
| **Judgement** | They can recognise when the situation has left the method behind |

The fourth is the one that separates a safe worker from a trained one, and it
is the hardest to certify. It is also why "he's done the course" is not an
answer to "is he competent to do this alone, at night, in the rain?"

Practical consequences a manager owns:

- **Record it, with dates and renewals.** Competence expires — through
  regulation, through equipment change, and through simple disuse.
- **Assess it, do not assume it.** Watch the task performed. Ten minutes of
  observation beats any certificate for the question you actually have.
- **Match it to the specific job.** Fifteen years of experience on one system
  is not competence on a different one, and long service can make that harder
  to say out loud.
- **New and young workers, and contractors, need it most** and are asked
  about it least. A contractor's competence is your risk when they work on
  your site.

## Permits, isolation and lone working

For higher-risk work — live electrical, confined space, hot work, height,
excavation near services — a **permit to work** does something a method
statement cannot: it forces a person with authority to confirm, at the moment
of the work, that the specific controls are in place, and it fixes a start
and an end.

Permits fail in two directions. Issued too freely they become a signature
ritual, and the signature is then worth nothing. Issued too restrictively
they get bypassed, because the job still has to happen and the permit desk
closes at five. Both failures are design failures rather than discipline
failures.

**Isolation** deserves its own discipline: energy sources locked off, keys
held by the person at risk, tested dead before touched. The rule that saves
lives is that the person exposed holds their own isolation — not a
colleague, not a supervisor.

**Lone working** removes the control nobody lists: another person noticing.
If lone work is genuinely necessary, replace that control explicitly —
check-in times, a person who will act if a check-in is missed, and a defined
response. A check-in scheme nobody monitors is worse than none, because it
produces confidence without cover.

## Stop-work authority, and whether you mean it

Every organisation says people can stop unsafe work. Very few make it true,
and the gap is visible in one question: *what happened the last time somebody
did?*

For it to be real:

- **State it explicitly and by name.** "You can stop this job, and I will
  back you" — said to individuals, not written in a policy.
- **Back the first person who uses it, publicly, even when they were wrong.**
  This is the whole thing. A person who stops a job unnecessarily and is
  supported has cost you an hour. A person who stops a job and is
  second-guessed has ended the practice for the entire team, permanently.
- **Never punish the delay.** If stopping is followed by questions about the
  schedule, the message received is the opposite of the message sent.
- **Fix what caused the stop.** A stop that changes nothing teaches people
  that stopping achieves nothing.

## When the schedule becomes the hazard

The most common root cause behind unsafe work is not attitude. It is a plan
in which the safe method does not fit — and the crew is then asked to hold
both, which is not possible.

The signatures: routine overtime to hit installation targets, jobs scheduled
back-to-back with no travel allowance, a fixed daily quota regardless of job
complexity, and the phrase *"just get it done today"* from someone who has
not seen the site.

This is a management control failure, and it is one you own personally. The
Team Leadership capacity arithmetic applies directly: a team committed beyond
its real capacity will find the time somewhere, and the safe method — which
is nearly always the slower one — is where they find it. **Nobody decides to
work unsafely. They decide to hit the target, and the method is what gives.**

## Do this at work

1. **Ask someone to talk you through a method without the document.** What
   they say is your real system of work.
2. **Cut one method statement to a page.** If it will not fit, the job may
   need splitting rather than the document shrinking.
3. **Observe one task being performed.** Ten minutes. It answers the
   competence question better than any certificate.
4. **Check renewals.** Which competences on your team have expired or will
   within three months? Diarise them now.
5. **Ask a contractor how they will do the job**, before they start, on your
   site. Their competence is your risk.
6. **Say the stop-work sentence out loud** to each person, by name, this
   week — and decide in advance how you will react the first time it is used.
7. **Look at the schedule as a safety document.** Where does the safe method
   not fit in the time allowed? That is a hazard with your name on it.

## Summary

- A safe system of work must be short, written with people who do the job,
  honest about bad conditions, and physically where the work is.
- Competence is knowledge, skill, experience and judgement — "attended a
  course" is none of them by itself. Assess it, record it, renew it, and
  match it to the specific job.
- Permits force confirmation at the moment of work; they fail by becoming a
  ritual or by being bypassed, and both are design failures.
- The person exposed holds their own isolation.
- Lone working removes the control nobody lists — another person noticing —
  so replace it explicitly, and never run a check-in nobody monitors.
- Stop-work authority becomes real the first time someone uses it and is
  backed publicly, even when they were wrong.
- A schedule in which the safe method does not fit is a management control
  failure: nobody decides to work unsafely, they decide to hit the target.
