# The Hierarchy of Controls

When managers reach for a control, they overwhelmingly reach for the two
weakest ones: a rule and some equipment. Both are cheap, fast, and visible —
and both put the entire burden of the control on the person at the sharp end,
every single time they do the job. The hierarchy is a ranking of controls by
how much they depend on human compliance, and using it in order is the single
biggest improvement available to most operations.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Rank the five levels and say what makes each stronger or weaker.
- Explain why PPE is last rather than first.
- Apply the hierarchy to a real hazard and generate options above the one you
  reached for.
- Combine controls without assuming they add up.
- Recognise a control that has quietly stopped working.

## The five levels

| | Control | What it does | Why it sits here |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Eliminate** | Remove the hazard entirely | Nothing to comply with. Works while everyone is tired, rushed and new. |
| 2 | **Substitute** | Replace it with something less dangerous | The hazard is smaller for everyone, permanently |
| 3 | **Engineer** | Guard, isolate, ventilate, interlock | Built into the equipment; works whether or not anyone remembers |
| 4 | **Administrative** | Procedures, training, signage, permits, rotation | Depends on people knowing, remembering and choosing to comply |
| 5 | **PPE** | Helmets, harnesses, gloves, respirators | Depends on correct selection, fit, condition and use — every time |

The ordering has one logic: **how much does this control depend on a human
doing something right, under pressure, on a bad day?** Level 1 depends on
nobody. Level 5 depends on one person, continuously, and fails silently when
they are wrong.

## Why PPE is last

PPE is not bad — it is often necessary, and sometimes it is genuinely the only
thing available. It is *last* because of what it does and does not do:

- **It protects one person, not the situation.** The hazard is entirely
  present; PPE only changes the outcome for whoever is wearing it correctly.
- **It fails silently.** A worn harness, a cracked lens, the wrong respirator
  cartridge, a helmet past its date — all look like protection right up to
  the moment they are not.
- **It depends on fit and discipline every time.** Gloves off for the fiddly
  bit, glasses pushed up in the heat, hearing protection out to hear a
  colleague. These are not failures of character; they are what happens when
  a control competes with doing the job.
- **It transfers the burden to the individual**, which is also why it is the
  cheapest and quickest to implement — and why it is over-reached-for.

The test worth applying: *if this person forgets, is distracted, or is new —
what happens?* If the answer is "serious harm", the control is too low on the
hierarchy.

## Worked example: one hazard, five ways

Cutting fibre-duct trenching material on site produces dust and blade injury
risk. A manager's instinct is masks and gloves. The hierarchy generates
options nobody was discussing:

| Level | Option | What it costs | What it buys |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · Eliminate | Order pre-cut lengths from the supplier | A small price premium; longer lead times | **The hazard is not on site at all** |
| 2 · Substitute | A material that scores and snaps rather than needing a saw | Procurement change | No blade, far less dust |
| 3 · Engineer | Bench-mounted saw with guard and on-tool extraction | ₦180,000 once | Works for everyone, including the new starter, forever |
| 4 · Administrative | Cutting only in a marked zone, trained operators, permit | Training and supervision time | Helps — while people remember and supervision is present |
| 5 · PPE | Masks and gloves | Low, recurring | Protects the wearer, correctly fitted, every time |

Read the right-hand column. Level 1 is the only row where the control cannot
be forgotten, defeated or worn out — and it is procurement, not safety, which
is why it never comes up in a safety meeting.

That is the practical point of the hierarchy: **it is a prompt for options,
not a scoring system.** Most teams generate levels 4 and 5 unaided and need
the framework to see 1, 2 and 3 at all.

## Combining controls, honestly

Real jobs use several levels together, and that is right. Two cautions:

**Controls do not add up.** Three administrative controls are not equal to
one engineering control, because they share a failure mode — all three depend
on the same person remembering under the same pressure. Diversity of control
type matters more than quantity, exactly as the Data course's paired guard
metrics matter more than more metrics.

**Every control has an owner and a check.** A guard nobody inspects, a permit
system nobody audits, a training record nobody renews — these decay into
paperwork while appearing on the assessment as live controls. The residual
risk in Chapter 2 is calculated on controls that exist *today*, which means
someone must look.

## Controls that quietly stopped working

The failure is rarely dramatic. Watch for:

- **The defeated interlock.** Taped, wedged or bypassed — always because it
  made the job slower, which is a design problem before it is a discipline
  problem.
- **The missing consumable.** Extraction with no filter, a first-aid kit
  half-empty, the spill kit used and never restocked.
- **The training that expired.** Competence has a date; nobody is notified.
- **The procedure overtaken by the work.** The method changed and the control
  did not, so following the procedure is now impossible and everybody has
  quietly agreed not to.
- **The control that is present but unusable** — the harness anchor twelve
  metres from where the work is.

Each of these is discoverable by walking the job and asking one question:
*show me how you actually do this.* Not "do you follow the procedure?", which
has only one answer.

## Do this at work

1. **Take one hazard you currently manage with PPE** and generate options at
   levels 1–3. Most teams find at least one they had never discussed.
2. **Ask procurement a safety question.** Pre-cut, pre-assembled, delivered
   differently, a less hazardous material. Level 1 usually lives outside the
   safety conversation.
3. **Apply the forgetting test** to three controls: if this person forgets,
   is distracted, or is new — what happens?
4. **Find a defeated control.** Taped, wedged, bypassed. Ask why it made the
   job slower before asking who did it.
5. **Give every control an owner and a check date.** Guards, permits,
   training, consumables. Unowned controls decay into paperwork.
6. **Count your control types on one job.** If they are all level 4 and 5,
   you have quantity without diversity, and they share a failure mode.
7. **Ask "show me how you actually do this."** On one task, this week. The
   gap between that and the procedure is your real control position.

## Summary

- The hierarchy ranks controls by how little they depend on a human doing
  something right under pressure: eliminate, substitute, engineer,
  administrative, PPE.
- PPE is last because it protects one person rather than the situation, fails
  silently, and depends on correct use every single time.
- Use the hierarchy as a prompt for options — teams generate levels 4 and 5
  unaided and need the framework to see 1, 2 and 3.
- Elimination often lives in procurement or scheduling rather than in safety,
  which is why it rarely comes up in a safety meeting.
- Controls do not add up: three administrative controls share one failure
  mode. Diversity of type beats quantity.
- Every control needs an owner and a check, or it decays into paperwork while
  still appearing live on the assessment.
