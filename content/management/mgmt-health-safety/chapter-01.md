# Why Safety Is a Management Job

Safety is usually presented to managers as a compliance obligation — posters,
forms, an annual briefing nobody remembers. That framing is why so much of it
fails. Safety is a management job in exactly the same sense that quality and
cost are management jobs: it is produced by how work is organised, and it
degrades quietly when nobody is watching the right things. This chapter is
about what a manager actually owns.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Say why safety outcomes are produced by systems rather than by care.
- Distinguish a hazard from a risk, and explain why the difference decides
  what you do next.
- Explain what "so far as is reasonably practicable" asks of you.
- Read the relationship between near misses and serious harm.
- Name what a manager personally owns, and what cannot be delegated.

## Care is not a control

The most common response to an incident is "be more careful." It is
universally understood, universally issued, and close to useless — because it
asks a person to supply, continuously and under pressure, what the system
failed to supply once.

Careful people get hurt. They get hurt when a guard is missing, when the
right ladder is on another site, when the shortcut saves forty minutes and
everyone takes it, when the job was scheduled so tightly that stopping felt
impossible. In each case the individual's care was the *only* control, and
individual care is the least reliable control there is: it varies with
fatigue, weather, deadline, mood and experience.

This is the same insight the Data course applies to drift and the Decision
Making course applies to root cause. **If your safety system depends on
everyone doing the right thing every time, it has already failed — you simply
have not met the day it fails on yet.**

## Hazard and risk are not the same word

- A **hazard** is something with the potential to cause harm: electricity,
  a height, a moving vehicle, a chemical, a fatigued driver.
- A **risk** is the *likelihood* of that harm occurring and *how bad* it
  would be.

The distinction is not pedantry — it decides what you do. You cannot remove
electricity from an electrical job, so you manage the risk. You *can* often
remove a hazard entirely: do the work at ground level instead of at height,
buy the pre-cut material instead of cutting it on site, schedule the visit in
daylight. Eliminating a hazard is worth more than any amount of managing the
risk it creates, and it is the option people skip fastest because it usually
requires changing the plan rather than issuing equipment.

Hold that order in mind; Chapter 3 makes it a hierarchy.

## Reasonably practicable

Most safety law does not demand absolute safety — it demands what is
**reasonably practicable**, which is a genuine test rather than a loophole:
weigh the risk against the time, cost and trouble of controlling it, and if
the control is not grossly disproportionate to the risk, do it.

Two things follow that managers routinely get wrong.

**Cost is a legitimate consideration, but only against the risk.** "It was
too expensive" is an argument that must be made in proportion. Against a
trivial risk it can succeed; against a foreseeable fatality it will not, in
any jurisdiction, at any price you were likely to have saved.

**The test is applied with hindsight, by someone reading your records.** The
question after an incident is not whether you meant well; it is what a
reasonable manager would have done, and what you can show you did. Which
makes the boring documentation of Chapter 2 the thing that protects you.

## The pyramid, and why near misses matter

For every incident causing serious harm, there are many minor injuries, more
near misses, and more unsafe conditions still. The exact ratios vary by
industry and are argued over; the shape does not. Serious harm sits on a base
of smaller events that share its causes.

That gives you something valuable: **near misses are free information about
the incident you have not had yet.** The scaffold that shifted but held, the
vehicle that stopped in time, the live circuit found by luck — each is the
same system failure as the serious version, arriving without the cost.

Which is why the single most damaging thing a manager can do is make
reporting expensive. If a near miss produces blame, paperwork, or a
reputation, people stop reporting them — and the flow of free information
stops while the underlying rate does not. **A rising near-miss count in a
team that has just been told reporting is safe is good news**, and a manager
who reads it as deterioration will destroy the thing they just built.

## What a manager personally owns

You are not expected to be a safety professional. You are expected to own:

| You own | In practice |
| --- | --- |
| **The conditions** | Equipment maintained, guards fitted, access safe, the right tools present |
| **The plan** | Enough time and people that the safe method is the practical one |
| **Competence** | Nobody does a task they have not been trained and assessed for |
| **The standard you enforce** | What you walk past is what you accept — and the team reads that, not the policy |
| **The escalation** | Raising what you cannot fix yourself, in writing, and chasing it |

The last two are where managers most often fall down, and neither can be
delegated. A rule you do not enforce is not a rule; it is a document. And a
hazard you reported once, verbally, to someone who forgot, is a hazard you
still own.

## Worked example: what one preventable incident costs

An unguarded machine causes an injury. Twelve days of disruption follow.

| | Cost |
| --- | ---: |
| Medical, repair, replacement | ₦450,000 |
| Investigation — 30 hours of management time | ₦72,000 |
| Cover for the injured worker, 12 days | ₦230,400 |
| Regulatory penalty | ₦1,500,000 |
| **Direct total** | **₦2,252,400** |

Now the prevention that would most likely have stopped it:

| | Annual cost |
| --- | ---: |
| Fitting guards | ₦180,000 |
| Training, 4 hours × 12 people | ₦115,200 |
| Monthly inspection, 2 hours | ₦57,600 |
| **Total** | **₦352,800** |

**The incident costs about 6.4 times a full year of the prevention** — and
that comparison excludes the largest financial item entirely. Twelve days of
lost operating capacity, at the ₦270,000 a day used elsewhere in this
curriculum, is a further **₦3,240,000**, which would take the ratio past 15.

Two honest qualifications. Penalties vary enormously by jurisdiction and by
what the investigation finds, so treat that line as illustrative rather than
predictive. And prevention spending does not buy certainty — it buys a lower
probability, which means the honest comparison is between a certain ₦352,800
and a probability-weighted ₦5.5m, not between two certainties.

The argument survives both, because the asymmetry is so large. But the
strongest argument is not on this table at all: **somebody was injured**, and
no ratio makes that a line item.

## Do this at work

1. **Walk your area looking only for hazards.** Thirty minutes, nothing else
   on your mind. Write what you find — the list is always longer than
   expected, and the act of writing is what converts noticing into owning.
2. **Find one hazard you can eliminate rather than control.** Work done at
   ground level, material pre-cut, a visit moved to daylight. Elimination
   beats every control that follows it.
3. **Check what you walked past last week.** Something you saw and did not
   stop. That is your actual standard, whatever the policy says.
4. **Ask for near misses, and react well to the first one.** How you respond
   the first time decides whether there is a second report.
5. **Check one person's competence for one task.** Trained, assessed, and
   recorded — or assumed?
6. **Escalate one thing in writing today.** Something you have raised
   verbally and nothing happened. Verbal reports that were forgotten remain
   yours.
7. **Cost one plausible incident** for your area, against what prevention
   would cost annually. Take it to whoever declines the prevention budget.

## Summary

- Safety is produced by systems, not by care; if the system needs everyone
  to do the right thing every time, it has already failed.
- A hazard is the potential for harm; a risk is how likely and how bad.
  Eliminating a hazard beats managing the risk it creates.
- "Reasonably practicable" weighs the control against the risk, is judged
  with hindsight, and is judged from your records.
- Near misses are free information about the incident you have not had yet —
  so never make reporting them expensive, and read a rising count correctly.
- A manager owns conditions, plan, competence, the standard they enforce,
  and the escalation. The last two cannot be delegated.
- One preventable incident cost ₦2.25m against ₦352,800 of annual
  prevention, before ₦3.24m of lost operating days — and the person injured
  is not on the table.
