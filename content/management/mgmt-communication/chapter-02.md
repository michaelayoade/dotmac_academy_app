# Written Reports That Get Read

Most workplace reports are written to be *sent*, not to be *read* — long,
chronological, and organised around the writer's effort rather than the
reader's decision. Then their authors wonder why nothing happens. Writing
that gets read and acted on follows learnable mechanics, and they are
almost the opposite of school habits: conclusion first, shortest workable
length, structure the reader can raid. This chapter covers those mechanics
for the reports managers actually write — status updates, operational
reports, incident summaries, and proposals.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Lead with the outcome instead of the chronology.
- Use the three standard skeletons for status, incident and proposal.
- Label a request so it cannot be skimmed past.
- Write for the reader who will forward it without context.
- Make your message easy to answer.

## Lead with the outcome

The single highest-value habit in professional writing: **put the
conclusion in the first sentence, not the last.** The reader's first
question is always "what happened?" or "what do you want?" — answer it
immediately, then support it.

- School order: background → analysis → evidence → conclusion.
- Work order: **conclusion → what it means → key support → detail for
  those who want it.**

This inverted shape respects how reports are actually consumed: everyone
reads the first lines; some read the middle; almost no one reads to the
end. Structure for that reality and every class of reader gets served. A
useful test before sending: if the reader stopped after your first two
sentences, would they know the most important thing? If not, the most
important thing is in the wrong place.

The same rule scales down to subject lines and message openers: "Generator
replaced — site back to normal" beats "Update regarding the site issue" in
every world.

## The standard skeletons

Three report types cover most of a manager's writing, and each has a
skeleton worth memorising:

- **Status report.** Overall state in one line (on track / at risk /
  blocked — commit to one), then: what was done, what's next, risks and
  blockers with what you need, and any decision required *clearly flagged*.
  Never bury a request for help in paragraph four; buried requests license
  the reader to miss them.
- **Incident/operational report.** What happened and current impact, in
  plain terms, first. Then timeline, cause as far as known (say plainly
  what is confirmed versus suspected — the observation/inference
  discipline from the Decision Making course), what was done, what happens
  next and when the reader hears more. Written calm is contagious exactly
  like spoken calm.
- **Proposal.** The business-case shape from the Finance course:
  recommendation first, then problem, options, costs, returns, risks.
  One page wherever possible — a proposal that can't summarise itself on
  one page usually isn't ready.

### Worked example: the same update, buried and led

A real status update, written the way most are:

> Hi all — hope the week is going well. Following on from the site visit on
> Tuesday, the team completed the survey work and we've been working through
> the readings with the contractor. There were a few complications with
> access on the north side which took longer than expected, and we've also
> been chasing the supplier about the delivery schedule. Amaka has done a
> great job keeping the documentation up to date throughout. On the timeline,
> we're still hopeful about the end of the month, although this does depend
> on whether we can get the revised permit signed off, and it would be
> helpful to know if anyone can assist with that as the office has not
> responded to two emails.

Everything a reader needs is in there. The problem is *where*: the project is
at risk, and the sentence saying so is the second-to-last clause of a
paragraph, after the thanks and the survey detail. A busy reader stops at
"hope the week is going well" and files it as fine.

The same content, led:

> **Status: AT RISK.** End-of-month delivery depends on a permit that has not
> been signed off.
>
> **What I need:** someone with a contact in the permits office. Two emails
> unanswered since the 4th. If it is not moving by Friday the date slips by
> about two weeks.
>
> **Done:** site survey complete; readings reconciled with the contractor.
> **Next:** finalise the cable schedule; confirm the delivery date.
> **Note:** north-side access took three days longer than planned — worth
> allowing for on the next site. Amaka's documentation has been consistently
> good.

Same facts, same length, roughly. Three differences that matter:

| | Buried version | Led version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to learn the project is at risk | Read the whole paragraph | First three words |
| The request for help | A clause near the end | Its own labelled block, with a date and a consequence |
| What a skim-reader takes away | "Things are progressing" | "At risk, needs a permit contact" |

The buried version is not badly written — it is written in the order the
author *experienced* the week. The led version is written in the order the
reader needs it, which is almost always the reverse: **conclusion, request,
then supporting detail.**

Note especially what happened to the request. Buried, it is phrased as "it
would be helpful to know if anyone can assist" — which asks nobody for
anything and gives no deadline, so nobody acts and the author can honestly
say they raised it. Led, it names what is needed, by when, and what happens
otherwise. A buried request licenses the reader to miss it; a labelled
request with a consequence is much harder to leave alone.

## Brevity is a service, not a shortcut

Short does not mean thin; it means **selected**. The discipline is
deciding what the reader's decision actually depends on, including that,
and cutting the rest — the history of your effort, the six options you
rejected early, the details that change nothing. Three edits that shorten
without starving:

- **Cut the throat-clearing.** Openings like "I am writing to provide an
  update regarding…" say nothing; start where the content starts.
- **One idea per paragraph, and front-load each.** Readers skim first
  lines of paragraphs — make those lines carry the skeleton of your
  argument.
- **Convert prose to structure where the content is list-shaped.** Three
  risks belong as three bullets, not a paragraph hiding three commas. But
  resist bulleting *everything*: bullets carry items; prose carries
  reasoning. A report that is all bullets has usually stopped explaining.

And numbers: report them with their meaning attached. "Downtime doubled
versus last month" informs; a bare figure makes the reader do the analysis
you were paid to do. (The Managing with Data course returns to honest
number-presentation in depth.)

## Write for the forwarded reader

Every work document has two audiences: the person you sent it to, and the
unknown person it gets forwarded to — their boss, another team, a file
opened in six months. Write so the forwarded reader survives: spell out
context a stranger needs ("the Kano installation project", not "the
project"), expand abbreviations on first use, and never rely on "as we
discussed" carrying load-bearing content. This is also the honesty check:
a report you'd be uncomfortable seeing forwarded is usually shading the
truth somewhere — per the Team Leadership rule, report failures plainly
with what you're doing about them. A reputation for unvarnished reports is
a career asset that compounds; the first discovered varnish cancels it.

## Make it easy to answer

End every report that needs something with a **clearly separated ask**:
what you need, from whom, by when. One ask per person where possible.
"Please advise" is not an ask; "Can you approve the ₦-level spend by
Thursday so the order ships this week?" is. If the report needs nothing,
say that too — "no action needed, for your awareness" — so the reader can
file it with a clear conscience. Reports whose purpose is ambiguous get
the worst of both worlds: no action *and* a vague sense you're generating
paperwork.

## Do this at work

1. **Rewrite one update, led.** Status in the first line, request in its own
   block with a date and a consequence, detail underneath.
2. **Find a buried request.** Look for something you asked for in the middle
   of a paragraph and never got. Re-send it labelled, with a deadline.
3. **Commit to one status word.** On track, at risk, or blocked — pick one.
   "Mostly on track with some challenges" is a way of not saying at risk.
4. **Test the forwarded reader.** Take your last long message and ask whether
   someone forwarded it with no context would understand the ask.
5. **Cut one message by a third.** Same content, fewer words. Brevity is what
   you do so the reader does not have to.
6. **Make one reply a yes/no.** Where you need a decision, offer the specific
   thing to approve rather than an open question about what to do.

## Summary

- Conclusion first, always: work order is conclusion → meaning → support
  → detail, and the two-sentence test tells you if you've honoured it.
- Use the skeletons: status (state, done, next, risks, asks flagged),
  incident (impact first, confirmed vs suspected, next update time),
  proposal (recommendation first, one page).
- Brevity is selection, not compression: cut throat-clearing, front-load
  paragraphs, structure list-shaped content — but keep prose for
  reasoning.
- Write for the forwarded reader: context a stranger needs, no varnish
  you'd regret.
- End with a separated, specific, dated ask — or an explicit "no action
  needed."
