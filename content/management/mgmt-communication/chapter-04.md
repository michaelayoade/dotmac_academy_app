# Running Effective Meetings

Meetings are the most expensive communication channel a business owns —
six people for an hour is nearly a person-day of capacity — and the only
channel routinely spent without anyone checking the price. Run well,
meetings do what no document can: align understanding, resolve
disagreement, and commit people to action in real time. Run by default,
they are where working hours go to die. This chapter is about paying the
price only when the purchase is worth it, and collecting the full value
when you do.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Decide whether a meeting should exist at all.
- Put a cost on a recurring meeting and price the fixes.
- Write an agenda that functions as a contract.
- Chair with a light but real hand.
- Close the loop in writing so the meeting produces something.

## First: should this meeting exist?

A meeting is justified when the work needs *interaction* — decisions with
discussion, genuine problem-solving, alignment where nuance and pushback
matter, or human moments (welcome, recognition, hard news). It is not
justified for transmitting information one direction: that's a document
or a message, which people consume in minutes at their own pace. The
one-way "update meeting" where each person recites what a shared page
already says is the single largest meeting waste in most organisations —
replace it with the written status skeleton from Chapter 2 and keep a
short live session only for the parts that need discussion.

Two structural prunes worth running quarterly:

- **Audit the standing meetings.** Recurring meetings outlive their
  purpose because cancelling feels rude. For each: what decision or
  output did the last three produce? None → kill it, shorten it, or
  halve its frequency, and see what breaks. Usually: nothing. (The
  Decision Making course's zero-consumer logic applies to meetings too.)
- **Audit the invite lists.** Every attendee should be there to decide,
  contribute, or commit. "Kept in the loop" is what minutes are for.
  Fewer people means faster meetings *and* franker ones.

### Worked example: what a standing meeting costs

Meetings feel free because nobody invoices for them. Put a number on one —
eight people, one hour, every week, at ₦2,400 an hour of loaded labour:

| | Per week | Per year (48 weeks) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| The hour itself (8 × 1h) | ₦19,200 | ₦921,600 |
| **With 15 minutes of context-switching each side** (8 × 1.5h) | **₦28,800** | **₦1,382,400** |

**A recurring one-hour meeting of eight people costs about ₦1.4 million a
year.** Nobody would approve that as a line item without asking what it
produces — and yet almost no organisation asks that of the meeting.

The second row is the one people forget. An hour in the middle of a morning
does not cost an hour; it costs the hour plus the settling either side, which
is why three scattered meetings can consume a day that shows nothing for it.

Now price the fixes, against that ₦1,382,400 baseline:

| Change | Annual cost | Saved |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Trim the invite list to the 4 who decide or contribute | ₦691,200 | **₦691,200** |
| Make it fortnightly | ₦691,200 | **₦691,200** |
| Cut it to 30 minutes | ₦921,600 | ₦460,800 |

Each is a single decision, taken once, that returns money every week
thereafter. Compare that to the effort of finding ₦691,200 of savings
anywhere else in a budget.

Two honest caveats. This is not cash — those hours are salaried whether or
not the meeting happens, so the saving is *capacity*, not money in the bank;
it only becomes real if the freed time goes somewhere useful. And some
meetings are worth far more than they cost: a decision made together in forty
minutes can be worth many times ₦1.4m, and the human moments have a value no
table captures. The point is not that meetings are expensive. It is that a
meeting nobody has ever priced is being renewed indefinitely on the assumption
that it is free.

## Before: the agenda is a contract

A meeting without an agenda is an ambush with refreshments. The agenda —
even three lines in the invitation — states what the meeting is for, what
will be decided, and what attendees should bring or pre-read. Phrase
items as outcomes, not nouns: "decide vendor for the generator contract"
tells everyone what done looks like; "generators" tells them nothing.

For decision meetings, the framing note from the Decision Making course
is the gold standard: the decision, the criteria, the options, a
recommendation — circulated before, so the room argues substance instead
of reconstructing context. Meetings are for *deciding together*, not
*reading together*.

## During: chair with a light but real hand

Chairing is a role, not a personality trait, and it has four jobs:

- **Start on time, state the goal.** Starting late taxes the punctual to
  subsidise the late, and trains next week's lateness. One sentence
  resets everyone: "We're here to decide X; we have forty minutes."
- **Keep the thread.** When discussion wanders, name it and route it:
  "that's real, but it's a different topic — parking it for follow-up,
  back to X." A visible parking list makes redirection feel like filing,
  not dismissal.
- **Balance the voices.** The loudest voice is not the best-informed one
  — invite the quiet expertise in ("Ada, you run this process — what are
  we missing?"), and use the silent-parallel-input technique from the
  Decision Making course when the topic is contested or the room is
  hierarchical. If you're senior, speak last; the chapter on group
  decisions explains why.
- **Land the decisions.** Blur is the default outcome of discussion;
  the chair's job is to force the sentence: "So we're deciding Y — any
  objection? Decided." Timebox agenda items and, when time expires,
  decide, explicitly defer, or explicitly escalate — never just drift on.

## After: the meeting isn't over until it's written

The value of a meeting is what happens afterwards, and that survives only
if captured. Within the day — sooner is better — send the record. Not a
transcript; three short lists:

- **Decisions made** (feeding the decision log from the Decision Making
  course where significant).
- **Actions** — each with one owner and a date. "The team will look
  into it" is Team Leadership's no-owner anti-pattern wearing meeting
  clothes; convert every action to who-what-when before it leaves the
  room, then write down what was converted.
- **Parked items** — what was raised and deferred, so raisers know they
  were heard and the items have somewhere to live.

Then the habit that separates teams that execute from teams that meet:
**open the next meeting with the last meeting's actions.** Thirty seconds
per item: done, not done and why, new date. Actions that are never
revisited teach everyone that meeting commitments are decorative — after
which no meeting in that room produces action again.

## Meetings you attend but don't run

You spend more time in other people's meetings than your own. Contribute
to the same standard: come prepared (read the pre-read — the meeting
where nobody has is the meeting that reads together), speak to move the
item ("proposal:", "risk:", "question:"), take your actions with dates,
and deliver them. And when a meeting you attend chronically wastes its
room, say so kindly to its owner with a suggestion — most bad meetings
have simply never been questioned. Modelling the standard is how it
spreads; meeting culture is set by what the room's managers visibly do.

## Do this at work

1. **Price one standing meeting.** Attendees × duration × an hourly rate ×
   the weeks per year. Then ask what the last three produced.
2. **Trim one invite list.** Everyone should be there to decide, contribute,
   or commit. "Kept in the loop" is what the notes are for.
3. **Halve one meeting.** Frequency or duration. See what breaks — usually
   nothing, and you have the number showing what it returned.
4. **Send the document before the meeting.** For your next complex item.
   Reading time beforehand converts presentation into discussion.
5. **Write one agenda as a contract.** Each item with a purpose — decide,
   discuss, or inform — and a time. Items without a purpose get dropped.
6. **Close one loop.** Send the decisions and actions within the day: who,
   what, by when. A meeting nobody wrote down did not happen.

## Summary

- Meetings buy interaction — decisions, problem-solving, alignment,
  human moments. Information transfer is a document; audit standing
  meetings and invite lists ruthlessly.
- The agenda is a contract: outcome-phrased items, pre-reads circulated,
  decision framing in advance.
- Chair actively: start on time, keep the thread with a parking list,
  balance voices (seniors last), and force the decision sentence before
  the timebox dies.
- Send the record same-day: decisions, owned-and-dated actions, parked
  items — then open the next meeting with the last one's actions.
- In others' meetings, model the standard: prepared, purposeful,
  actions delivered.
