# Communicating as a Manager

The day you become a manager, your words change weight. A stray remark
becomes "management said"; a vague instruction becomes a week of wrong work;
silence becomes a message you didn't intend to send. Most communication
failures at work are not eloquence failures — they are targeting failures:
the right message shaped for the wrong audience, sent through the wrong
channel, or never sent at all. This chapter builds the manager's habit of
choosing audience, message, and channel deliberately.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Start from what the audience needs rather than what you want to say.
- Adapt to the three directions — up, down, sideways — and their different
  disciplines.
- Choose a channel deliberately, and say why written or spoken fits.
- Recognise that you now speak for the team, not only for yourself.
- Read silence as the message it is.

## Start from the audience, not the message

The amateur asks "what do I want to say?"; the professional asks "**what
does this audience need, and what should they do after hearing it?**" Before
any significant communication — a briefing, an announcement, a report —
answer three questions:

- **What do they already know?** Explaining context they have wastes their
  attention; skipping context they lack loses them at sentence one. The
  most common miss is the curse of knowledge: you've lived the details for
  weeks, and forget the listener hasn't.
- **What do they care about?** The same project update is a budget story
  to your director, a workload story to your team, and a dependency story
  to the neighbouring department. The facts don't change; the *framing*
  must.
- **What should happen next?** Communication that assigns no action and
  changes no understanding is noise. If you can't name what the audience
  should do or know afterwards, you're not ready to send it.

## Three directions, three disciplines

A manager communicates in three directions, and each has its own failure
modes:

- **Downward (your team).** The job is clarity and repetition. Instructions
  need what-why-when — what's needed, why it matters, when it's due — and
  important messages need saying more than once, through more than one
  channel, because attention is uneven. The classic downward failure is
  assuming one announcement equals understanding; the test is not "did I
  say it?" but "can they say it back?" (The delegation playback from the
  Team Leadership course is this test in miniature.)
- **Upward (your manager).** The job is honest compression: what's on
  track, what's at risk, what you need. Chapter 3 covers the craft in
  depth; the principle here is that upward communication is judged on
  *reliability* — leaders quickly learn whose reports can be acted on
  without double-checking, and that reputation is worth more than
  polish.
- **Lateral (peers and other teams).** The job is negotiation between
  equals: no authority to instruct, so clarity about requests, commitments,
  and dates does the work authority would. Most cross-team friction is two
  teams each certain they told the other something that was never actually
  agreed. Write down lateral agreements — informally is fine — because
  neither side owns the other's memory.

## You now speak for the team

Two identity shifts come with the role. First, **outward, you are the
team's voice**: you present their work, their constraints, and their needs
in rooms they're not in. Represent accurately — inflating their capacity
wins praise today and impossible commitments tomorrow; underselling them
costs opportunities they earned.

Second, **inward, you are the company's voice**: decisions from above reach
your team through you. Deliver them like an owner, not a bystander.
"Management wants us to…" (eye-roll included) is the abdication pattern —
it buys cheap solidarity by spending the decision's credibility. If you
disagreed, you argued upstream; once decided, you explain the decision with
its honest reasoning — the disagree-and-commit discipline from the Decision
Making course, applied at the microphone. And when you *don't* know the
reasoning, say "I'll find out" — inventing justifications you don't believe
reads as exactly what it is.

## Choose the channel like it matters — because it does

Every channel trades richness against reach and record:

- **Face-to-face (or a call)** carries tone, allows questions, and defuses
  ambiguity — use it for anything sensitive, complex, or likely to be
  misread: feedback, bad news, conflict, big changes. Chapter 5 lives
  entirely here.
- **Written** (messages, email, documents) creates a record, travels
  faithfully, and can be re-read — use it for instructions, agreements,
  specifications, and anything that must survive memory. The rule pair:
  **sensitive → spoken first; binding → written always.** Spoken
  agreements about money, dates, or scope don't exist until written down.
- **Broadcast channels** (group chats, notice boards, all-hands) reach
  everyone and confirm nothing — fine for awareness, wrong for anything
  requiring individual acknowledgement. "I posted it in the group" is not
  delivery, as the handover discipline in Team Leadership already
  established.

The compound move for anything important: **say it, then write it.** The
conversation carries the meaning; the written follow-up ("as discussed:
X by Friday, you'll handle Y") carries the record.

As a decision table, when you are not sure which to reach for:

| The message is… | Channel | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sensitive, personal, or likely to be misread | Spoken, privately — then a short written note | Tone survives; the record follows |
| Binding: money, dates, scope, commitments | Written, always | Spoken agreements about these do not exist |
| A simple fact people need to know | Written, once, where they look | A meeting for this wastes everyone's hour |
| Complex and needs pushback to get right | Spoken, with the document sent first | Reading time before discussion doubles its value |
| Bad news | Spoken, early, to those affected first | Hearing it second-hand is a separate injury |
| Routine coordination | Written, in the shared place | Keeps the record where the next person will look |
| Recognition | Spoken, in front of people who matter to them | Written praise is pleasant; public praise compounds |

The row that costs organisations most is the fourth. Sending a complex
document *with* the meeting invitation, rather than presenting it live,
converts an hour of people reading silently at you into an hour of people
arguing with what they already understand. It also surfaces the objection
that would otherwise arrive a week later.

The row people get wrong most often is the first. The instinct under
discomfort is to write, because writing avoids the reaction — and writing is
precisely what strips the tone that would have made it survivable.

## Silence is also a message

Whatever you don't address, the team reads anyway — usually less
charitably than the truth. Unexplained decisions read as arbitrary
(Team Leadership's "invisible managing"); unacknowledged rumours read as
confirmation; ignored bad behaviour reads as approval. You cannot opt out
of communicating; you can only choose whether the message is deliberate.
When something is genuinely confidential, say *that* — "there's a decision
coming I can't discuss yet; you'll hear it first from me" beats silence,
because it replaces speculation with a promise you can keep.

## Do this at work

1. **Rewrite one message for its reader.** Take something you sent recently
   and rewrite it starting from what that person needed to know or do.
2. **Check your last sensitive message.** Was it written because writing was
   right, or because writing avoided the reaction? The instinct under
   discomfort is to type.
3. **Say it, then write it.** For your next important conversation, follow up
   within the hour with two lines: what was agreed, by when.
4. **Find one thing you posted and assumed was delivered.** Broadcast is
   awareness, not acknowledgement. Confirm individually where it mattered.
5. **Audit your silences.** Where has your team heard nothing for a while?
   That gap is being filled with a story, and it is rarely a generous one.

## Summary

- Design every significant communication from the audience backwards:
  what they know, what they care about, what should happen next.
- Downward needs clarity and repetition (test: can they say it back?);
  upward needs honest compression; lateral needs written agreements
  because authority can't settle disputes between equals.
- You are the team's voice outward — represent them accurately — and the
  company's voice inward: deliver decisions like an owner, never as
  "management wants."
- Sensitive → spoken first; binding → written always; important → say it,
  then write it. Broadcast reaches everyone and confirms nothing.
- Silence communicates too — replace unavoidable secrecy with a promise,
  not a vacuum.
