# Difficult Conversations

Every manager's calendar eventually contains a conversation they dread:
the underperformance talk, the two colleagues at war, the customer
escalation that reaches your desk still smoking. These conversations are
where management is actually earned — and they are governed by craft, not
courage. The craft is mostly preparation, sequence, and the discipline to
stay on the problem when everything in the room invites you onto the
person. This chapter covers the general method, then the three hard cases.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Apply one general method to any difficult conversation.
- Prepare and hold a performance conversation without it becoming a verdict.
- Mediate between two team members without taking a side prematurely.
- Handle an escalated customer so the relationship survives the complaint.
- Say the hard sentence plainly, early, and once.

## The general method

Four principles run through every difficult conversation:

- **Early beats polished.** The conversation you delay grows: the
  behaviour compounds, resentment accumulates on both sides, and the
  eventual talk must carry months of freight. A slightly clumsy
  conversation this week beats an eloquent one next quarter — Team
  Leadership's "what you tolerate becomes your standard" is the deadline.
- **Prepare the facts, not the script.** Bring specifics — dates, events,
  observable behaviour (the situation-behaviour-impact form from Team
  Leadership) — but don't memorise lines; scripts shatter on the first
  unexpected reply. Prepare instead the two anchors: the *issue* in one
  sentence, and the *outcome* you want from the meeting.
- **Private, face-to-face, unhurried.** Hard content deserves the richest
  channel (Chapter 1) and enough time that neither of you is watching the
  door. Never in front of the team; never by message; never as a drive-by.
- **Say it, then stop.** State the issue plainly in the first minute —
  soft-starting with ten minutes of pleasantries makes the pivot crueller,
  and burying the message in cushioning ("the sandwich") leaves people
  genuinely unsure what was said. Then *stop talking and listen*: half the
  time there is context that changes the picture, and all of the time
  people accept outcomes better when they were genuinely heard first —
  the same fairness mechanics as the Decision Making course's
  consultation rule.

Expect emotion — yours and theirs — and plan for it rather than around
it. If the room gets too hot to think, name it and pause: "this matters
and we're both worked up; let's take ten minutes." A paused conversation
resumes; a detonated one gets remembered.

## Case one: the performance conversation

Team Leadership covers the management system around underperformance —
diagnosis, plans, documentation. The *conversation* itself adds three
communication disciplines:

- **One issue, clearly owned.** Don't arrive with a grab-bag of six
  months of grievances; lead with the pattern that matters, evidenced.
  And own the message yourself — "I'm raising this" — never "people have
  been saying," which is unanswerable and reads as ambush.
- **Dialogue before prescription.** After the SBI statement, ask before
  telling: "what's your read on this?" The diagnosis conversation (skill,
  clarity, obstacles, will) only works as a conversation.
- **Land the agreement out loud, then in writing.** End with both of you
  saying the same sentence about what changes and by when — then the
  three-line written note. Difficult conversations have famously
  divergent memories; the note is kindness, not bureaucracy.

## Case two: conflict between team members

When two of your people are at war, your job is referee of the *working
relationship*, not judge of the *friendship*:

- **Don't absorb the triangle.** Each party lobbying you separately feels
  like managing; it's actually being the message-carrier in a proxy war.
  Hear each side once, separately, to understand — then bring them
  together, because the agreement has to be *theirs* to hold.
- **Structure the joint conversation.** Each states their view
  uninterrupted; each must then restate the *other's* view to the other's
  satisfaction before any solutions — this single mechanic dissolves more
  conflict than any ruling, because most workplace war is mutual
  misreading with logistics on top.
- **Steer to behaviour and future.** "Who was right in March" is
  unwinnable; "how do handovers between you work from Monday" is
  decidable. Close with specific working agreements, written, revisited
  in a fortnight.
- **Know the floor.** Disagreement is healthy; disrespect is not.
  Personal attacks, sabotage, or bad-faith conduct convert this from
  mediation to a performance conversation with the aggressor — held with
  the same early-and-evidenced discipline.

## Case three: the escalated customer

The Customer Experience course owns the complaint lifecycle; here, the
communication craft for the moment the furious escalation reaches *you*:

- **Your arrival changes the conversation — say so.** "I'm the manager;
  I own this now" satisfies half of what escalation was seeking: proof of
  being taken seriously. Then the sequence you already know: listen
  without interrupting, acknowledge the experience specifically, move to
  facts, commit to a named next step with a time.
- **Defend your staff without defending mistakes.** If your team member
  erred: "we got this wrong and here's what we'll do" — accountability
  without staff-blaming theatre. If the customer mistreated your staff:
  correct course with the customer politely and squarely; a manager who
  trades staff dignity for customer appeasement pays in a currency
  (trust) no refund reimburses. Afterwards, debrief the team member —
  they carried the heat, per Team Leadership's after-pressure habits.
- **Repair, then close the loop.** Proportionate make-good (Customer
  Experience's recovery anatomy), and when the dust settles, feed what
  the escalation revealed back into the complaint pattern analysis.

## Do this at work

1. **Prepare one hard conversation properly.** What you will say in the
   first thirty seconds, what outcome you want, and what you might be wrong
   about.
2. **Say the hard sentence first.** Practise stating it plainly. Difficult
   conversations get worse when the point arrives in minute nine.
3. **Ask before concluding.** In your next conflict, ask each person what
   they think happened before you decide what did.
4. **Handle one escalation personally.** Take the next angry customer
   yourself rather than routing it. What you learn about the cause is worth
   the discomfort.
5. **Follow up in writing.** After any difficult conversation: what was
   discussed, what was agreed, when you will review. Three lines, shared
   with the person.

## Summary

- Early beats polished; facts prepared, script not; private and
  unhurried; say it plainly in minute one, then genuinely listen.
- Performance: one owned issue, dialogue before prescription, agreement
  spoken and then written.
- Conflict: refuse the triangle, make each restate the other, steer to
  future behaviour with written working agreements — and enforce the
  respect floor.
- Escalations: take visible ownership, listen-acknowledge-facts-commit,
  defend staff dignity while owning mistakes, then feed the lesson back.
- If any room overheats, pause it on purpose — paused conversations
  resume; detonated ones get remembered.
