# Briefing Up

Communicating upward is a distinct craft with a distinct constraint: your
audience has less time, less context, and more competing demands than you —
and their decisions move more money and more people than yours. The
managers who become trusted advisers rather than status-reporters master
three things: compression without distortion, recommendations instead of
option-dumps, and answering the question that was actually asked. This
chapter covers all three.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Structure upward communication as a pyramid: conclusion first, detail
  underneath.
- Bring a recommendation rather than a menu of options.
- Answer the question that was actually asked.
- Deliver bad news early, with a plan attached.
- Frame your message in terms of what your reader is accountable for.

## The pyramid: summary on top, detail underneath

Senior readers consume information top-down and stop when satisfied.
Structure everything you send upward as a pyramid:

- **The top line** — the whole story in one or two sentences: situation,
  so-what, and what you recommend or need. Assume this is all that gets
  read, because often it is.
- **The middle** — the three or four supporting points that justify the
  top line, each a short paragraph or bullet.
- **The base** — the full detail, attached or available on request, for
  the rare deep-dive.

The pyramid isn't dumbing down; it's *pre-digestion*. You did the analysis
so they don't have to re-do it — that's the job. The mark of a good brief
is that the reader can act after the top line and would make the same
decision after reading everything.

Compression has one absolute rule: **shorten by selection, never by
distortion**. Dropping detail is your job; dropping the inconvenient risk,
rounding the numbers flattering-ward, or summarising a mixed picture as
rosy is how briefs become lies with good formatting. Your compression is
trusted exactly once.

## Bring a recommendation, not a menu

The chronic upward failure is the option-dump: three choices, neutrally
described, decision fully outsourced upward. It feels respectful; it
actually exports your homework. You are closer to the facts than your
manager — that's why you're briefing them. So:

- **Recommend.** "I recommend option B, because X and Y; the main risk is
  Z and here's how we'd watch it." Options belong in the brief (per the
  Decision Making course, one option is a preference), but *ranked*, with
  a named choice.
- **Show your reasoning, briefly.** The recommendation carries more weight
  when the criteria are visible — it lets the senior reader check your
  logic instead of re-doing your analysis.
- **Separate decision from information.** Open by saying which this is:
  "I need a decision on X" versus "no action needed — heads-up on Y."
  Senior readers triage everything; label your traffic.

Being overruled is fine and instructive — you'll learn a criterion you
didn't have. Bringing no recommendation is the only losing move: do it
twice and you've taught them you're a messenger, not a manager.

## Answer the question that was asked

When a senior person asks a question, answer *that question, first,
directly* — then add context if it genuinely changes the answer:

- "Will it be done by Friday?" → "Yes." / "No — Tuesday, because X." /
  "Most likely yes; the one risk is Y, and I'll know by Wednesday."
- Not: a three-minute history of the task that leaves the asker to
  extract the answer themselves.

Evasive-sounding answers usually aren't dishonesty; they're preparation
dumped in narrative order. Practise the direct form: **answer, then
reason, then caveat** — the spoken version of conclusion-first. When you
don't know, say "I don't know — I'll have it by [time]" and then hit the
time; that answer, delivered reliably, builds more credibility than fluent
guessing ever will. Guesses presented as facts are career-limiting the day
they're discovered, and they're always discovered.

## Bad news travels first class

The defining test of upward communication is what you do with bad news.
The rule: **bad news goes up early, honestly, and from you.** Your manager
should never learn of a problem in your area from someone else — not
because of politics, but because their ability to help (or shield) is
largest when the problem is young (the escalate-at-the-fork principle from
the Decision Making course).

Deliver it in the working shape: what happened, impact, what you've done,
what you're doing, what you need. That last section converts a confession
into a plan. And resist the two temptations: sitting on it hoping to fix
it quietly (works until it doesn't, catastrophically), and softening it
until it no longer sounds like news ("some minor challenges" describing a
stalled project). Managers who surface problems early with plans attached
get *more* autonomy over time, not less — because their reporting is the
early-warning system leadership can rely on.

## Know their world

The final upgrade is framing your area in their terms. Your manager
weighs your update against budgets, other teams, and their own upward
commitments. Translate accordingly: effort becomes cost, delays become
customer or revenue impact, requests become trade-offs ("this needs two
weeks of Chidi's time, which pushes X — worth it because Y"). The
finance course's vocabulary — margin, payback, cash timing — is much of
this translation. You don't need to speak like a director; you need your
facts to arrive pre-fitted to the decisions they feed.

## Do this at work

1. **Put the conclusion first.** Take your next upward report and move the
   answer to the top. Everything else becomes support.
2. **Add your recommendation.** Where you have presented options, say which
   one you would choose and why. A menu asks your manager to do your
   thinking.
3. **Deliver one piece of bad news early.** Something you are hoping resolves
   itself. Raise it this week with what you propose to do about it.
4. **Answer the actual question.** Re-read a recent reply and check whether
   you answered what was asked or what you wanted to discuss.
5. **Learn one thing about their world.** What is your manager measured on
   this quarter? Frame your next request in those terms.

## Summary

- Pyramid everything: a top line that can act alone, supports beneath,
  detail on request — compression by selection, never distortion.
- Bring ranked options and a named recommendation with visible
  reasoning; label whether you need a decision or are giving awareness.
- Answer the asked question first — answer, reason, caveat — and replace
  guesses with "I don't know, by when I'll know."
- Bad news goes up early, from you, with impact and plan attached; early
  honest escalators earn autonomy.
- Frame your area in their decision terms: cost, impact, trade-offs.
