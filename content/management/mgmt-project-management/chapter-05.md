# Tracking, Risk & Change Control

A plan is a photograph of intentions; a project is a moving thing. The
disciplines in this chapter — honest tracking, a working risk register,
and formal change control — are how the plan and reality stay in
conversation, so that steering happens while steering still works. They
are also, together, the antidote to the three killers from Chapter 1:
late bad news, surprise, and creep.

## Tracking: measure done, not effort

Project tracking goes wrong by measuring activity ("the team was busy")
or sentiment ("feels about eighty percent done"). Track facts instead:

- **Milestones hit or missed** — the binary heartbeat from Chapter 2.
- **Tasks completed against plan** — done means done: the "90% done for
  three weeks" task is the classic self-deception; its last tenth
  contains the problem, and honest tracking calls it *not done*.
- **Money spent against budget, at progress** — spend tracking to the
  Finance course's variance habit: the number matters less than the
  story, and overspend that tracks faster progress is a different story
  from overspend at half pace.

Run a short, regular project review — weekly suits most projects — on
exactly four questions: what finished, what's next, what's blocked or at
risk, and are we still landing on the date and budget? The last question
must be answered with the plan open, not from optimism. And report
upward on the same rhythm with the same honesty (Chapter 3's update
cadence): a project lead's credibility is worth more than a green
status, because credibility is what makes the eventual "we need a
decision" land.

## Risk: manage the future while it's cheap

A **risk** is a problem that hasn't happened yet — which is precisely
when it's cheapest to handle. The tool is humble: a **risk register**,
one page, listing for each risk what could happen, how likely it seems,
how bad it would be, what you're doing about it, and who owns it. The
craft is in the verbs:

- **Reduce** — act now so it's less likely or less severe: the early
  permit submission, the second supplier qualified, the spare parts
  ordered with the main batch.
- **Prepare** — a contingency plan and a **tripwire** (the Decision
  Making course's tool): decide in advance what evidence triggers the
  fallback, so the response fires on schedule instead of after a week
  of hopeful waiting.
- **Accept** — consciously, for small risks: noted, owned, no action.
  Acceptance by decision is management; acceptance by silence is luck.

Review the register at every project review — five minutes: anything
closer? anything new? anything fired? A register written once and filed
is a souvenir. And notice where risks come from: the wide-range
estimates of Chapter 2, the external dependencies of Chapter 3, the
suppliers of Chapter 4 — the earlier chapters' loose ends *are* the
register's first draft.

## Change control: the gate that saves the budget

Change is not the enemy — uncontrolled change is. Requirements really do
evolve; the site really does surprise; the stakeholder really did
forget something important. **Change control** is the discipline that
lets the project say yes to the right changes and survive them:

1. **Every change is written down** — what's requested, by whom, why.
   The one-line form is fine; informality is the enemy, not brevity.
2. **Every change is priced before it's approved** — in money, time,
   and knock-on effects, by the people who'd do the work (and through
   the contract's variation clause where suppliers are involved,
   Chapter 4). "It's small" is a claim, not an estimate.
3. **The right person decides** — changes within the project's
   contingency and authority, the project lead; changes that move the
   date, the budget, or the outcome, the sponsor, because those are the
   trade-offs the sponsor owns (Chapter 1). Criteria for the decision
   come from the success criteria: does this change serve what the
   project is *for*?
4. **Approved changes update the plan** — schedule, budget, spec, and
   the log. A change that's approved but not planned is just creep with
   a signature.

The refusal conversation matters as much as the process. Most creep
arrives as a friendly, reasonable, small request from someone you'll
work with again. The answer that preserves both the project and the
relationship is not "no" — it's *"yes, through the gate: here's what it
costs, here's who decides."* Half of requests evaporate when priced;
the survivors were worth doing properly.

## When the project is genuinely off track

Sometimes tracking tells you the truth you didn't want: the date or the
budget won't hold. The failure mode is narrating the slide — reporting
each slip as it happens, hoping. The management move is a **re-plan**:
stop, re-estimate honestly from where you are (sunk costs stay sunk —
the Decision Making course's rule), and bring the sponsor options, not
apologies: the scope–time–cost triangle, priced. Deliver less, or
later, or spend more — or, sometimes, stop entirely, because the
project that no longer clears its business case (the Finance course's
question: *would we start this today?*) deserves an honest ending, not
a slow one. A project killed at the right moment is a management
success; the slow-motion failure everyone saw coming is the expensive
kind of politeness.

## Summary

- Track facts — milestones, done-means-done tasks, spend against
  progress — on a weekly rhythm, and keep the upward report exactly as
  honest as the inward one.
- Run a one-page risk register with real verbs: reduce now, prepare
  with tripwired contingencies, or accept consciously — and review it
  every week, because a filed register is a souvenir.
- Put every change through the gate: written, priced before approval,
  decided at the right level, and folded into the plan — "yes, through
  the gate" protects both budget and relationships.
- Half of change requests evaporate when priced; the rest were worth
  doing properly.
- When the truth turns bad, re-plan from where you are and bring the
  sponsor priced options — including, sometimes, the honest ending.
