# What Makes a Project

Some work repeats — the daily operations that keep a business running. Other
work happens once, on purpose, to change something: open the new location,
install the new system, launch the new product, run the relocation. That
second kind is a **project**, and it fails differently from routine work.
Routine work fails loudly and gets fixed tomorrow; projects fail quietly,
over months, by drifting away from a destination nobody wrote down. This
chapter covers the definitions that prevent that drift — and the reasons
projects overrun, which are remarkably consistent across every industry.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Say what makes a piece of work a project rather than ongoing operations.
- Name the four standard causes of overrun and spot them starting.
- Identify the roles a project must have, and what breaks when one is vacant.
- Write success criteria specific enough to be judged against.

## A project is defined by its ending

A project is a **temporary effort with a defined result**: it starts, it
delivers something specific, and it ends. That sounds obvious and is
routinely violated — the "project" that has run for two years with no
agreed finish line is not a project; it is an expensive habit. Three
definitions, written down before work starts, make the difference:

- **The outcome.** What exists when this is done, concretely enough that
  everyone would recognise it. "Improve the warehouse" fails the test;
  "goods received are shelved and findable in the system within one day,
  in the new layout, by end of June" passes. The Decision Making course's
  discipline of criteria-before-options applies directly: agree what
  success will be judged on before debating how to get there.
- **The scope — including what's out.** Every project attracts good ideas
  the way a light attracts insects, and each is small on its own. Writing
  down what the project will *not* do ("no change to the billing process";
  "the old building is not being renovated") is more protective than the
  in-scope list, because the boundary is where the arguments will happen.
- **The constraints.** The budget, the deadline, the people available, and
  which of the three bends first when pressure comes. Every project trades
  between scope, time, and cost; pretending all three are fixed just means
  the trade-off will be made later, silently, usually as quality.

## Why projects overrun

The causes of overrun are so consistent they deserve to be memorised —
they will all knock on your door:

- **Optimistic estimating.** Estimates come from imagining the work going
  well. Real work includes the sick week, the late delivery, the rework,
  the approval that took a fortnight. Chapter 2 covers the corrective.
- **Undefined scope.** If the destination was vague, everyone built toward
  a different one, and the differences surface at the most expensive
  moment — the end.
- **Scope creep.** Not one big change — a hundred reasonable little ones,
  each accepted informally, none of which came with more time or money.
  Chapter 5 gives creep its formal enemy: change control.
- **The missing decider.** Projects stall waiting for decisions nobody
  owns. A project without a named decision-maker doesn't avoid decisions;
  it queues them (decision debt, in the Decision Making course's terms).
- **Late bad news.** On troubled projects, everyone knows before anyone
  says. The status was "green" for months because reporting problems felt
  like failure — so the problem compounded quietly until it was
  undeniable and unfixable.

Notice what's absent from the list: technical difficulty. Projects rarely
fail because the work was too hard; they fail because the *management* of
the work — definition, estimation, decisions, honesty — was skipped.

## The roles that must exist

Titles vary everywhere; the functions don't. Three must be filled, by
name, before work starts:

- **The sponsor** — the person who wants the result and pays for it. The
  sponsor owns the *why*, decides the trade-offs when scope, time, and
  cost collide, and is the escalation path when the project needs
  authority it doesn't have. A project whose sponsor has moved on
  emotionally is already dying; part of the project lead's job is keeping
  the sponsor genuinely engaged.
- **The project lead** — one person accountable for delivery. Not
  necessarily the most senior or the most technical: the person who plans
  the work, watches progress, surfaces problems early, and coordinates
  everyone else. The Team Leadership course's single-owner principle at
  project scale: shared leadership of a project is no leadership.
- **The doers** — the people executing, whose *actual availability* is a
  planning fact, not a hope. "Amina will do it alongside her day job" is
  the single most common resourcing lie in small-business projects.

## Success criteria: decide how you'll keep score

Before starting, agree with the sponsor how success will be measured —
not just "delivered", but delivered *to what standard, by when, at what
cost*, and, where it matters, what the result should achieve afterwards
(the Finance course's business-case habit: the benefits that justified
the project deserve a check-back date). Written success criteria do two
jobs: they aim the team, and they protect it — against the retrospective
memory that the project was "supposed to" include things it never was.

## Do this at work

1. **Write the ending.** For a piece of work in your area, write the sentence
   that says when it is finished. If you cannot, it is operations, not a
   project, and it needs a different kind of management.
2. **Name your sponsor.** Who can resolve a conflict over resources or
   priority? If the answer is "nobody" or "a committee", that is the first
   thing to fix.
3. **Write three success criteria.** Specific enough that two people would
   agree whether they were met. "Improved efficiency" fails this test;
   "installations per crew per day above six" passes.
4. **Check for the vacant role.** Project lead, sponsor, and the people who
   will operate the result. Vacant roles do not announce themselves — they
   surface as decisions nobody can make.
5. **Diagnose one past overrun.** Scope creep, optimistic estimates, an
   unmanaged dependency, or a late decision? The answer tells you which of
   the next five chapters to read hardest.

## Summary

- A project is temporary and defined by its ending: outcome, scope (with
  explicit exclusions), and constraints written down before work starts.
- All projects trade between scope, time, and cost — choose consciously
  which bends, or the trade will be made silently as quality.
- Overruns come from optimistic estimates, vague scope, informal creep,
  unowned decisions, and late bad news — management failures, not
  technical ones.
- Fill three roles by name: an engaged sponsor who owns trade-offs, one
  accountable project lead, and doers whose availability is real.
- Agree written success criteria with the sponsor — how the score will be
  kept — before the first task begins.
