# Managing Suppliers & Quality

Few projects are delivered entirely by your own people. Contractors,
suppliers, installers, and specialists do the work you can't or
shouldn't — which means part of your project's fate is in hands you
don't manage day to day. This chapter covers commissioning external work
so it arrives on time and to standard: clear specifications, contracts
that anticipate problems, inspection that catches defects while they're
cheap, and acceptance discipline that doesn't stall delivery. (The
Vendor & Procurement Management course covers sourcing and supplier
relationships in depth; this chapter is the project-side view.)

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Write a specification precise enough to be the quality decision it is.
- Agree what happens on a bad day before the bad day arrives.
- Inspect at the moments where a defect is still cheap to fix.
- Run an acceptance gate that actually gates.
- Act early and proportionately on a failing supplier.

## The specification is the quality decision

Quality problems with contractors are usually specification problems
with a delay. The supplier built what the document said — the document
just didn't say what you meant. Before any external work is agreed:

- **Specify outcomes and standards, not vibes.** "Good quality finish"
  is a future argument; "surfaces smooth, edges sealed, load rating X,
  compliant with standard Y" is a contract. Where a formal standard
  exists, cite it — that one line imports a hundred settled arguments.
- **Specify the checkable.** Every requirement should imply its test:
  how will both sides know it's met? Requirements nobody can verify are
  decorations.
- **Walk the site or the context together before pricing.** Half of
  variation claims ("we didn't know the access was like this") are
  killed by a joint visit and a photographed record of conditions.

## Contracts: agree the bad day in advance

A contract's real job is not the good day, when everyone performs — it's
the bad day, agreed while everyone is still friends. However formal or
simple the document, four things belong in it: **what** exactly is
delivered to what standard (the specification), **when** — with staged
dates on longer work, not just a final one, **what it costs and when
it's paid** — payments tied to verified progress, never fully in
advance, with a final slice held until acceptance, and **what happens
when it goes wrong** — delay remedies, defect rectification, how
variations get priced and approved, and how either side exits. The
variation clause deserves emphasis: mid-project changes are where
budgets die (Chapter 5), so the contract should force every variation
through written pricing and approval *before* the work, not negotiation
after it.

## Inspect early, inspect at the right moments

The cost of a defect rises with every stage built on top of it. The
wrong base, caught the day it's laid, is a bad afternoon; caught at
handover, it's a demolition. So inspection is scheduled, not saved for
the end:

- **First-article checks.** Inspect the first unit, the first room, the
  first batch — before the pattern is repeated fifty times. Approving a
  first article also fixes the standard: "like that one" becomes the
  benchmark.
- **Hold points at soon-to-be-hidden work.** Anything about to be
  covered up — foundations, wiring, waterproofing — gets checked before
  it disappears, by agreement written into the schedule. A hold point
  skipped to "save time" is the most expensive time ever saved.
- **Progress visits with eyes open.** Regular short visits beat rare
  audits: you see pace, housekeeping, and corner-cutting culture —
  leading indicators of the defects you haven't found yet.

Inspection tone matters. The goal is a supplier who surfaces problems
early because raising them is safe and hiding them is not — the
blameless-review logic from the Team Leadership course, applied
outward. A supplier punished for honesty becomes a supplier who buries
problems under the next layer of work.

## Acceptance: the gate that pays

Acceptance is the formal moment the work becomes yours — and the last
moment you hold real leverage, because the final payment is still in
your hands. Run it as a process, not a handshake:

- **Test against the specification**, item by item, with the supplier
  present. The checkable requirements of the spec now do their job.
- **Document defects as a punch list** — each with a standard reference,
  a fix owner, and a date. Minor defects don't have to block acceptance;
  they have to block *final payment* until cleared.
- **Never accept by default.** Occupying, using, or paying for work is
  acceptance in practice whatever the paperwork says. If it isn't
  accepted, don't use it and don't release the retention.

Balance is the craft here. Acceptance discipline protects the project;
weaponised perfectionism stalls it and poisons the relationship. Judge
against the standard you specified — not a standard you wish you'd
specified. If the spec was wrong, that's a variation to negotiate
honestly (and a lesson for the next spec), not a defect to bully
through.

## When a supplier is failing

Slipping dates, thinning crews, quality drifting, invoices arriving
faster than progress — supplier failure telegraphs itself. Act on the
leading signals: raise it formally and early, ask for a recovery plan
with dates, increase inspection frequency, and check your contractual
options *before* you need them. Paying ahead of verified progress is how
projects lose both the money and the leverage; if the worst comes, the
contract's exit clause and your documentation (photos, inspection
records, correspondence) decide whether the project recovers in weeks
or in court. The Decision Making course's tripwire discipline applies:
decide in advance what evidence triggers escalation, so the decision
fires even when the relationship makes it awkward.

## Do this at work

1. **Read one live specification as a supplier would.** Where could a
   reasonable person deliver something you would reject? Every such gap is
   a dispute waiting to be scheduled.
2. **Find the missing bad-day clause.** In a current agreement, what happens
   if delivery is late, quality is wrong, or volumes change? If it is not
   written, it will be negotiated at the worst possible moment.
3. **Move one inspection earlier.** Find a check that currently happens at
   delivery and ask whether a sample, a first article, or a factory visit
   would catch the problem while it is still cheap.
4. **Put teeth in one acceptance gate.** Write what "accepted" requires, who
   signs it, and what payment or milestone depends on it. A gate with no
   consequence is a formality.
5. **Escalate one supplier early.** If performance is drifting, have the
   conversation now, with specifics and a date. Suppliers rarely recover
   after the relationship has soured; they often recover after a clear,
   early, unemotional conversation.

## Summary

- Most contractor quality problems are specification problems: specify
  checkable outcomes against named standards, and walk the context
  together before pricing.
- Contracts exist for the bad day: staged dates, payment tied to
  verified progress with retention to acceptance, and variations priced
  and approved in writing before the work.
- Inspect early and at hold points — first articles and soon-to-be-
  hidden work — and keep honesty safe so problems surface instead of
  getting buried.
- Run acceptance against the spec with a punch list; minor defects
  block final payment, not necessarily acceptance — and never accept by
  default through use or payment.
- Act on the leading signals of supplier failure with formal recovery
  plans and pre-decided tripwires; leverage lives in unpaid money and
  good records.
