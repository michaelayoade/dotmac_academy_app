# Closeout & Handover

Projects don't end when the work is done; they end when the result is
*owned* — running in someone's hands, documented, paid for, and learned
from. Skipping closeout is the most common way a good project curdles
into a bad memory: the undocumented system nobody can maintain, the
punch list that never closed, the supplier retention never released, the
same mistakes rediscovered by the next project at full price. This
chapter is about landing the plane, not just approaching the runway.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Hand over ownership rather than merely delivering an output.
- Confirm the receiving team can actually run what you built.
- Close the commercial and administrative business, not just the work.
- Run a review that captures what the next project should inherit.
- Declare the ending clearly enough that the project stops consuming attention.

## Handover is a transfer of ownership, not a delivery

Whatever the project built, someone else will run it — a team, a
department, sometimes a customer. The Team Leadership course's handover
rule scales up directly: **ownership transfers only when the receiver
explicitly accepts it**, and acceptance requires that they can actually
operate what they're receiving. That means handover is planned work
(it was on the Chapter 2 breakdown, with the invisible tasks), not a
ceremony at the end:

- **Involve the operators before the end.** The people who'll run the
  result should have been stakeholders since Chapter 3 — reviewing
  designs, present at testing, raising the operational facts the
  project can't see. Operators meeting the system for the first time at
  handover is a project that will be handed back.
- **Train against reality, not slides.** Running the real thing, doing
  the real tasks, handling the planned failures — with the project team
  still around to catch what training misses.
- **A run-book of the unglamorous facts:** how to operate it, how to
  tell it's healthy, what routine care it needs, what to do when it
  misbehaves, who to call, and where every warranty, certificate,
  manual, and as-built record lives. The test of the documentation is
  simple: could a competent person who wasn't on the project keep this
  alive using only what's written?
- **A supported first period.** Agree a window — weeks, typically —
  where the project team stays reachable and defects found in real use
  route back fast. Then *end it explicitly*, or the project team
  becomes the permanent unpaid operations team.

## Close the business, not just the work

The administrative tail is where money and leverage quietly leak:

- **Final acceptance, formally.** The Chapter 4 punch list driven to
  zero (or to a written, dated residual list), the acceptance signed by
  the sponsor and the receiving owner. Ambiguous endings invite the
  eternal project.
- **Contracts and money closed.** Final supplier accounts settled
  against verified work, retentions released on schedule *after*
  defects clear, warranties registered with dates and claim routes
  recorded, and the project account closed so nothing keeps billing
  against it. The Finance course's discipline applies to the end as
  much as the start: the last five percent of the budget deserves the
  same scrutiny as the first.
- **The benefits check-back, scheduled.** The business case that
  justified the project (Finance course, Chapter 5) named benefits —
  put a date in the calendar, months out, to measure whether they
  arrived, and report it either way. This is the loop that keeps the
  *next* business case honest, and the step nearly every organisation
  skips.

## The review: pay yourself for the experience

Every project generates two products: the result, and the experience of
building it. The second is free — and forfeited unless it's collected.
Within a few weeks of the end, while memory is fresh and tempers are
cool, run the project version of the blameless review (Team Leadership,
Chapter 5): what did we plan versus what happened — estimates, risks
that fired or didn't, changes and their real costs; what went *well*
that should become standard practice; what hurt, and what specific,
owned change prevents it next time. Three findings that actually change
the next project beat thirty that get filed. Feed the concrete numbers
— how long things actually took, what things actually cost — into
wherever the next project's estimators will look (Chapter 2's "check
against history" is only possible if someone keeps the history).

## Declare the ending

Finally, say it's over — plainly, to everyone from Chapter 3's map. The
result is live, the owner is named, the project team is released,
residual items live on the operations list, and thanks are given
specifically (credit down, per the Team Leadership course: name who did
what). Undeclared endings leave a ghost project that haunts calendars
and budgets for months. Declared endings return the scarcest resource —
attention — to whatever comes next, and end the story while it can
still be told as a success.

## Do this at work

1. **Name the receiving owner.** One person or team who owns the result from
   the handover date. A handover to "the business" is not a handover.
2. **Test the handover, don't assert it.** Have the receiving team run the
   thing while you watch. What they stumble on is what your documentation is
   missing.
3. **Close the commercial tail.** Final invoices, retentions, warranties,
   unused purchase orders, access that should be revoked. This is the part
   that quietly stays open for months.
4. **Hold the review while people remember.** Within two weeks. What went
   well, what did not, and specifically what the next project should do
   differently — written down, not just discussed.
5. **Check one estimate against actuals.** Compare planned to actual
   duration and cost. That comparison is the only thing that makes your next
   estimate better.
6. **Declare it over.** Tell the stakeholders, release the team, and stop the
   status reports. Projects that are never declared finished keep consuming
   attention from people who could be doing the next thing.

## Summary

- Handover transfers ownership, not boxes: operators involved early,
  trained on the real thing, holding a run-book that lets a stranger
  keep it alive, with a supported period that ends explicitly.
- Close the business: formal acceptance against a zeroed punch list,
  supplier accounts and retentions settled after defects clear,
  warranties registered, the project account shut.
- Schedule the benefits check-back against the original business case
  and report it either way — the loop that keeps future cases honest.
- Run a blameless review while memory is fresh: a few owned changes
  that improve the next project, and real duration/cost history kept
  where future estimators will find it.
- Declare the ending: named owner, released team, specific thanks —
  ghost projects haunt; finished ones teach.
