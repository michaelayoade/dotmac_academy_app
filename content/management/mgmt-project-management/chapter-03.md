# Stakeholders & Approvals

Projects don't happen in empty rooms. Around every project stands a ring
of people who can help it, delay it, or kill it: the sponsor, the
departments it touches, the neighbours it disturbs, the authorities who
must approve it, the staff whose work it changes. Most project delay is
not technical — it is a person or an office the plan forgot to involve,
discovered at the moment they could do the most damage. This chapter is
about finding those people early and turning them from ambush into
asset.

## Map the stakeholders before they map you

A **stakeholder** is anyone affected by the project or able to affect it.
At the start — as part of planning, not after — list them, and for each
answer two questions: *how much can they influence this project?* and
*how much does it affect them?* The combination tells you the treatment:

- **High influence, high stake** — the sponsor, the approving authority,
  the department whose operations you're changing. Manage these closely:
  involved in shaping decisions, never surprised.
- **High influence, low stake** — the executive who can veto but barely
  cares, the authority with a rubber stamp and a queue. Keep satisfied:
  know their requirements precisely and meet them without drama.
- **Low influence, high stake** — the staff whose daily work changes,
  the customers who feel the disruption. Keep genuinely informed and
  listened to: they can't stop the project, but they decide whether the
  result is adopted or quietly sabotaged — and they know operational
  facts the plan needs.
- **Low influence, low stake** — monitor politely; don't build machinery.

Two groups get forgotten so reliably they deserve their own line: the
people who must *operate* what the project delivers (Chapter 6 is their
chapter, but their involvement starts now), and the people *between*
the project and its goal — landlords, neighbouring businesses, the
office that controls the loading bay. The project rarely needs their
enthusiasm; it needs them not to become a blocker with a grievance.

## Approvals are tasks, not weather

Sign-offs, permits, budget releases, safety inspections, landlord
consents — every project has gates that only someone else can open.
Treat each as a first-class task in the plan (Chapter 2's external
dependencies), with an owner, a submission date, and a realistic
duration measured from *complete submission*, not first contact. The
practical disciplines:

- **Find out the real requirements early** — the documents, the format,
  the fees, the inspection windows. Most approval delay is rework: the
  application bounced for a missing item nobody had asked about.
- **Submit early, chase politely, keep records.** The approval that
  takes three weeks takes three weeks from a complete submission;
  submitting in week one instead of week six is free schedule. Log every
  contact — when escalation is needed, the record is your case.
- **Never build past an ungranted approval.** Work done ahead of a
  permit is a bet with someone else's decision — sometimes it's demoled,
  always it's leverage handed to the approver. Where the schedule truly
  can't wait, that's a sponsor-level risk decision (Chapter 5), made
  explicitly, never a site-level habit.

## Expectations: the stakeholder work that never ends

Stakeholder trouble is usually expectation trouble. The department head
who "understood" the system would do something it won't; the sponsor who
remembers a date that was never promised; the team next door that
assumed the noise would last a week. The correctives are communication
habits, not charisma:

- **A steady rhythm of honest updates** — the Team Leadership course's
  crisis-communication pattern, in peacetime: what's done, what's next,
  what's at risk, on a cadence people can rely on. Silence gets filled
  by rumour, and rumour always writes a worse story.
- **Bad news early, with a plan.** Chapter 1 named late bad news as a
  killer. The stakeholder version: a slip disclosed at month three with
  options attached is management; the same slip discovered at month six
  is betrayal. Early bad news costs discomfort; late bad news costs
  trust — and trust is the currency the next approval runs on.
- **Decisions in writing.** Who agreed what, when, and why — the
  Decision Making course's decision log, kept for the project. Not
  bureaucracy: the polite end of arguments. When the disputed
  instruction surfaces in month five, the log answers in one line what
  meetings would relitigate for a week.

## When stakeholders collide

Sometimes stakeholders want incompatible things — the sponsor wants the
date, the operations head wants zero disruption, the authority wants a
review that takes a month. The project lead's job is not to please
everyone; it is to surface the collision early, frame the options with
their costs (criteria first, per the Decision Making course), and get
the trade-off decided by the person who owns it — usually the sponsor.
Unresolved stakeholder conflict doesn't stay unresolved; it resolves
itself at the worst moment, in the approver's favour, with interest.

## Summary

- Map stakeholders at the start by influence and stake; manage the
  powerful closely, meet the approvers' requirements precisely, and keep
  the affected genuinely informed — they decide adoption.
- Remember the forgotten two: future operators, and the parties between
  the project and its goal.
- Approvals are plan tasks with owners and dates: learn real
  requirements early, submit complete and early, chase with records, and
  never build past an ungranted permit without an explicit sponsor
  decision.
- Run a steady update rhythm, deliver bad news early with options, and
  keep decisions in a written log.
- Surface stakeholder collisions early and route the trade-off to its
  owner — conflicts left alone resolve themselves at the worst moment.
