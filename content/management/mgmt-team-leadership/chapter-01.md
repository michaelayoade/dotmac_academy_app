# From Doer to Manager

You were promoted because you were good at the work. That is exactly why the
first months of managing are hard: the skills that got you here are not the
skills the new role runs on. This chapter is about the shift — what you stop
doing, what you start doing, and how to measure yourself once "did I do good
work today?" is no longer the right question.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Say what changes when you move from doing the work to leading it.
- Recognise the player-coach trap in your own week.
- Name what a manager actually produces, and judge yourself against it.
- Use your first weeks to listen before you change things.

## What actually changes

As an individual contributor, your output was your own work: the tasks you
completed, the problems you solved, the quality you delivered personally. As a
manager, **your output is the output of your team**. That single sentence
changes everything downstream:

- A brilliant day where you personally solved three hard problems, while your
  team of five idled or stayed blocked, is a *bad* day.
- An ordinary-looking day where you unblocked two people, clarified a priority,
  and coached someone through a task they had never done before is a *good*
  day — even though you "did nothing" you could point at.

New managers consistently misjudge this because doing the work feels
productive and managing feels vague. Learn to see the leverage: an hour spent
removing a blocker for three people returns three hours of team output.

## The player-coach trap

The most common first-year failure mode is the **player-coach trap**: keeping
all your old work while adding management on top. It feels safe — you stay
useful in the way you already know — but it fails predictably:

1. Management tasks (planning, feedback, unblocking) are *interruptible* and
   have no deadline, so they always lose to hands-on work. They quietly stop
   happening.
2. You become the bottleneck. Hard tasks route to you because you are fastest,
   so nobody else ever becomes fast.
3. Your team learns that the way to succeed is to hand problems upward.

You do not have to drop all hands-on work — in small teams you often can't.
But treat hands-on work as a *scheduled minority* of your time, not the
default you retreat to. A useful starting split for a working supervisor is
roughly half hands-on, half managing — and the managing half is the part you
protect when the week gets busy, not the part you sacrifice.

## What managers actually produce

If your output is the team's output, your daily work is whatever raises it.
In practice that clusters into four products:

- **Clarity.** Everyone knows what they are doing, why it matters, and what
  "done" looks like. Most team underperformance is unclear expectations, not
  low effort.
- **Throughput.** Work is assigned to the right people, blockers are removed
  quickly, and nobody is idle while others drown.
- **Quality.** Standards are explicit, checked, and enforced consistently —
  not by you redoing the work, but by the team knowing what good looks like.
- **Growth.** Each person can do a little more this quarter than last. A team
  that only its manager can operate is a failure of management, however good
  the manager.

When you are unsure what to do with an hour, pick the item on this list your
team is weakest at.

## The identity shift

Two uncomfortable adjustments come with the role, and it is better to name
them than to be ambushed by them:

**Your relationships change.** You may now manage former peers and friends.
You do not have to become distant, but you do have to be *consistent*: the
same standards, the same transparency, the same access for everyone. The
fastest way to lose a team is visible favouritism toward the people you were
close to before.

**Credit flows differently.** Good managers give credit down and absorb
pressure from above. When the team succeeds, name the people who did it. When
the team fails, the account you give upward starts with what *you* will change.
Doing this consistently is how you earn the authority the job title alone does
not give you.

## Your first weeks: listen before you change

Resist arriving with a reform agenda. In your first weeks:

- Meet each team member one-to-one. Ask what is working, what is wasting
  their time, and what they would change first. You will hear the same two or
  three problems repeatedly — that is your real starting backlog.
- Learn the commitments the team is already carrying before you add new ones.
- Change small, visible irritations first. Fixing a broken rota or a pointless
  report earns more trust than announcing a vision.

## Common failure modes

- **Staying the hero.** Taking the hardest tasks yourself every time. Feels
  generous; guarantees the team never develops.
- **Managing by mood.** Standards that depend on your stress level. People
  can work with a tough manager; they cannot work with an unpredictable one.
- **Avoiding the role.** Not addressing poor performance or conflict because
  it is uncomfortable. Silence reads as approval, and your best people notice
  first.
- **Invisible managing.** Doing real management work but never explaining
  decisions, so the team experiences you as arbitrary.

## Do this at work

1. **Audit last week's calendar.** How many hours went to doing the work
   yourself versus making the team more effective? The ratio is your job
   description as actually practised.
2. **Find the task you cannot let go of.** There is usually one you keep
   because you are the best at it. That is the player-coach trap with a name.
3. **Ask three people what would help.** Before changing anything, ask what
   gets in their way. Listening first is not delay; it is how you avoid
   fixing what was not broken.
4. **Write what your team produces.** One sentence. If it reads like a list
   of activities rather than an outcome, keep rewriting.
5. **Name one thing only you can do.** Then protect time for it this week.
   Everything else is a candidate for Chapter 2.

## Summary

- A manager's output is the team's output; judge your day by what the team
  achieved, not what you personally produced.
- Avoid the player-coach trap: hands-on work is a scheduled minority, and the
  management work is what you protect under pressure.
- You produce four things: clarity, throughput, quality, and growth.
- Be consistent with former peers, give credit down, absorb pressure from
  above, and listen before you change things.
