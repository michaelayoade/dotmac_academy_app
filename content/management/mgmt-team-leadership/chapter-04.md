# Coaching & Performance

Your team's capability is not fixed — it is something you actively build or
passively let stagnate. This chapter covers the tools for building it:
feedback that changes behaviour, coaching that develops judgement, and the
harder discipline of dealing with underperformance early, fairly, and in
writing.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Give feedback as situation, behaviour and impact rather than as a verdict.
- Make praise specific enough to be repeatable.
- Coach by building judgement instead of supplying answers.
- Hold a performance conversation early, while it is still small.
- Record what needs recording, and know why.

## Feedback: specific, prompt, and about behaviour

Most feedback fails because it is vague ("be more professional"), late (six
weeks after the event), or about the person rather than the behaviour
("you're careless"). A simple structure fixes all three — describe:

1. **Situation** — when and where, specifically. "In yesterday's client
   meeting…"
2. **Behaviour** — what the person actually did, observably, without motive
   guessing. "…you gave the client a delivery date we hadn't agreed as a
   team…"
3. **Impact** — the effect it had. "…so we're now committed to a date two
   people say is impossible."

Then stop and let them respond. Half the time there is context you lack;
the other half, the person draws the conclusion themselves, which beats you
announcing it.

Three habits multiply the effect:

- **Promptly, privately, small.** Feedback within a day or two, one-to-one,
  while the stakes are small. Saving up incidents for a quarterly ambush is
  the worst of all options.
- **Positive feedback with the same specificity.** "Great job" is pleasant;
  "the way you rechecked the figures before sending caught a real error" is
  repeatable. Aim for far more specific-positive than corrective — it is
  what makes the corrective kind survivable.
- **Feedback flows up too.** Ask your team what you should do differently,
  and visibly act on one thing they say. It buys you the right to be heard.

The difference the structure makes, on the same underlying concern:

| Vague and personal | Situation · Behaviour · Impact |
| --- | --- |
| "You're careless with detail." | "In yesterday's client meeting, you gave a delivery date we hadn't agreed as a team. We're now committed to a date two people say is impossible." |
| "You need to be more of a team player." | "On Tuesday the rota changed and Ada found out from the customer. It meant she'd already promised a visit she couldn't make." |
| "Great job this week." | "You spotted that the delivery address didn't match the survey notes and checked before dispatch. That's a wasted trip we didn't take." |

Read the left column and notice what it gives the recipient: a verdict about
their character, no evidence, and nothing to do differently on Monday. It is
also unanswerable — you cannot discuss whether someone is careless, only
whether they did a specific thing.

The right column is answerable, which is the point. It may well come back
with context you did not have ("the client had already been given that date
by sales"), and that is the structure working rather than failing. Feedback
that cannot be corrected by new information is not feedback; it is a
sentence.

Note that the third row applies the same discipline to praise. "Great job" is
pleasant and teaches nothing; naming the behaviour makes it repeatable by the
person who did it and by everyone who hears it.

## Coaching: build judgement, not dependence

Telling someone the answer solves today's problem and guarantees they bring
you tomorrow's. Coaching reverses that: when someone brings a problem they
could reasonably work through, resist solving it. Ask instead:

- "What have you tried?"
- "What are the options you see?"
- "What would you do if I weren't here?"
- "What's the risk if that's wrong, and how would you catch it?"

Then let them run with their answer wherever the stakes allow — including
when their approach is merely *different* from yours, not worse. People
commit to their own plans far harder than to yours.

Coaching needs raw material: **stretch assignments**. Deliberately hand each
person work slightly beyond their current level — the first-time task, the
small project lead, the meeting they run in your place — matched with the
right delegation level from Chapter 2. Growth lives in that gap; a manager
who never assigns beyond proven ability caps the team at its current size.

A simple development habit that outperforms most formal schemes: for each
person, know the answer to two questions — *what is this person's next
capability?* and *what assignment this month moves them toward it?*

## Performance conversations: early and honest

Underperformance rarely announces itself; it accumulates. The redone tasks,
the missed handovers, the colleagues quietly routing around someone. Managers
usually wait too long, for understandable reasons — the conversation is
uncomfortable, the person is likeable, maybe it will self-correct. It almost
never does, and everyone else on the team is watching what you tolerate.
**What you accept is your actual standard, whatever your stated one.**

When a pattern emerges — not a single bad day — address it directly:

1. Name the gap using the feedback structure: expected standard, observed
   performance, impact. Patterns need examples; bring specifics, not vibes.
2. **Diagnose before prescribing.** Underperformance has four common roots:
   unclear expectations, missing skills, missing resources or obstacles, and
   will. Only the last is a pure discipline problem — and it is the rarest.
   The fix differs completely by root cause: clarify, train, unblock, or
   confront.
3. Agree a specific, short-horizon plan: what improves, by when, measured
   how, with what support from you.
4. Follow up on the agreed dates, and acknowledge improvement when it
   happens. A performance plan that only ever tightens teaches people that
   recovery is impossible.

## Write it down

Document performance conversations from the first serious one: date, the gap
discussed, examples, the agreed plan. Not as a trap — as fairness and memory.
Documentation protects the employee (the expectations are explicit and
consistent), protects you (the history is facts, not recollection), and is
simply required if the situation ever becomes formal. A three-line note after
the meeting, shared with the person, is usually enough: *what we discussed,
what we agreed, when we review.*

The converse also matters: document the good. Recognition tied to specifics —
in reviews, in front of the team where appropriate — is the cheapest
performance tool you have, and the one most managers underuse.

## Do this at work

1. **Rewrite one piece of feedback.** Take something you have said or meant
   to say and put it into situation, behaviour, impact. Notice that it
   becomes answerable — that is the point.
2. **Give one specific positive.** Name the behaviour and its effect, not
   "great job". Specific praise is repeatable; general praise is only
   pleasant.
3. **Say the thing you have been saving.** Find the concern you have been
   storing for a formal review and raise it this week, small and private.
   Saved-up feedback arrives as an ambush.
4. **Ask for feedback upward.** Ask your team what you should do differently,
   then visibly act on one thing. It buys the right to be heard.
5. **Replace one answer with a question.** Next time someone brings you a
   problem, ask what they would do. Then usually agree with it.
6. **Check a record.** For any performance concern in progress, is there a
   written note of what was discussed and agreed? If not, write it now.

## Summary

- Give feedback as situation → behaviour → impact: promptly, privately, and
  about observable behaviour, with specific praise outnumbering correction.
- Coach with questions instead of answers, and fuel growth with deliberate
  stretch assignments one level beyond proven ability.
- Address performance patterns early; what you tolerate becomes the team's
  real standard.
- Diagnose the root — clarity, skill, obstacles, or will — before choosing
  the fix; most underperformance is not a will problem.
- Put agreements in writing from the first serious conversation: it is
  fairness, not aggression.
