# Organising the Team's Work

A team can be full of capable, motivated people and still perform badly if the
work itself is disorganised: priorities unclear, load uneven, coverage full of
gaps, and tasks silently dropped in the space between two people. Organising
the work is unglamorous, and it is one of the highest-leverage things a
manager does. This chapter covers planning capacity, setting priorities,
building coverage, and running handovers that don't leak.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Make the team's work visible enough to manage.
- Compute real plannable capacity rather than nominal hours.
- Spot the overloaded individual hiding inside a comfortable team average.
- Set priorities so the team is not deciding them privately.
- Build handovers and rotas that do not drop things.

## Make the work visible

You cannot organise what you cannot see. The first step in any disorganised
team is a single, shared view of the work — a board, a list, a tracker;
the tool matters far less than the habit. Every piece of work the team is
expected to do appears in it, with three facts: **what it is, who owns it,
and its status**.

Visibility changes behaviour on its own. Uneven workloads become obvious.
"I thought you had that" conversations disappear, because the board answers
the question. And your own planning stops depending on memory and starts
depending on facts.

The discipline that keeps a board honest: work that isn't on the board isn't
owed, and work on the board has exactly one owner. If people are doing
significant untracked work, the board is fiction — fix that first.

## Capacity: plan with real numbers

Overload is a planning failure before it is an effort failure. A person's week
is not 40 hours of task time — meetings, interruptions, breaks, and the
unplanned work that always arrives eat a large share. Plan team commitments
against **realistic available hours**, and hold back a buffer for the
unplanned work your team predictably receives. If history says a third of the
week goes to unplanned requests, committing 100% of capacity to planned work
is a decision to miss deadlines.

Watch load per person, not just totals. A team at 80% capacity can still
contain one person at 130% — usually your most reliable one, because reliable
people attract work. Rebalance deliberately; do not wait for them to complain,
because the best ones don't until they resign.

### Worked example: where a 40-hour week actually goes

One person, one nominal week:

| | Hours |
| --- | ---: |
| Nominal week | 40.0 |
| Recurring meetings | −4.0 |
| Admin, email, reporting | −2.0 |
| Available for work | 34.0 |
| Unplanned work (history says ~30%) | −10.2 |
| **Plannable time** | **23.8** |

**A 40-hour week holds about 24 hours of plannable work — 60% of nominal.**
Across a team of five, 200 nominal hours are 119 plannable ones.

This is why "we're only at 80% capacity" is so often wrong. A manager who
commits 160 hours of planned work to that team believes they are at 80%; they
have actually committed **134%** of real capacity, and the overflow will be
paid for in missed deadlines, evenings, or quality — usually whichever is
least visible.

Now the second trap, distribution. Suppose the same team is committed to 96
hours — a genuine 81% of the 119 available, which looks healthy:

| | Committed | Share of that person's 23.8h |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 31h | **130%** |
| B | 26h | **109%** |
| C | 20h | 84% |
| D | 12h | 50% |
| E | 7h | 29% |
| **Team** | **96h** | **81%** |

The team average is comfortable and two people are underwater. This is not a
hypothetical shape — it is the normal one, because reliable people attract
work: they say yes, they deliver, so the next thing goes to them too. A is
almost always the person you would least like to lose.

Neither problem is visible in a total. Both are obvious the moment you write
the per-person column, which takes about ten minutes and is the single
highest-return planning habit in this chapter.

## Priorities: decide so the team doesn't have to

When everything is urgent, each person quietly decides their own priorities —
and the decisions won't match. Setting priority is your job:

- Keep a **single ranked order** for the team's significant work. Ranked
  means 1, 2, 3 — not "high/high/high".
- When new work arrives, place it in the order *explicitly*, and say what
  moves down as a result. Priority calls that don't displace anything are
  just wishes.
- Make the trade-off visible upward too: "We can take this on; it pushes X
  to next month" is a professional answer, and it protects your team from
  absorbing every request as overtime.

Revisit the order on a fixed rhythm — a short weekly planning session is
enough for most teams — rather than re-arguing it ad hoc every day.

## Coverage: rotas people can live with

If your team provides any kind of continuous or on-call service, coverage is
part of organising the work. Three rules keep rotas fair and functional:

1. **Publish ahead.** People can arrange their lives around a rota they can
   see weeks out; last-minute rotas burn goodwill fast.
2. **Rotate the pain evenly.** Nights, weekends, holidays and unpopular
   shifts are distributed transparently. Track it; perceived unfairness is
   corrosive even when actual unfairness is small.
3. **Design for absence.** Every critical duty needs at least two people who
   can perform it. Single points of failure are a rota problem *and* a
   training problem — close them with deliberate cross-training, not hope.

## Handovers: where balls get dropped

Most dropped work is lost at a boundary: between shifts, between people,
between teams. A handover is not a courtesy; it is a transfer of ownership,
and it needs the same discipline as delegation:

- **Written, in a fixed format.** A short template beats prose: open items,
  status of each, next action, and anything unusual. Verbal-only handovers
  degrade with tiredness and time.
- **Explicit acceptance.** The receiver confirms they have it. Until then,
  the sender still owns it. "I mentioned it in the group chat" is not a
  handover.
- **Nothing implicit.** An experienced person's "obviously you'd check X" is
  not obvious at 6 a.m. to someone covering their first week. Write it down.

Audit your handover points occasionally: take a recent dropped ball and trace
where it fell. It is nearly always a boundary with no format, no acceptance,
or no owner.

## Meetings that organise, not consume

A short daily or every-other-day team check — each person: what's done,
what's next, what's blocked — keeps the board true and surfaces problems in
hours instead of days. Keep it to minutes, standing on the facts of the
board. Anything that becomes a discussion between two people moves out of the
meeting. Your weekly planning session then handles priorities and load with
everyone already sharing the same picture.

## Do this at work

1. **Compute your team's real capacity.** Nominal hours, minus meetings and
   admin, minus your historical share of unplanned work. Compare it to what
   you have committed.
2. **Write the per-person column.** Total capacity hides overload; the
   individual rows expose it. Look particularly at your most reliable person.
3. **Rebalance one person's load.** Do not wait for them to complain — the
   best ones do not complain until they resign.
4. **Check your unplanned-work share.** Look back over four weeks. If a third
   of the week goes to unplanned requests, committing 100% of capacity to
   planned work is a decision to miss deadlines.
5. **Publish the priority order.** If everything is urgent, each person is
   quietly deciding their own priorities, and they will not match yours.
6. **Fix one handover.** Find where work passes between people or shifts and
   ask what gets dropped there. Handovers are where balls fall unwitnessed.

## Summary

- Put all the team's work in one visible place with one owner per item;
  untracked work makes every other plan fiction.
- Plan against realistic capacity with a buffer for predictable unplanned
  work, and watch per-person load, not just totals.
- Maintain a single ranked priority order; new work explicitly displaces old.
- Publish rotas early, rotate unpopular duties transparently, and eliminate
  single points of failure with cross-training.
- Treat handovers as ownership transfers: written format, explicit
  acceptance, nothing implicit.
