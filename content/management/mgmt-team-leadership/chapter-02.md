# Delegation & Accountability

Delegation is the core mechanical skill of management. Done well, it
multiplies your team's capacity and grows your people. Done badly, it produces
either micromanagement (you delegate the task but keep all the decisions) or
abdication (you hand something over and discover at the deadline that it went
wrong weeks ago). This chapter gives you a repeatable way to hand over work so
that it actually comes back done.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Delegate outcomes with an explicit definition of done.
- Choose a delegation level for the task rather than the person, and say it
  out loud.
- Move a recurring responsibility up a level over time.
- Follow up on outcomes without re-litigating methods.
- Respond usefully when delegated work comes back wrong.

## Why delegating feels wrong — and why you must anyway

Every new manager hits the same objection: *"It's faster to do it myself."*
For any single task, that is usually true. But it optimises the wrong thing:

- Doing it yourself is faster **once**. Teaching someone is faster **every
  time after the first**.
- Every task only you can do is a queue with one server: you. Your absence,
  overload, or leave becomes the team's outage.
- People grow by doing work slightly beyond their current level, with support.
  If you keep all the stretch work, you keep all the growth.

The delegation test: if a task is recurring, teachable, and not genuinely
above your team's reach with support, it should be on its way to someone else.

## Defining done

Most delegation failures are actually *specification* failures. The task in
your head and the task the other person heard were different. Before handing
over any meaningful task, be explicit about four things:

1. **Outcome** — what exists when this is done, described concretely enough
   that both of you would recognise it. "Tidy the store room" fails this
   test; "every item shelved, labelled, and recorded in the register, aisle
   clear" passes.
2. **Constraints** — deadline, budget, quality standard, anything that must
   not change, and anything that must be escalated rather than decided.
3. **Authority** — what they may decide alone, and where the line is. This is
   the piece new managers most often forget, and its absence is why everything
   still comes back to you.
4. **Check-in** — when and how you will look at progress (see below), agreed
   up front so follow-up is routine rather than distrust.

Have the other person play the plan back in their own words. Thirty seconds
of playback catches most misunderstandings before they cost a week.

## Levels of delegation

Delegation is not all-or-nothing. Match the level to the person's experience
with *this kind of task* — not their seniority in general:

| Level | They do | You keep | Use when | Costs you |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Follow your steps | Every decision | Brand new to this task; safety-critical | Most — this is teaching time |
| 2 | Gather facts and options | The decision | They know the ground but not the judgement | A lot |
| 3 | Decide, tell you before acting | A veto | Judgement forming; consequences still large | Some |
| 4 | Act, then report | Awareness | Demonstrated competence on this task | Little |
| 5 | Own it entirely | Nothing routine | Proven, and the work is genuinely theirs | Nothing |

Two things this table is for. First, **the level belongs to the task, not the
person** — a fifteen-year veteran handling their first disciplinary case
belongs at level 2, and a new starter who has done a hundred of one particular
job belongs at level 4. Managers who set one level per person get both wrong
at once.

Second, the level is supposed to *move*. The goal for every recurring
responsibility is to climb one every few cycles. If someone has been at level
1 or 2 on the same task for months, that is a finding: either coach
differently, or reconsider the assignment. Holding them there costs them
growth and costs you the hours — and it is usually not a decision anyone made,
just a level nobody revisited.

The commonest failure is the invisible jump: delegating at level 4 while
privately expecting level 3, then being annoyed at an action taken without
consulting you. The person did exactly what was asked. **Say the level out
loud** — "decide it and tell me before you act" takes four seconds and
prevents the entire class of problem.

## One owner per task

Shared ownership is a polite name for no ownership. Every task, decision, and
follow-up should have exactly **one named owner** — the person who answers
for it, even when several people contribute to it. In meetings, never let an
action end as "we should…"; convert it on the spot: *who* will do *what* by
*when*. Write it down where the team can see it.

Ownership also means the owner reports status without being chased. Set that
expectation explicitly: "If it's going to be late, I want to hear it from you
before the deadline, not discover it after."

## Following up without micromanaging

The difference between follow-up and micromanagement is simple: follow-up is
**agreed in advance and looks at outcomes**; micromanagement is unannounced
and re-litigates methods.

- Set check-ins when you delegate, spaced to the risk. A routine task might
  get one check; a first-time, high-stakes task might get three.
- At a check-in, ask about the outcome and the blockers — resist redoing
  their approach unless it genuinely won't work or breaks a constraint.
- When you see a problem, name it as a question first: "Walk me through how
  this part will handle X." Half the time they have an answer you didn't see.
- Never silently take a task back. If you must reclaim work, say so
  explicitly and say why, or you teach the team that missed expectations are
  consequence-free — and that delegation from you is temporary theatre.

## When it comes back wrong

It will, sometimes. Your response determines whether delegation survives:

- Separate *specification* failures (you were unclear — own that) from
  *execution* failures (they had what they needed and didn't deliver).
- For execution failures, have them fix it rather than fixing it yourself,
  wherever the deadline allows. The redo is where the learning happens.
- Escalate your involvement, not your volume: move the task down a delegation
  level for the next cycle rather than performing frustration now.

## Do this at work

1. **Name the level for one delegation this week.** Say it explicitly:
   "decide it and tell me before you act." Four seconds, and it prevents the
   commonest delegation failure.
2. **Find someone stuck at level 2.** Same task, same level, months on end.
   Either coach differently or move the assignment — both beat leaving it.
3. **Write "done" for one task.** What it looks like, by when, and what
   "good" includes. Most delegation failures are definition failures.
4. **Check your ownership list.** Any task owned by "the team" is owned by
   nobody. Put one name against each.
5. **Agree a check-in in advance.** Then do not check in between. That single
   discipline is the whole difference between follow-up and micromanagement.
6. **Debrief one thing that came back wrong.** Was the brief unclear, the
   level too high, or the skill missing? Each has a different fix, and
   guessing wrong makes it worse.

## Summary

- Delegate recurring, teachable work even when doing it yourself is faster —
  you are buying capacity and growth, not saving minutes.
- Specify outcome, constraints, authority, and check-ins; get a playback.
- Use delegation levels deliberately and move work upward through them.
- Every task has exactly one named owner, with who/what/when written down.
- Follow up on the schedule you agreed, on outcomes not methods, and never
  silently take work back.
