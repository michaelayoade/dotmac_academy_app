# Leading Under Pressure

Every team eventually has its bad day: the major outage, the lost shipment,
the deadline that suddenly halves, the customer crisis at 5 p.m. on Friday.
Pressure does not build character so much as reveal systems — teams with
clear roles and rhythms perform; teams held together by improvisation crack.
This chapter covers leading during a crisis, communicating while it runs, and
converting the mess into improvement afterwards.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Change what you personally do when a crisis starts.
- Protect the team's thinking under pressure instead of adding to the noise.
- Communicate on a rhythm while the situation is still unresolved.
- Run a blameless review that produces changes rather than blame.
- Notice the human cost a crisis leaves behind.

## Your job changes during a crisis

Under normal conditions you delegate broadly and optimise for growth. In a
genuine crisis, the structure temporarily tightens:

- **One incident lead.** Somebody — often but not always you — visibly runs
  the response: sets priorities, assigns actions, and is the single point
  where information converges. Committees do not resolve crises.
- **Triage before action.** Sixty seconds of "what do we know, what is the
  impact, what is the fastest way to stop the bleeding" beats an immediate
  scramble. Stabilise first; root-cause later. The elegant permanent fix is
  tomorrow's work — today's work is stopping the damage.
- **Short cycles.** Assign small actions, reconvene fast ("check X, back in
  10 minutes"), reassess. Long silent stretches in a crisis usually mean
  people are stuck or duplicating effort.
- **Log as you go.** One person keeps rough notes: timeline, decisions,
  actions taken. Memory reconstructs badly after adrenaline, and the review
  afterwards depends on this record.

Equally important is recognising what is *not* a crisis. A manager who
declares emergencies weekly trains the team to ignore the real one. Reserve
crisis mode for genuine, significant, time-critical impact — and end it
explicitly when the situation is stable, so the team knows normal rules have
resumed.

## Protect the thinking

Stress narrows people — including you. Your added value as the leader under
pressure is mostly *calm sequencing*:

- Speak more slowly than you feel. The team calibrates its panic to yours;
  composure is contagious in both directions.
- Take the blame-search off the table immediately: "how it happened is for
  later; right now we fix it." Fear of blame makes people hide exactly the
  information you need most.
- Watch for tunnel vision — the whole team clustering on one theory while
  contrary evidence waits unread. Deliberately ask: "what else could this
  be?" and "what does that observation *not* explain?"
- Feed and rotate people in long incidents. Two tired people alternating
  beat four exhausted heroes, and the error rate of hour twelve undoes the
  work of hour two.

## Communicating while it burns

During a crisis you have two audiences with opposite needs, and both go wrong
by default:

**Outward and upward** — your boss, other teams, customers. They need a
steady rhythm of honest, bounded updates: what happened, current impact,
what is being done, when the next update comes. Set the interval explicitly
("update every 30 minutes") and *keep it even when there is no news* —
"still working, next update at 3:00" prevents the anxious pinging that
otherwise consumes your responders. Never promise a fix time you do not
have; give the time of your next update instead.

**Inward** — the team. They need priorities and role clarity, not an anxious
audience. Shield them from the upward pressure you are absorbing; relaying
every escalated "is it fixed yet?" into the room adds stress and zero
information.

If the crisis touches customers, err toward telling them early and plainly.
Trust survives incidents; it rarely survives discovered concealment.

## Afterwards: the blameless review

The crisis pays for itself only if you collect the lesson. Within a few days
— while memory is fresh but tempers are cool — run a short review with
everyone involved. The ground rule that makes it work: **assume everyone
acted reasonably on the information they had**. The question is never "who
failed?" but "what made that failure possible, and what makes it impossible
or survivable next time?"

Structure it simply:

1. Reconstruct the timeline from the incident log, facts first.
2. Identify what went well — responses worth keeping are as valuable as
   faults worth fixing.
3. Trace the causes past the first answer (Chapter 3 of the Decision Making
   course covers root-cause tools that apply directly here).
4. Agree a *small number* of concrete improvements, each with one owner and
   a date. Three fixes that happen beat fifteen that don't.

Then actually track the actions. A review whose findings evaporate teaches
the team that reviews are theatre — and the same incident will happen again,
minus the goodwill.

## Watch the human ledger

Sustained pressure has a cost that invoices later: the quiet extra hours, the
skipped breaks, the same reliable people absorbing every emergency. After an
intense period, deliberately pay it down — time off actually taken, workload
visibly rebalanced, and thanks that are specific and public. Burnout rarely
announces itself; it presents as your steadiest person becoming cynical,
error-prone, or silent. By then you are late. The cheapest intervention is
noticing early, and the second cheapest is asking.

## Do this at work

1. **Write your crisis role.** Before the next one: what will you personally
   do, and what will you deliberately not do? Managers who grab a tool are
   one more pair of hands and one fewer coordinator.
2. **Set a communication rhythm now.** Decide in advance how often you will
   update during an incident, even when there is nothing new. Silence gets
   filled with worse information.
3. **Name a single decision-maker.** For your most likely failure scenario,
   decide now who runs it. Choosing under pressure wastes the first hour.
4. **Run one blameless review.** What happened, what made it possible, what
   changes. If the output is a person's name, the review has not finished.
5. **Check the human ledger.** After the last hard stretch, who worked the
   most unreasonable hours? Say thank you specifically, and give the time
   back before it becomes resentment.

## Summary

- In a real crisis: one visible lead, triage before action, short assign-and-
  reconvene cycles, and a running log. Declare crisis mode rarely and end it
  explicitly.
- Stay calmer than you feel, ban blame-hunting until after, and guard
  against tunnel vision and exhaustion.
- Communicate on a fixed rhythm: bounded honest updates outward, priorities
  inward, next-update times instead of guessed fix times.
- Review blamelessly within days: timeline, what went well, causes, and a
  few owned, dated fixes that you then track to done.
- Pay down the human cost of every crisis deliberately, and watch your most
  reliable people hardest.
