# Measuring Experience

You cannot manage what you only feel. Every service team believes it knows
how customers feel — and every team that starts measuring discovers
surprises, usually uncomfortable ones. This chapter covers the practical
measurement toolkit: the standard metrics and what each actually tells you,
the complaint analytics you already own, and the discipline that separates
measurement from wallpaper — closing the loop.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Choose between CSAT, NPS and effort for the question you actually have.
- Publish a response rate beside every score.
- Use the operational data you already own as experience measurement.
- Close the loop with the individual customer and with the process.
- Recognise a measurement that has been gamed.

## The standard gauges

Three metrics dominate experience measurement worldwide. None is magic;
each answers a different question:

- **CSAT (customer satisfaction).** "How satisfied were you with X?" —
  asked right after a specific interaction: the installation, the support
  call, the repair. Transactional and diagnostic: it tells you how *that
  process* is performing, quickly, in volume. Its limit: satisfaction with
  one interaction says little about the whole relationship.
- **NPS (net promoter score).** "How likely are you to recommend us?" —
  scored 0–10; the standard scoring treats 9–10 as promoters, 0–6 as
  detractors, and reports promoters-minus-detractors. It gauges the overall
  relationship and correlates with referral behaviour. Its limits are real:
  it's a lagging, blunt instrument, sensitive to when and whom you ask —
  treat its *trend* and its *verbatim comments* as the value, not the
  number itself.
- **Customer effort.** "How easy did we make it to get this handled?" —
  the strongest known predictor of disloyalty in service interactions, per
  Chapter 1's reliability-effort-feeling ordering. High-effort experiences
  (repeats, chases, transfers) drive customers out even when the eventual
  outcome was good.

Three habits make any of them worth collecting. **Ask few questions, often,
at the moment** — a two-question survey after the event beats a twenty-
question annual one nobody finishes. **Read the comments** — the score is
a thermometer; the verbatims are the diagnosis. And **never weaponise the
scores against individual staff** by default — the fastest way to corrupt
a metric is to make someone's salary depend on begging customers for tens,
and begged scores measure begging, not experience.

Side by side, so you can pick rather than collect all three by default:

| | CSAT | NPS | Customer effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Asks | "How satisfied were you with X?" | "How likely are you to recommend us?" | "How easy did we make it?" |
| About | One interaction | The whole relationship | One interaction |
| When | Immediately after the event | Periodically | Immediately after the event |
| Best for | Diagnosing a specific process | Tracking relationship trend | Predicting who will leave |
| Blind to | The relationship beyond this event | Which process caused the score | Whether the outcome was right |
| Fails when | Asked too late to recall | Asked of the wrong sample, or read as a number rather than a trend | Treated as a satisfaction score |

The practical shape for most businesses is **effort or CSAT after
interactions that matter, plus NPS on a slow cycle for trend** — not all
three, everywhere, forever.

### The response rate belongs beside the score

One number makes or breaks all three, and it is almost never published: how
many people answered.

| | Team A | Team B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Average score | **4.8** | 4.3 |
| Responses | 6% of customers | 61% of customers |

Team A's 4.8 is the opinion of the few percent motivated enough to reply,
which skews to the delighted and the furious. Team B's 4.3 is close to what
its customers actually think. Reported as bare scores, A wins a comparison it
should lose, and the manager who quietly stopped chasing responses has been
rewarded for it.

**Publish the response rate next to every score, always.** It costs one extra
column and it is the difference between a measurement and a decoration.

## The measurements you already own

Before adding surveys, mine what your operations already record:

- **Complaint analytics** (Chapter 2's learned-from state): complaints per
  hundred customers, by category, trending over time. Twelve complaints
  about billing this quarter versus three last quarter is a finding no
  survey will hand you faster. Categorise at capture — a taxonomy of ten
  honest categories beats fifty precise ones nobody applies consistently.
- **Repeat contacts.** The customer who contacts you twice about the same
  issue is a first-contact-resolution failure and an effort signal, visible
  entirely from your own records.
- **Operational truth.** SLA hit rates (Chapter 3), time-to-resolve,
  first-response times. These are experience measurements — the customer
  experiences your operations, not your intentions.
- **Silence patterns.** Customers who stop using, stop renewing, stop
  responding. Attrition data is the experience metric with the highest
  stakes and no survey fatigue; by the time it moves, the softer signals
  already knew.

Together these form a simple dashboard any manager can run monthly:
satisfaction trend, effort signals, complaint categories, SLA performance,
attrition. One page. The point is not sophistication; it's *regularity* —
the same few numbers, honestly tracked, month after month.

## Closing the loop — twice

Measurement earns nothing until it changes something. The discipline has an
inner and an outer loop:

- **The inner loop: act on the individual signal.** The detractor score,
  the bad CSAT, the bitter comment — someone contacts that customer,
  within days, to understand and where possible to fix. This is Chapter
  3's recovery triggered by measurement instead of complaint — and it
  routinely converts your angriest scorers into your most loyal customers,
  because being *followed up on* is the rarest experience in commerce.
- **The outer loop: fix the pattern.** Monthly, the dashboard's worst
  recurring theme gets a root-cause treatment (the Decision Making
  course's Chapter 3 applies directly) and one operational fix with an
  owner and a date. Then — the step nearly everyone skips — *tell
  customers and staff what changed*: "you said X, we did Y." It converts
  surveys from an imposition into a conversation, and staff cynicism about
  metrics into evidence that the numbers do something.

The anti-pattern, everywhere, is the dashboard that is reviewed, lamented,
and filed — measurement as ritual. If the last three months of scores
produced no operational change, you don't have a measurement programme;
you have a subscription to bad news.

## An honest word about gaming

Every measured number invites management of the number instead of the
thing. Surveys sent only to happy customers; complaints reclassified as
"enquiries"; tickets closed and reopened to reset clocks. Assume the
temptation exists, audit occasionally against raw reality (call some
customers yourself; read raw tickets), and — the deeper fix — keep the
metrics low-stakes for individuals and high-stakes for *decisions*. The
Managing with Data course returns to this at length; for experience metrics
the rule is simple: the customer's actual experience is the referee, and
any number that drifts from it has stopped being information.

## Do this at work

1. **Add the response rate column.** To every experience score your
   organisation publishes. It is one column and it changes what the numbers
   mean.
2. **Pick the right gauge for one question.** If you want to fix a process,
   effort or CSAT after the event beats an annual relationship score.
3. **Read fifty verbatims.** The score is a thermometer; the comments are
   the diagnosis. Most organisations collect them and nobody reads them.
4. **Use data you already have.** Repeat contacts, cancellations, time to
   resolve, failed first visits. No survey required, and no response bias.
5. **Close one loop both ways.** Go back to a customer who complained, and
   change the process that caused it. Doing only the first is politeness;
   doing both is management.
6. **Look for begged scores.** If anyone's pay depends on a rating, you are
   measuring begging. Check before trusting the trend.

## Summary

- CSAT diagnoses interactions, NPS gauges the relationship, effort
  predicts disloyalty — ask few questions, at the moment, and read the
  verbatims.
- Your operations already measure experience: complaint categories, repeat
  contacts, SLA performance, attrition. One honest page, monthly.
- Close the loop twice: follow up individual bad signals within days, and
  convert the worst monthly pattern into one owned operational fix.
- Tell customers and staff what changed because of what was measured —
  that's what keeps both the scores and the culture honest.
- Keep metrics low-stakes for individuals, high-stakes for decisions, and
  audit against raw reality; a gamed number is worse than no number.
