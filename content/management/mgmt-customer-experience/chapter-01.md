# The Customer Journey

Every business believes it treats customers well; far fewer have ever looked
at their own service the way a customer experiences it. Customers don't see
your departments, your systems, or your org chart — they see one continuous
journey, and they judge you on its weakest moment. This chapter builds the
habit of seeing that journey whole, finding where experience is won and lost,
and understanding why the moments you overlook are usually the ones that
decide.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Map a customer journey across its touchpoints rather than defending one.
- Identify the moments of truth that carry disproportionate weight.
- Treat experience as a system produced by process, not by attitude.
- Name who owns the journey end to end.

## One journey, many touchpoints

A **touchpoint** is any moment a customer interacts with your business: the
first advert or recommendation, the enquiry, the signup or purchase, delivery
or activation, the first use, the first bill, the first problem, the renewal.
Map them in order for your own business and two things become obvious:

- **The customer crosses your internal boundaries constantly.** Sales hands
  to operations, operations to billing, billing to support. Each handover is
  invisible to you and *visible to the customer* — as a repeated question, a
  dropped promise, or a "that's another department." Journeys fail at the
  seams far more often than at the stations.
- **Some touchpoints matter far more than others.** Customers don't average
  their experience; they remember **peaks and endings** — the best or worst
  moment, and the most recent one. A year of quiet good service can be
  erased in memory by one badly-handled problem; equally, one brilliantly
  handled failure is remembered for years.

The practical exercise, worth doing annually and after any process change:
walk your own journey as a customer. Sign up as one, call your own support
line, receive your own bill. What you find is reliably humbling.

## Moments of truth

Certain touchpoints carry decisive weight — call them moments of truth:

- **The first delivery of the promise.** The gap between what sales said
  and what was delivered sets the customer's trust baseline permanently.
  Overpromising at signup is borrowing experience from the future at
  punishing interest.
- **The first problem.** Counterintuitively, a well-handled problem often
  produces *higher* loyalty than no problem at all — because it's the only
  moment the customer sees how you behave when things are hard. Chapter 3
  builds on this.
- **Moments of vulnerability.** The customer who just moved house, lost a
  service they depend on, or doesn't understand the product. How you treat
  people at low power is what your reputation is actually made of.
- **The ending.** Cancellations and departures handled gracefully seed
  returns and referrals; departures made difficult guarantee the loudest
  negative stories your brand will ever earn.

## Experience is a system, not a smile

The instinct is to fix experience with friendliness training. But customers
rarely leave because someone was insufficiently cheerful; they leave because
**something didn't work and nobody seemed to own it**. Politeness is the
paint; the structure underneath is:

- **Reliability first.** The core promise, kept, repeatedly. No warmth
  compensates for a product that keeps failing or a promise that keeps
  slipping.
- **Effort second.** How hard must the customer work to get value or get
  help? Repeating their story to three people, chasing you for updates,
  navigating a menu maze — every unit of customer effort is experience
  burned. The single most predictive question in service research is not
  "were we excellent?" but "did we make it easy?"
- **Feeling third.** Courtesy, being heard, small acknowledgements — real,
  but they sit on top of the other two, never in place of them.

This ordering tells you where to invest: fix the thing that fails, then
reduce the effort of dealing with you, then polish the warmth.

## Who owns the journey?

Every department owns its own touchpoints, which is precisely why nobody
owns the seams between them — and the seams are where journeys die. Someone
— a role, not a committee — must own the journey end-to-end: mapping it,
measuring it (Chapter 5), and having the standing to make departments fix
their handovers. In a small business that owner is often simply the manager
who cares; in a larger one it's a named responsibility. The test of real
ownership: when a customer's problem spans three departments, is there one
person whose job is unambiguously to see it resolved? If the answer is no,
Chapter 2 is about fixing exactly that.

## Do this at work

1. **Walk one journey yourself.** Buy, enquire, or report a fault as a
   customer would. What you notice in an hour is usually not in any report.
2. **List the touchpoints.** Every point where a customer meets your
   organisation. The ones nobody owns are where the experience fails.
3. **Find your moments of truth.** Which two or three interactions decide how
   the whole relationship is remembered? Those deserve disproportionate care.
4. **Fix one systemic cause.** Take a recurring complaint and ask what
   process produces it. Training people to apologise better is not a fix.
5. **Name the journey owner.** If no single person owns the end-to-end
   experience, the handoffs between departments are nobody's problem — which
   is precisely where customers feel the damage.

## Summary

- Customers experience one continuous journey and judge it by its weakest
  moment; map your touchpoints and walk them yourself.
- Journeys fail at the seams — the handovers between your departments that
  customers see and you don't.
- Peaks and endings dominate memory; treat first deliveries, first
  problems, moments of vulnerability, and departures as the high-stakes
  moments they are.
- Experience is reliability, then ease, then warmth — in that order; a
  smile never compensates for a broken promise or high effort.
- The journey needs one owner with the standing to fix the seams.
