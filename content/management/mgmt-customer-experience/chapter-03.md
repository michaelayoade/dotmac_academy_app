# Service Commitments

Customers can live with almost any honest limitation; what they cannot live
with is not knowing what to expect. Service commitments — what you promise,
how you measure it, and what happens when you miss — are how a business
converts good intentions into something a customer can rely on. This chapter
covers setting commitments deliberately, keeping them honestly, and
recovering well when you fail, which you sometimes will.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Set promises you can keep rather than promises that win the moment.
- Keep the promise visible to the customer without them chasing.
- Recover a failure so the relationship survives it.
- Price a goodwill gesture against lifetime value.
- Say when generosity has become rent paid to avoid a fix.

## Promise deliberately, not optimistically

Every business makes service promises constantly — in contracts, in adverts,
in a frontline employee's cheerful "that'll be done by Friday." Three rules
govern them all:

- **A commitment is a decision, not a hope.** Promise what your *system*
  can deliver on a bad week, not what your best person managed once on a
  good one. The gap between marketing's promise and operations' reality is
  paid for at the service desk, one disappointed customer at a time.
- **Specific beats vague — for you as much as them.** "Within two working
  days" can be measured, managed, and improved; "as soon as possible"
  cannot, and quietly means never. Vague promises feel safe to give and are
  actually the most dangerous, because every customer resolves the
  ambiguity in their own favour.
- **Under-promise margins are honesty, not sandbagging.** If the realistic
  time is three days, promising three and delivering in two delights;
  promising one and delivering in two — the same two days — infuriates.
  Experience is measured against expectation, and *you* set the
  expectation. Managing it is half the job.

Formal service-level agreements (SLAs) simply write these decisions down:
the commitment, the measurement, the exclusions, and the remedy on a miss.
The discipline of writing them is valuable even where no customer demands a
contract — it forces the promise-makers and the promise-keepers to agree.

## Keep the promise visible

A commitment you don't measure is a decoration. For each real commitment:
track performance against it, make misses visible internally *before*
customers report them, and treat systematic misses as an operations problem
to fix or a promise to renegotiate — never as normal background noise. The
moment a team internally accepts "yes, we say two days but it's really
four," the business is training its own staff that promises are decorative;
customers learn the same lesson shortly after.

When you *know* you'll miss — the part is delayed, the technician is ill —
**tell the customer before they find out**. A proactive "we're going to be
late, here's the new time" is a kept meta-promise: it says we're watching
your case. The same lateness discovered by the customer chasing you says
the opposite. This one habit, costing minutes, moves more satisfaction
scores than most service investments.

## Service recovery: the miss is a moment of truth

Chapter 1 noted the paradox: a well-recovered failure often builds *more*
loyalty than flawless service — the failure is the only time the customer
sees your character rather than your process. Recovery has a reliable
anatomy:

1. **Acknowledge fast and specifically.** Name what went wrong. The
   non-apology ("we regret any inconvenience that may have occurred") reads
   as evasion because it is.
2. **Fix the customer's actual situation first** — before root cause,
   before fault-finding. What do they need right now to be whole?
3. **Make good proportionately.** A remedy should cost you something
   visible — a credit, a redo, a genuine upgrade — scaled to the harm. A
   trivial gesture for a serious failure insults; the remedy is how the
   customer measures your sincerity.
4. **Say what changes.** "Here's what we've fixed so this doesn't repeat"
   converts a private apology into evidence of a business that learns —
   and links straight into Chapter 2's learned-from state.

Budget for recovery in advance and pre-authorise the frontline (Chapter 2's
bounded authority) to grant remedies. Recovery delayed by approval-hunting
is recovery halved.

## The economics of generosity — and its limits

Service recovery generosity is not charity; it's the arithmetic of Chapter
1's peaks-and-endings memory. The customer made whole after a failure
becomes a story told in your favour; the customer nickel-and-dimed after a
failure becomes the other kind of story, told more often and louder. In
subscription and repeat-purchase businesses especially, the credit that
feels expensive today is trivial against the lifetime value it protects.

### Worked example: pricing a goodwill credit

A customer on the ₦16,200 subscription from the Finance course suffers a
three-day outage. They ask for compensation. The service manager, guarding
the monthly margin of ₦8,200, offers a ₦1,600 credit — three days' pro rata,
technically correct and visibly grudging.

Put the two options beside the thing they actually affect:

| | Grudging credit | Generous recovery |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Credit given | ₦1,600 | ₦8,100 (half a month) |
| Cost this month | ₦1,600 | ₦8,100 |
| **Lifetime value at risk** | **₦164,000** | **₦164,000** |
| Extra cost of the generous option | | **₦6,500** |

The difference between the two gestures is **₦6,500 — about 4% of what the
customer is worth over their 20-month lifespan.** And it is spent at the
exact moment the customer is deciding which kind of story to tell about you.

Now the acquisition side. Replacing that customer costs a full acquisition
effort — at the Sales course's ratio, roughly seventeen conversations. The
recovery costs one phone call and ₦6,500. A manager who declines the ₦6,500
to protect this month's margin has, in expectation, spent far more than they
saved, and has done it invisibly, which is why the decision keeps getting
made that way.

Two limits, and they are real. **This arithmetic only works on the
exception.** Run the same numbers on a business with a recurring failure and
generosity becomes a subscription to a defect: ₦8,100 a month, indefinitely,
against a fix that might cost ₦200,000 once. When the miss is the rule, the
credit is not recovery — it is rent paid to avoid an operational decision.

And **the arithmetic must not become an incentive to complain**. A customer
whose every month contains a claim is not being recovered; they are being
farmed. Proportion, records, and pattern-awareness keep the policy sane —
which is a reason to record recovery decisions, not merely to make them.

## Do this at work

1. **Find one promise you routinely miss.** Change the promise, not the
   apology. A date you keep 95% of the time beats a date that wins the sale.
2. **Make one status visible.** Where customers currently chase you for
   progress, publish it instead. Chasing is effort, and effort predicts
   leaving.
3. **Price one recovery.** Take a recent goodwill decision and compare the
   gesture to the customer's lifetime value. The ratio is usually startling.
4. **Check for rented patience.** Find a failure you compensate for
   repeatedly. Cost a year of those credits against fixing the cause once.
5. **Give one frontline recovery budget.** A defined amount staff may spend
   to fix things on the spot, without asking. Then review how it was used
   rather than approving each case.

## Summary

- Commit to what the system delivers on a bad week; specific, measurable
  promises only — vagueness is resolved in the customer's favour.
- Expectation-setting is half of experience: honest margins delight,
  optimistic promises manufacture disappointment from identical service.
- Measure every real commitment, surface misses internally first, and
  warn customers before they discover lateness themselves.
- Recover with the full anatomy: fast specific acknowledgement, fix the
  person first, proportionate make-good, and say what changes.
- Generosity in recovery is sound economics with two limits: proportion,
  and never letting vouchers substitute for fixing a repeating defect.
