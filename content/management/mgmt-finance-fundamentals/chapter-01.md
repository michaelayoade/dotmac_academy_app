# How a Business Makes Money

Most managers run a piece of a business without ever being shown the money
machine as a whole — and then wonder why finance keeps saying no. This
chapter builds the whole picture in plain language: where money comes from,
where it goes, why "we're selling a lot" and "we're making money" are
different claims, and the handful of terms that let you hold your own in any
budget conversation.

## Revenue: the top of the machine

**Revenue** is the money customers pay you — the total value of what you
sold in a period, before anything is taken out. Businesses earn it in a few
recognisable shapes, each with its own management character:

- **One-off sales** — a product or job sold, paid, done. Every month
  starts at zero; the pipeline (see the Sales course) is life.
- **Recurring revenue** — subscriptions, contracts, retainers. Each month
  starts at last month's base minus cancellations; predictable, and
  brutally sensitive to retention (Chapter 3).
- **Usage revenue** — billed by consumption. Scales with customer
  activity; harder to predict, worth watching for concentration in a few
  heavy users.

Two revenue truths every manager needs. First, **revenue is not cash** — a
sale on credit is revenue today and cash whenever the customer actually
pays, an interval where businesses die (Chapter 4). Second, **not all
revenue is equal** — revenue that recurs, or that arrives without heavy
discounts or heroic effort, is worth more per unit than revenue bought with
margin giveaways and burnout.

## Costs: fixed, variable, and the shape of risk

Everything the business spends divides, usefully, two ways.

By **behaviour**:

- **Fixed costs** stay roughly the same whether you sell a lot or a little:
  rent, salaries, insurance, software licences. They are the treadmill —
  the amount you must earn *before anything counts*.
- **Variable costs** rise with each unit sold: materials, transaction
  fees, delivery, per-job labour. Each sale brings revenue and drags its
  variable costs with it.

The gap between a sale's revenue and its variable costs is its
**contribution** — what that sale contributes toward the fixed-cost
treadmill and, after the treadmill is covered, toward profit. This gives
you one of the most useful pictures in business: the **break-even point**,
the sales volume at which total contribution exactly covers fixed costs.
Below it, every month loses money regardless of effort; above it, each
additional sale's contribution falls almost straight through to profit —
which is why volume beyond break-even feels so different from volume before
it, and why high-fixed-cost businesses are dangerous at low volume and
wonderful at high volume.

By **purpose**, finance splits spending differently: **operating expense
(opex)** — the recurring cost of running (salaries, rent, fuel) — versus
**capital expenditure (capex)** — buying assets that serve for years
(vehicles, equipment, buildings). The distinction matters to you
practically: capex is usually a bigger approval gate but is spread
("depreciated") over the asset's life in the accounts, while opex hits the
current period in full. When finance asks "is that capex or opex?", they're
asking whether you're buying a durable asset or consuming a resource.

## Profit: several floors of the same building

"Profit" alone is ambiguous; the layers each answer a different question:

- **Gross profit** = revenue minus the direct costs of what you sold. *Is
  the thing we sell fundamentally worth selling?*
- **Operating profit** = gross profit minus overheads (the fixed running
  costs). *Is the business as a whole run economically?*
- **Net profit** = what remains after everything, including interest and
  tax. *What did the owners actually earn?*

Expressed as percentages of revenue, these are **margins**, and margins are
the language of comparison — across products, months, and business lines.
A manager who knows their area's gross margin can answer the daily
questions that stump most: what a discount really costs (it comes straight
out of the thin margin slice, not the big revenue number), why a
low-margin, high-effort product line deserves scepticism, and why "we made
it up on volume" needs actual arithmetic before applause.

## Why busy businesses still fail

Put the pieces together and the classic failure modes explain themselves:
selling below true cost (revenue up, contribution negative — scale just
loses money faster); the fixed-cost treadmill outgrowing realistic volume
(nice offices, wrong break-even); margin leaking through undisciplined
discounts; and profitable-on-paper businesses dying of cash timing
(Chapter 4). None of these announce themselves in the shop-floor bustle —
busy is not the same as profitable, and the numbers are where the
difference shows first.

## Summary

- Revenue is what customers pay — not cash until collected, and not all
  of equal quality; recurring, full-margin revenue is worth the most.
- Fixed costs are the treadmill, variable costs travel with each sale;
  contribution per sale must cover the treadmill before profit begins —
  know your break-even.
- Capex buys durable assets, opex runs the period; expect different
  approval gates.
- Gross, operating, and net profit answer different questions; margins are
  the comparison language, and discounts come out of margin, not revenue.
- Busy ≠ profitable: below-cost selling, treadmill bloat, margin leaks,
  and cash timing kill busy businesses — the numbers show it first.
