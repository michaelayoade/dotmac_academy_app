# Billing, Collections & Cash Flow

Profit is an opinion about a period; **cash is a fact about today**. A
business can be profitable on paper and unable to pay Friday's wages —
and businesses die of exactly that, routinely, while their income
statements look fine. This chapter covers the disciplines that keep money
actually arriving: billing that invites payment, collections without
drama, and the manager's working view of cash flow.

## Cash flow: the timing layer under the profit numbers

Chapter 1 flagged it; here is the mechanism. Revenue is recognised when
you earn it; cash arrives when customers pay. Costs behave the same way in
reverse. **Cash flow is the net of actual arrivals and departures**, and
its timing routinely diverges from profit:

- You buy stock or equipment now, sell over months — cash out long before
  revenue.
- You deliver now, invoice, and wait — revenue now, cash in thirty, sixty,
  ninety days… or never.
- You grow fast — every new customer brings acquisition and setup costs
  today against margin that arrives over months (Chapter 2's payback
  period). **Growth eats cash first and pays it back later**, which is
  why expanding businesses, counterintuitively, are the ones that run
  out.

The working tool is not the annual statement but a simple **rolling cash
view**: expected ins and outs, week by week, a few months forward. Its job
is to make the crunch visible while options still exist — delay a
purchase, chase receivables, arrange finance *before* the gap. A cash
crisis foreseen is a planning task; discovered, it's an emergency at the
worst possible negotiating position, because everyone can smell the
desperation.

## Billing: the invoice is a service touchpoint

Money arrives no faster than billing sends for it, and billing quality is
one of the most underrated levers in any business:

- **Bill promptly and accurately.** Every day between delivery and invoice
  is an interest-free loan to the customer that *you* initiated. And every
  billing error costs three times: the rework, the delayed payment (the
  disputed invoice waits at the bottom of their pile), and the trust —
  per the Customer Experience course, incomprehensible or wrong bills are
  a top complaint category everywhere.
- **Make the bill understandable.** A customer who understands what
  they're paying for pays faster and argues less. Line items in plain
  language, no surprises that weren't agreed — Chapter 4 of Customer
  Experience called this a right; here it's also self-interest.
- **Make paying easy.** Every step between "decided to pay" and "paid" is
  friction that delays cash: offer the channels your customers actually
  use, put the payment details on the invoice itself, confirm receipt
  promptly. In subscription businesses, failed automatic payments deserve
  a specific, monitored retry-and-contact routine — a large share of
  "non-payment" churn is just an expired card nobody chased.

## Collections: firm, early, and human

Receivables age badly — the older a debt, the lower the odds it's ever
paid. Effective collection is therefore mostly *earliness*, not toughness:

- **Set terms before the work.** Payment timing, deposits for large jobs,
  what happens on lateness — agreed up front, in writing. The moment to
  establish payment expectations is while you still have leverage — before
  delivery — and deposit-taking is qualification (Sales course): the
  customer who resists any commitment is telling you something.
- **Follow up on a fixed rhythm.** A reminder just before due date, a
  contact at due date, then an escalating sequence — polite, factual,
  unembarrassed, automatic. Most late payment is disorganisation, not
  refusal, and a business that visibly notices lateness gets paid before
  the ones that don't. The rhythm matters more than the wording; what
  kills collections is the awkward silence of a manager who feels rude
  asking for their own money.
- **Escalate deliberately.** Personal contact from the manager, then
  agreed consequences: paused service, payment plans for genuine hardship
  (partial reliably paid beats total never paid — and documented), and
  eventually the formal steps. Weigh the relationship honestly: some
  customers deserve patience; serial late-payers are unprofitable
  customers (Chapter 2) wearing a loyal face.
- **Watch concentration.** A large share of receivables in one customer
  is risk, not achievement; their cash problem becomes yours on no
  notice.

## The manager's cash instincts

Beyond the mechanics, the instincts that mark cash-literate managers:
treat cash timing as a term of every deal (a slightly lower price paid
now can genuinely beat a higher price paid in ninety days — and discounts
for early payment are purchasable cash acceleration); respect the
asymmetry that your suppliers' invoices are someone else's receivables
(paying chronically late is borrowing your reputation); and when the
rolling view shows a gap, act at once — the cheap options (deferring
spending, accelerating billing, arranging a line of credit) are all
early options. Profit is the destination; cash is the road condition.
Managers who watch only the destination drive into the pothole.

## Summary

- Profit is periodic opinion, cash is daily fact; growth eats cash first
  — keep a rolling weekly cash view months ahead so crunches are planning
  tasks, not emergencies.
- Bill promptly, accurately, and comprehensibly; every billing delay and
  error is self-inflicted slow payment.
- Make paying easy, and chase failed automatic payments — much
  "non-payment" is an expired card nobody contacted.
- Collect early on a fixed, unembarrassed rhythm; terms and deposits
  before the work; escalate deliberately with documented payment plans.
- Old debts rot, concentrated receivables are risk, and cash timing is a
  negotiable term of every deal — trade for it consciously.
