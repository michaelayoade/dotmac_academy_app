# Unit Economics

Chapter 1 looked at the business as one machine. This chapter zooms to the
level where decisions actually get made: the single unit — one customer,
one product, one job. If the unit makes money, scale multiplies profit; if
the unit loses money, scale multiplies loss, faster and more confidently.
Unit economics is the discipline of knowing which one is true *before*
scaling.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Choose the right unit for a business and list every cost that genuinely
  belongs to it.
- Compute margin per unit, CAC, payback period, and LTV from ordinary
  operating figures.
- Say how much extra volume a proposed discount actually requires — before
  agreeing to it.
- Spot the four places unit margin leaks in a working business.

## Choose your unit, then be honest about it

The right unit is whatever your business naturally repeats: a customer for
subscription and service businesses, a job for project work, an item for
products, a delivery for logistics. Per unit, you want three numbers:

- **Revenue per unit** — what the average unit actually pays, after
  discounts and freebies, not the list price. Averages hide spread: five
  premium customers and fifteen bargain-hunters make a misleading mean, so
  know the distribution too.
- **Cost per unit** — everything the unit genuinely consumes: direct
  costs, plus its fair share of the costs that scale with units (support
  time, delivery, payment fees). The classic self-deception is counting
  only materials and calling the rest "overhead" — service time,
  rework, and handling are real unit costs wearing camouflage.
- **Margin per unit** — the difference, absolute and as a percentage. This
  is the number that discount conversations should start from, and the
  number that identifies which products deserve promotion and which
  deserve retirement or repricing.

## Worked example: costing one customer

A service business bills a monthly subscription. List price is ₦18,000, but
after the discounts sales actually grants, the average customer pays
₦16,200. Finance says the margin is "about 64%" because direct delivery
cost is ₦6,500. Here is the honest costing:

| Line | Per customer per month | How it was worked out |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Revenue (realised, not list) | ₦16,200 | ₦18,000 list less the 10% average discount actually granted |
| Direct delivery cost | ₦6,500 | Bandwidth, materials, last-mile capacity |
| Support time | ₦600 | 0.6 contacts/month × 25 min × ₦2,400/hour loaded labour |
| Field visit (amortised) | ₦900 | One visit costing ₦9,000 every 10 months |
| Payment processing | ₦243 | 1.5% of realised revenue |
| **Total unit cost** | **₦8,243** | Everything that scales with one more customer |
| **Margin per unit** | **₦7,957** | 49% of realised revenue — not 64% |

The 15-point gap between "about 64%" and the real 49% is not an accounting
nicety. It is the difference between a discount you can afford and one that
eats the year. All fifteen points were hiding in costs someone called
overhead.

Now extend the same figures to acquisition. Last quarter the business spent
₦4,200,000 on advertising, sales salaries, and commissions, and gained 140
customers:

- **CAC** = ₦4,200,000 ÷ 140 = **₦30,000** per customer.
- **Payback period** = ₦30,000 ÷ ₦7,957 = **3.8 months**. Until month four,
  every new customer is an investment, not profit.
- **LTV** = ₦7,957 × 26 months of realistic average lifespan = **₦206,882**.
- **LTV : CAC** = 206,882 ÷ 30,000 = **6.9 : 1**.

That is a healthy unit — and note that nothing here required an accountant.
Four operating figures and a division produced the number that should gate
every growth decision the business makes this year.

## The acquisition question: what does a customer cost to get?

Every customer arrives at a price: advertising, sales time, commissions,
promos. Total acquisition spend divided by customers gained is **customer
acquisition cost (CAC)** — a number most small businesses have never
computed and are reliably shocked by. Sales effort is part of it: a
customer that took five site visits to close cost five visits' worth of
someone's paid time.

Acquisition cost only means something against what the customer *brings*:

- **Payback period** — how long the customer's monthly margin takes to
  repay their acquisition cost. A business acquiring aggressively with a
  long payback is buying future profit with present cash, which is fine
  exactly as long as the cash and the retention hold (Chapters 3 and 4).
- **Lifetime value (LTV)** — the margin a customer contributes over their
  whole stay. Estimate it honestly: monthly margin × realistic average
  lifespan, not hoped-for lifespan. A customer costing more to win than
  they'll ever contribute is charity with paperwork, however good the win
  feels.

The pair explains strategy shifts that otherwise look like fashion: why
mature businesses drift budget from acquisition to retention (Chapter 3
does the arithmetic), and why "growth" without unit economics is just
speed.

## The discount table every manager should memorise

Sales proposes a 10% price cut and promises 10% more volume in return. The
promise sounds proportionate. It almost never is, because the cut comes
out of the *margin* slice, not the price:

| Margin before | Margin after a 10% price cut | Extra volume needed to stand still |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 50% | 40% | +25% |
| 40% | 30% | +33% |
| 30% | 20% | +50% |
| 20% | 10% | +100% |
| 10% | 0% | impossible |

Read the last row before every pricing conversation: on a thin unit, a
"small" discount does not reduce profit, it deletes it. The worked
example's 49% unit survives a 10% cut with about 26% more volume — hard,
but arithmetic rather than fantasy. The same cut on the 20% unit next door
requires the sales team to double the business.

## Where the margin hides — and leaks

Unit-level thinking pays off in the unglamorous places:

- **The customer mix.** Averages conceal that some customer types are
  profitable and others, fully costed, are not — usually the high-touch,
  heavy-support, always-discounted ones. The point is not to fire them on
  sight but to *know*, then reprice, restructure service, or stop
  acquiring more of that type.
- **Serving cost creep.** Units that were profitable at design time
  accumulate costs quietly: more support contacts, longer visits, extra
  delivery attempts. In the worked example, support contacts rising from
  0.6 to 1.2 a month costs ₦600 — 8% of the margin — and no one would
  ever see it in a monthly P&L. Re-cost your units annually; the
  spreadsheet from two years ago is a historical document.
- **The discount ratchet.** Each "small" discount comes out of the margin
  slice, permanently, because prices ratchet down far more easily than
  up. Sellers who haven't done the table above give away in a sentence
  what operations saves all year.
- **The free extras.** The bonus visit, the waived fee, the extended
  support call — all real unit costs, all invisible until costed. Kindness
  is a fine strategy *when priced in*; unmeasured, it's a slow leak with
  good manners.

## Do this at work

Take one repeating unit from your own area and cost it this week. One
page, four steps:

1. **Name the unit and pull realised revenue** — what it actually collects
   over the last three months, after discounts, divided by the count. Not
   list price.
2. **List every cost that would disappear if that unit vanished.** Include
   support minutes, travel, rework, payment fees. If you argue about
   whether a cost belongs, ask: does it grow when units grow?
3. **Divide** to get margin per unit, then compute CAC, payback, and LTV
   from last quarter's acquisition spend and a realistic lifespan.
4. **Take it to whoever sets prices** with one question: given this margin,
   what discount can we actually afford?

Expect two surprises: the margin will be lower than the number people quote
in meetings, and at least one customer type will be underwater. Both are the
point.

## Small numbers, big consequences

The power of unit economics is that tiny per-unit changes multiply by
volume: a modest price increase that survives, a small serving cost
removed, one delivery attempt saved — each flows through hundreds or
thousands of units into serious annual money. This is also its warning:
per-unit *losses* multiply identically. The business that "loses a little
on each but makes it up on volume" is a joke with a body count. Before any
scaling decision — more marketing, more staff, more territory — the
question is always the same: *does the unit, honestly costed, make money?*
If yes, scale is a multiplier. If no, scale is an accelerant.

## Summary

- Pick the natural unit and know its honest revenue, full cost, and
  margin — service time and extras included, distributions as well as
  averages.
- Cost it properly: the worked example's "64% margin" was 49% once support,
  visits, and payment fees were counted.
- Compute CAC, payback, and realistic LTV from four operating figures; a
  customer who costs more than they'll contribute is charity with paperwork.
- Know the discount table: a 10% price cut needs +25% volume at 50% margin,
  +50% at 30%, and is unrecoverable near 10%.
- Hunt margin leaks at unit level: unprofitable customer types, serving
  cost creep, the discount ratchet, unpriced extras.
- Scale multiplies whatever the unit truly is: profit-making units
  compound, loss-making units accelerate.
