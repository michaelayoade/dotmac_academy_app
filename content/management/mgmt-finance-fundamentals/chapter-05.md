# Budgets & Business Cases

Sooner or later every manager wants something that costs money — a hire,
a vehicle, equipment, a marketing push — and discovers that wanting it
eloquently is not the same as getting it funded. This chapter covers the
two instruments of that conversation: the **budget**, which is how a
business plans and controls its spending, and the **business case**, which
is how a manager earns a yes for something new. Both are exercises in the
same skill: turning intentions into honest numbers.

**By the end of this chapter you can:**

- Build a budget line from unit economics rather than from last year plus
  ten percent, and read a variance for its story.
- Answer the five questions every funding decision asks, before they are
  asked.
- Separate one-off benefits from recurring ones — and spot a case whose
  headline return depends entirely on the one-off.
- Compute a simple payback and say over what horizon a case still holds.
- Close your own loop after a yes, so the next case is easier to fund.

## Budgets: a plan, not a punishment

A budget is the business's money plan for the period: expected revenue,
planned costs, and the profit the two imply — broken down by team or
activity so each manager owns a piece. Working well with one is mostly
attitude plus three habits:

- **Build it bottom-up where you can.** A budget assembled from real unit
  economics (Chapter 2) — so-many customers at such-margin, so-many jobs
  needing so-many hours — is a plan you can defend and steer by. A budget
  that is "last year plus ten percent" is a guess wearing a spreadsheet.
- **Track variance monthly, and investigate the story.** The budget's
  management value is the *gap* between plan and actual — overspend,
  underspend, revenue ahead or behind — and the reason why. Variance is
  neither sin nor virtue: underspend that means postponed maintenance is
  a problem; overspend that tracks higher-than-planned sales volume is
  the plan working. The habit is asking *what does this gap tell us?*
  fast enough to act inside the year, not at its post-mortem.
- **Re-forecast when reality diverges.** The budget was the January view;
  by June you know more. Businesses that update the forecast steer;
  businesses that defend the original numbers navigate by a photograph
  of the road.

The known budget pathologies deserve naming, because you'll meet them:
the year-end spending spree ("use it or lose it"), padding requests in
anticipation of cuts, and treating the budget line as an entitlement.
Each is rational behaviour under bad incentives — recognise them, resist
contributing to them, and where you set budgets, reward honesty rather
than gamesmanship, or the numbers you collect will be negotiating
positions forever.

## The business case: five questions, honestly answered

Every funding decision, from a laptop to a new branch, is the same five
questions. A strong case answers them before they're asked:

1. **What problem does this solve?** Anchored in evidence — the numbers
   from Chapters 1–4, not adjectives. "We lose roughly this much monthly
   margin to X" opens wallets that "we really need Y" never will.
2. **What are the options?** Including honest alternatives (rent vs buy,
   hire vs redistribute, fix vs replace) and always including **do
   nothing, honestly costed**. A case with one option is a preference;
   options make it a decision. (This is the Decision Making course's
   criteria-and-alternatives discipline, applied to money.)
3. **What does it really cost?** Full cost over the horizon: purchase
   plus running, training, maintenance, and the opportunity cost of the
   people it will absorb — not the sticker price. Cases that hide costs
   get approved once and distrusted forever after.
4. **What does it return, and when?** The benefit in money where
   possible — margin gained, cost removed, churn reduced (Chapter 3
   arithmetic is persuasive here) — with the payback period stated. Where
   the benefit is genuinely unquantifiable (safety, compliance,
   morale), say so plainly rather than inventing decorative numbers;
   decorated guesses discredit the real figures beside them.
5. **What are the risks, and what would tell us we were wrong?** The
   downside cases, the assumptions the return depends on, and — the mark
   of a trustworthy case — the checkpoint: *here's what we'll measure,
   and here's the result that would mean stop.* (A tripwire, in Decision
   Making terms.) Proposers who define their own failure condition get
   trusted with the next case too.

Present it in that order, one page where possible, led by the
recommendation. The discipline is not bureaucracy — it is Chapter 2's
"worth doing *instead*" question in formal dress, and building the case
honestly will occasionally convince *you* the idea doesn't clear the bar.
That is the system working: the case you talk yourself out of on paper is
the cheapest failure you'll ever have.

## Worked example: the case that fell apart in year five

A manager proposes a part-time collections clerk at ₦150,000 a month —
₦1,800,000 a year. Using Chapter 4's office, the case claims two benefits:
closing the delivery-to-cash gap by 15 days (₦270,000 a day, so ₦4,050,000)
and cutting bad-debt write-offs from 2% of revenue to 1% (₦972,000 a year on
₦97,200,000 of annual revenue).

As submitted, it looks overwhelming: **₦5,022,000 of benefit against
₦1,800,000 of cost.** Nearly threefold. Approve it.

Now separate the two kinds of benefit, which is the single most useful move
in reading any case:

| | One-off | Recurring, per year |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash released by closing the gap | ₦4,050,000 | — |
| Bad debt avoided | — | ₦972,000 |
| Cost of the clerk | — | (₦1,800,000) |
| **Net** | **₦4,050,000** | **(₦828,000)** |

The cash release happens **once**. The clerk is paid **every year**. So the
case is a large first-year win sitting on top of a permanent annual loss:

| | Y1 | Y2 | Y3 | Y4 | Y5 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Net for the year | ₦3,222,000 | (₦828,000) | (₦828,000) | (₦828,000) | (₦828,000) |
| Cumulative | ₦3,222,000 | ₦2,394,000 | ₦1,566,000 | ₦738,000 | (₦90,000) |

Approved on the headline, this decision looks brilliant for three years,
ordinary in year four, and wrong in year five — by which time nobody
connects the loss to the choice that caused it.

That does not make the clerk a bad idea. It makes the *submitted case* a bad
case. The honest versions are available: size the role to what the recurring
benefit actually supports, fold the task into an existing job, or argue the
recurring benefit is larger than ₦972,000 and show why. What you may not do
is let a one-off number carry a permanent cost.

**The rule:** one-off benefits pay for one-off costs; recurring costs need
recurring benefits. Whenever a case's headline return is carried by a single
non-repeating item, ask what the steady state looks like — and ask it about
your own cases before a sceptic asks it for you.

## After the yes: close your own loop

The step that separates managers who get funded repeatedly from those who
got funded once: **go back and check**. Did the benefits arrive? Is the
saving real, the revenue there, the payback on schedule? Report it either
way — including the misses and what they taught. Post-decision reviews do
for spending what the measurement loop (Customer Experience, Chapter 5)
does for service: they keep the estimates honest, they compound your
judgement about your own forecasts, and they build the track record that
makes your *next* case's numbers believable on your signature. Finance
teams everywhere fund track records long before they fund enthusiasm.

## Do this at work

1. **Rebuild one budget line bottom-up.** Take a line you own and derive it
   from units — customers, jobs, hours — instead of last year's figure.
   Where the two disagree, you have found either a saving or a shortfall.
2. **Read one variance for its story.** Pick the largest gap between plan
   and actual in your area. Is it timing, volume, price, or a real
   overspend? Each has a different response, and the label matters more
   than the number.
3. **Write a one-page case for something you want.** Five questions, in
   order, recommendation first. Include "do nothing, honestly costed".
4. **Split your own benefits.** Mark every benefit in that case as one-off
   or recurring, and check the recurring column alone covers the recurring
   costs. If it does not, fix the case before someone else finds it.
5. **Name your own tripwire.** Write the measurement and the result that
   would mean stop. Proposers who define their own failure condition are
   the ones trusted with the next case.
6. **Close one old loop.** Find something funded a year ago in your area
   and check whether the promised benefit arrived. Report it either way.
   This is the habit that makes your future numbers believable.

## Summary

- A budget is a plan built from unit economics, steered by monthly
  variance-with-reasons, and re-forecast when reality diverges — not an
  entitlement, a guess, or a punishment.
- Recognise the budget games — sprees, padding, entitlement — and don't
  feed them; reward honest numbers wherever you set the rules.
- A business case is five honest answers: the evidenced problem, real
  options including do-nothing, full cost, valued-and-dated return, and
  risks with a stop condition.
- Separate one-off benefits from recurring ones: recurring costs need
  recurring benefits, and a headline return carried by a single
  non-repeating item hides a permanent annual loss.
- Never decorate unquantifiable benefits with invented numbers; plain
  honesty protects the credibility of the real figures.
- Close the loop after the yes — checked results and reported misses are
  what make your next case fundable on sight.
